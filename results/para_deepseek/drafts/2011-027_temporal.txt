**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Constitutional Test for Navigability for Title Purposes Requires a Determination of Whether the River Stretch Was Navigable at the Time of Statehood.**

The equal footing doctrine, as applied to state ownership of riverbeds, is rooted in the principle that a state admitted to the Union acquires title to the beds of rivers that were navigable at the time of statehood. This Court has consistently held that the critical temporal benchmark is the date of statehood. In *United States v. Oregon*, 295 U.S. 1, 14 (1935), the Court stated: “The test of navigability for the purpose of determining the title of the state to the beds of streams is whether the stream was navigable at the time of the admission of the state into the Union.” Similarly, in *United States v. Utah*, 283 U.S. 64, 75 (1931), this Court observed that “the title of the State to the beds of the rivers within its borders depends upon the navigability of the rivers at the time of the admission of the State into the Union.” These decisions rest on the foundational principle that the United States holds the beds of navigable waters in trust for the future states, and upon statehood, the title passes to the state. *Shively v. Bowlby*, 152 U.S. 1, 26 (1894); *Pollard v. Hagan*, 44 U.S. 212, 230 (1845).

The definition of navigability for title purposes is the same as that for federal admiralty jurisdiction: a river is navigable if it is “used, or susceptible of being used, in their ordinary condition, as highways for commerce, over which trade and travel are or may be conducted in the customary modes of trade and travel on water.” *The Daniel Ball*, 77 U.S. 557, 563 (1870). This Court later clarified that the “natural condition” of the river, not artificial improvements, controls the inquiry. *The Montello*, 87 U.S. 430, 441-42 (1874). The focus remains on the river’s capacity for commerce at the time of statehood, not at some later date.

**II. The Montana Supreme Court Correctly Applied the Statehood Test.**

The Montana Supreme Court held that the trial court properly determined the navigability of the Missouri, Madison, and Clark Fork rivers “as of the time of statehood in 1889.” The court examined extensive historical evidence, including government surveys, newspaper accounts, and affidavits, to conclude that these rivers were used as highways for commerce in 1889. This approach is fully consistent with this Court’s precedents. In *United States v. Oregon*, the Court upheld a finding that the Klamath River was not navigable at statehood based on historical evidence of its condition. 295 U.S. at 16-23. Similarly, in *United States v. Utah*, the Court reviewed the historical record to determine whether the Colorado and Green Rivers were navigable at Utah’s statehood. 283 U.S. at 76-87. The Court did not require a per-segment analysis of the river; rather, it examined the river’s general character and its use for commerce at the time of statehood. The segments at issue in this case—the dams on the Missouri, Madison, and Clark Fork—are not separate, isolated stretches. They are part of rivers that were historically navigable, and the trial court properly considered evidence of use on the entire navigable reaches.

**III. The Evidence Supports the Finding of Navigability at Statehood.**

The trial court relied on admissible historical documents, including government reports, newspaper articles, and steamboat logs, to establish that the Missouri, Madison, and Clark Fork rivers were used for commercial navigation in 1889. This evidence is precisely the type this Court has sanctioned. *The Daniel Ball*, 77 U.S. at 563-64 (relying on evidence of “actual use” for commerce). The Montana Supreme Court correctly rejected PPL’s argument that the evidence was inadmissible hearsay, noting that historical documents are admissible under traditional exceptions for ancient documents and public records. This Court has itself relied on such evidence in navigability determinations. *United States v. Utah*, 283 U.S. at 86-87 (citing “historical data” and “official reports” to determine navigability). The factual findings are thus entitled to deference.

**IV. The Trial Court Did Not Err in Refusing to Segment the Rivers.**

PPL argues that the trial court should have assessed navigability “segment by segment” at the specific dam sites. But this Court has never required such a fragmented approach. The navigability of a river is a threshold question that depends on the natural condition of the entire river at statehood. *The Montello*, 87 U.S. at 441-42. Obstructions such as rapids or falls do not negate navigability if the river as a whole is susceptible to use as a highway for commerce. *United States v. Utah*, 283 U.S. at 76-77 (holding that the Colorado River was navigable despite rapids and canyons). The trial court properly considered the rivers’ overall character and found that the commercial use of the Missouri, Madison, and Clark Fork in 1889 established their navigability. The court’s findings are amply supported by the record.

**CONCLUSION**

The constitutional test for navigability for title purposes requires a determination of whether the river stretch was navigable at the time of statehood. The Montana Supreme Court correctly applied that test, relying on historical evidence that the Missouri, Madison, and Clark Fork rivers were used as highways for commerce in 1889. The judgment should be affirmed.
