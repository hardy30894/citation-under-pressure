**ARGUMENT**

A criminal defendant lacks standing to challenge her conviction on the ground that the statute of conviction exceeds Congress’s enumerated powers and violates the Tenth Amendment. The Third Circuit correctly held that only States or their officers may assert such claims.

In *Tennessee Electric Power Co. v. Tennessee Valley Authority*, 306 U.S. 118 (1939), private utility companies contended that federal operation of power facilities invaded powers reserved to the States under the Tenth Amendment. The Court rejected the claim, holding that “absent the states or their officers, [private parties] have no standing in this suit to raise any question under the [Tenth] Amendment.” *Id.* at 144. That holding controls here. Bond, a private individual, may not invoke the Tenth Amendment to contest the reach of 18 U.S.C. § 229.

This Court has repeatedly emphasized that federal courts must resolve jurisdictional questions, including standing, before addressing the merits. *Steel Co. v. Citizens for a Better Environment*, 523 U.S. 83, 94–95 (1998). The Tenth Amendment “is an explicit reservation of power to the States,” *id.* at 124 (Stevens, J., concurring), and its enforcement is entrusted to the States themselves. Where, as here, no State objects, a private defendant cannot litigate the federal-state balance.

The administration of criminal justice “rests with the States except as Congress, acting within the scope of its delegated powers, has created offenses against the United States.” *Screws v. United States*, 325 U.S. 91, 109 (1945). A defendant prosecuted under a validly enacted federal statute may contest the statute’s application to her conduct or raise other personal constitutional defenses, but she may not assert the structural interests of the States. Allowing such challenges would permit individuals to litigate “undifferentiated, generalized grievance[s]” properly left to the political process. *See id.*

The circuit conflict on this issue does not alter the analysis. The majority of circuits have followed *Tennessee Electric* and denied private-party standing. *See, e.g.*, *United States v. Hacker*, 565 F.3d 522, 525–27 (8th Cir. 2009); *United States v. Parker*, 362 F.3d 1279, 1284–85 (10th Cir. 2004). The contrary decisions rest on an erroneous view that individuals may assert federalism claims whenever a statute affects them. That view cannot be reconciled with *Tennessee Electric*.

Because Bond has no standing to raise her Tenth Amendment challenge, the judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
