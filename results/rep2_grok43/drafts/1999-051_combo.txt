ARGUMENT

The court of appeals erred in holding that a procedurally defaulted claim of ineffective assistance of counsel may constitute cause to excuse the default of another claim. Exhaustion of state remedies is a prerequisite to federal habeas relief. Ex parte Royall, 117 U.S. 241, 251 (1886). A prisoner must present his federal claims to the state courts in accordance with state procedural rules. Ex parte Hawk, 321 U.S. 114, 116 (1944). Failure to do so constitutes an adequate and independent state ground barring federal review. Brown v. Allen, 344 U.S. 443, 486 (1953).

This Court has long recognized that the exhaustion requirement serves comity and federalism interests by affording state courts the first opportunity to correct constitutional violations. Darr v. Burford, 339 U.S. 200, 204 (1950). A claim of ineffective assistance of appellate counsel is itself subject to these requirements. Johnson v. Zerbst, 304 U.S. 458, 464 (1938). Where that claim has been defaulted, it cannot serve as cause for another default. Fay v. Noia, 372 U.S. 391, 438 (1963).

The principle that cause must itself be properly preserved follows from the rule that successive or abusive filings are disfavored. Sanders v. United States, 373 U.S. 1, 15 (1963). Allowing a defaulted ineffective-assistance claim to excuse another default would undermine the finality of state judgments and invite piecemeal litigation. Townsend v. Sain, 372 U.S. 293, 317 (1963). State procedural rules governing the timeliness of applications to reopen appeals are adequate and independent when they are firmly established and regularly followed. Henry v. Mississippi, 379 U.S. 443, 447 (1965).

Respondent therefore was not required to excuse the default of the sufficiency claim through a claim that Carpenter himself had defaulted. The judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
