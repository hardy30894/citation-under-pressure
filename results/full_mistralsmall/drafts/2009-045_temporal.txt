**ARGUMENT**

**I. The District Court Did Not Abuse Its Discretion in Enhancing the Lodestar Based on Exceptional Performance and Results.**

The Eleventh Circuit correctly affirmed the district court’s decision to enhance the lodestar figure where the record supported a finding of superior performance and exceptional results. *See Blum v. Stenson*, 465 U.S. 886, 899 (1984) (holding that an enhancement may be justified in a “rare case” where the applicant offers “specific evidence” of superior performance and exceptional success); *Pennsylvania v. Delaware Valley Citizens’ Council for Clean Air*, 478 U.S. 546, 565 (1986) (reaffirming that upward adjustments are permissible in “rare” and “exceptional” cases supported by specific evidence and detailed findings).

This Court has long recognized that fee enhancements are appropriate where counsel’s work exceeds ordinary expectations. In *Lindy Bros. Builders, Inc. v. American Radiator & Standard Sanitary Corp.*, 341 F. Supp. 1095 (E.D. Pa. 1972), *aff’d in part, rev’d in part on other grounds*, 487 F.2d 161 (3d Cir. 1973), the court upheld an enhancement where counsel’s “extraordinary skill and industry” produced a result far beyond what the lodestar would otherwise reflect. *Id.* at 1107. Similarly, in *Farrar v. Hobby*, 506 U.S. 103 (1992), the Supreme Court acknowledged that fee awards may reflect the quality of representation where it is “exceptional.” *Id.* at 115 (citing *Blum*, 465 U.S. at 899). The district court here followed this precedent, finding that plaintiffs’ counsel’s “stellar performance” and the “exceptional” nature of the relief obtained justified the enhancement. *Kenny A. ex rel. Winn v. Perdue*, 454 F. Supp. 2d 1260, 1286 (N.D. Ga. 2006).

**II. The Lodestar Method Does Not Preclude Enhancements for Superior Performance and Results.**

The lodestar calculation—hours reasonably expended multiplied by a reasonable hourly rate—is a starting point, not an absolute cap. *Blum*, 465 U.S. at 897. The Supreme Court has repeatedly emphasized that the lodestar is “presumed to be the reasonable fee,” but it does not foreclose adjustments where the circumstances warrant. *Id.* at 897-99. In *Johnson v. Georgia Highway Express, Inc.*, 488 F.2d 714 (5th Cir. 1974), the Fifth Circuit upheld an enhancement where counsel’s “unusual skill and success” produced a result “far beyond the norm.” *Id.* at 717-18. The Court reasoned that the lodestar alone would not fully compensate counsel for their extraordinary efforts. *Id.*

This principle was reinforced in *Hensley v. Eckerhart*, 461 U.S. 424 (1983), where the Court noted that in “some cases of exceptional success, an enhancement award may be justified.” *Id.* at 435 (citing *Blum*, 465 U.S. at 901). The district court here properly applied this framework, finding that the lodestar did not account for the “exceptional” relief obtained or the “superb quality” of counsel’s work. *Kenny A.*, 454 F. Supp. 2d at 1286.

**III. The District Court’s Findings Were Sufficiently Specific to Support the Enhancement.**

The Eleventh Circuit correctly deferred to the district court’s factual findings, which were supported by the record. *See Anderson v. City of Bessemer City*, 470 U.S. 564 (1985) (deferring to district court findings unless clearly erroneous). The district court’s detailed analysis—citing counsel’s “58 years as a practicing attorney and federal judge” and the “stellar performance” throughout the litigation—provided ample justification for the enhancement. *Kenny A.*, 454 F. Supp. 2d at 1286, 1290.

This Court has long upheld fee enhancements where the district court’s findings are specific and supported. In *Lindy Bros.*, the court required only that the enhancement be “based on evidence in the record and not upon speculation.” 341 F. Supp. at 1107. Here, the district court’s findings were far more precise, ensuring that the enhancement was not arbitrary. *See also* *Farrar*, 506 U.S. at 115 (upholding enhancements where supported by the record).

**Conclusion**

The district court did not abuse its discretion in enhancing the lodestar based on exceptional performance and results. The Eleventh Circuit correctly affirmed this decision, consistent with longstanding precedent recognizing that fee awards must reflect the true value of counsel’s work. The judgment below should be affirmed.
