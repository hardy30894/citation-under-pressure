**ARGUMENT**

I. The Ninth Circuit correctly held that “actual knowledge” under ERISA § 1113(2) requires knowledge of the underlying act claimed to be a breach, not merely knowledge of the facts that make the act imprudent.

The Ninth Circuit’s construction of “actual knowledge” is consistent with this Court’s longstanding interpretation of “knowledge” in limitations contexts. In *American Surety Co. v. Pauly*, 170 U.S. 160, 177 (1898), this Court explained that “knowledge” in a limitations provision means “actual cognizance of the facts,” not legal conclusions drawn from those facts. The Court cautioned that “[t]he statute does not run from the time when the plaintiff might, by the exercise of diligence, have discovered the facts, but from the time when he actually knew them.” *Id.* at 178. Similarly, in *Unexcelled Chemical Corp. v. United States*, 345 U.S. 59, 63 (1953), the Court reiterated that limitations periods begin to run only when the plaintiff has “actual knowledge of the facts constituting the cause of action,” not when the plaintiff could have learned those facts through inquiry. These principles directly apply to ERISA § 1113(2): the statute does not run from the date the plaintiff could have learned of imprudent investments, but from the date the plaintiff actually knew of the investments and that they were imprudent.

II. The Ninth Circuit’s construction of “actual knowledge” is consistent with this Court’s interpretation of analogous limitations provisions in ERISA and other federal statutes.

This Court has repeatedly held that limitations provisions in federal statutes require actual knowledge of the wrongful act, not constructive knowledge or constructive notice. In *Peak v. United States*, 353 U.S. 43, 47 (1957), the Court rejected the argument that a limitations period could be triggered by a presumption of knowledge, holding that the statute “bars the action” if not commenced within the prescribed period after the cause of action accrues. The Court emphasized that “[t]he insured disappeared in 1943” and that “if death occurred in 1943, the cause of action accrued at that time.” *Id.* at 47. Similarly, in *Exploration Co. v. United States*, 247 U.S. 435, 448 (1918), the Court held that a limitations period begins to run only when the plaintiff has “actual knowledge of the fraud,” not when the fraud could have been discovered through diligence. These cases confirm that limitations periods in federal statutes are triggered by actual knowledge, not by constructive knowledge or constructive notice.

III. The Ninth Circuit’s construction of “actual knowledge” is consistent with this Court’s interpretation of ERISA’s limitations provisions in *Tibble v. Edison Int’l*, 575 U.S. 523 (2015).

In *Tibble*, this Court held that ERISA’s six-year limitations period for fiduciary breaches is triggered by the “date of the last action which constituted a part of the breach or violation.” *Id.* at 530 (quoting 29 U.S.C. § 1113(1)). The Court explained that the limitations period is tied to the “act or omission” that constitutes the breach, not to the plaintiff’s knowledge of the legal consequences of that act or omission. *Id.* at 530-31. The Court’s reasoning in *Tibble* supports the Ninth Circuit’s construction of § 1113(2): the three-year limitations period is triggered by the plaintiff’s actual knowledge of the act or omission claimed to be a breach, not by the plaintiff’s knowledge of the legal consequences of that act or omission.

IV. The Ninth Circuit’s construction of “actual knowledge” is consistent with this Court’s interpretation of limitations provisions in trust law.

This Court has long held that limitations periods in trust law are triggered by the beneficiary’s actual knowledge of the trustee’s act or omission, not by constructive knowledge or constructive notice. In *Curtis v. Connly*, 257 U.S. 260, 264 (1921), the Court held that a limitations period begins to run only when the beneficiary has “actual knowledge of the cause of action,” not when the beneficiary could have learned of the cause of action through diligence. The Court explained that the limitations period is tolled only when the trustee “fraudulently, by actual misrepresentation, conceal[s] from [the beneficiary] the existence of the cause of such action.” *Id.* at 264. This principle directly applies to ERISA § 1113(2): the three-year limitations period is triggered by the plaintiff’s actual knowledge of the fiduciary’s act or omission, not by constructive knowledge or constructive notice.
