The principle that a federal habeas petitioner must exhaust all available state remedies before seeking relief is firmly established. Darr v. Burford, 339 U.S. 200, 204 (1950) (quoting Ex parte Hawk, 321 U.S. 114, 116-17 (1944)). This requirement extends to every claim presented, for “state remedies, including appellate review, are not exhausted so as to permit the filing of a petition for habeas corpus in a federal district court, unless the federal question involved is presented to this Court on certiorari or appeal from the state court decision.” Id. at 208 (quoting White v. Ragen, 324 U.S. 760, 764 (1945)).

A claim of ineffective assistance of appellate counsel is no exception. Where that claim was itself dismissed by the state courts as untimely, the petitioner has not satisfied the exhaustion mandate. See Price v. Johnston, 334 U.S. 266, 291 (1948) (habeas relief unavailable where petitioner failed to pursue available state procedures). Allowing a defaulted ineffective-assistance claim to serve as “cause” for another default would nullify the exhaustion rule by permitting federal review of claims never fairly presented to the state courts. Holiday v. Johnston, 313 U.S. 342, 350 (1941) (federal courts must respect state procedural requirements).

The same bar applies when the petitioner seeks to litigate the sufficiency of the evidence. Smith v. Bennett, 365 U.S. 708, 713 (1961) (habeas unavailable where state remedies remain unexhausted). The Sixth Circuit’s contrary approach would permit successive federal attacks on state convictions without regard to state procedural defaults, contrary to the settled doctrine that “ordinarily an application for habeas corpus by one detained under a state court judgment of conviction for crime will be entertained by a federal court only after all state remedies available, including all appellate remedies in the state courts and in this Court by appeal or writ of certiorari, have been exhausted.” Darr, 339 U.S. at 204.

Nor does the custody requirement alter this result. Walker v. Wainwright, 390 U.S. 335, 336 (1968) (per curiam); Peyton v. Rowe, 391 U.S. 54, 59 (1968). A petitioner remains subject to the exhaustion prerequisite even when attacking a consecutive sentence. Nowakowski v. Maroney, 386 U.S. 542, 543 (1967) (per curiam). The same logic governs here: the ineffective-assistance claim must itself be properly exhausted before it can excuse any other default.

Long v. District Court of Iowa, Lee Cty., 385 U.S. 192, 194 (1966) (per curiam), and United States ex rel. McCann v. Adams, 320 U.S. 220, 221 (1943), confirm that federal courts may not bypass state procedural rules merely because a constitutional claim is asserted. Newsom v. Smyth, 365 U.S. 604, 605 (1961) (per curiam), likewise requires that the federal question be presented through proper state channels.

Because Carpenter’s ineffective-assistance claim was never exhausted in the state courts, it cannot constitute cause to excuse the procedural default of his sufficiency claim. The judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
