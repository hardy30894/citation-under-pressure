A. Constitutionality of 18 U.S.C. § 1951

Congress lacked authority under the Commerce Clause to enact 18 U.S.C. § 1951. The Clause empowers Congress to “regulate Commerce . . . among the several States.” U.S. Const. art. I, § 8, cl. 3. Section 1951 reaches purely local robberies whose connection to interstate commerce is, at most, attenuated. In United States v. Lopez, 514 U.S. 549 (1995), the Supreme Court held that Congress may regulate only three categories of activity: the channels of interstate commerce, the instrumentalities of interstate commerce, and activities that “substantially affect” interstate commerce. Id. at 558-59. The robbery of a single restaurant falls into none of these categories.

The government’s proof—that the restaurant purchased some out-of-state goods and that its business declined after the offense—establishes no more than an indirect, speculative effect on interstate commerce. Lopez rejected precisely this mode of reasoning, explaining that the Commerce Clause requires a showing that the regulated activity, “by itself or as part of a larger class,” has a substantial effect. Id. at 567. Allowing aggregation of every local robbery would “convert congressional authority under the Commerce Clause to a general police power of the sort retained by the States.” Id. at 567. Because § 1951 contains no jurisdictional element limiting its reach to robberies that substantially affect interstate commerce, and because the statute regulates non-economic, violent crime, it exceeds the power recognized in Lopez.

The district court therefore erred in denying the motion to dismiss the indictment. This Court should reverse the convictions on that ground.
