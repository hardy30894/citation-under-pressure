The respondent's argument that an uncounseled conviction in tribal court can be used to satisfy the predicate offense requirement of 18 U.S.C. 117(a) is supported by the Supreme Court's decision in Nichols v. United States is not directly on point, but several older cases provide guidance. In Gryger v. Burke, 334 U.S. 728 (1948), the Court held that a recidivist statute does not violate the Due Process Clause, even when it relies on prior convictions that may have been obtained without counsel. The Court noted that the "sentence as a fourth offender or habitual criminal is not to be viewed as either a new jeopardy or additional penalty for the earlier crimes" but rather as "a stiffened penalty for the latest crime, which is considered to be an aggravated offense because a repetitive one." Id. at 732. Similarly, in McDonald v. Commonwealth of Massachusetts, 180 U.S. 311 (1901), the Court upheld a state recidivist statute, reasoning that the enhanced punishment is for the latest offense, not the prior ones.

The use of prior uncounseled convictions as predicate offenses under 18 U.S.C. 117(a) is consistent with the principle that a recidivist statute punishes the most recent offense, not the prior ones. As the Court stated in Moore v. State of Missouri, 159 U.S. 673 (1895), "the punishment is for the new crime only, but is the heavier if he is an habitual criminal." Id. at 677. This principle is also reflected in Graham v. State of West Virginia, 224 U.S. 616 (1912), where the Court held that a recidivist statute is constitutional because it "imposes a higher punishment for the same offense upon one who has been convicted of other crimes." Id. at 623.

The Sixth Amendment right to counsel, as established in Gideon v. Wainwright, 372 U.S. 335 (1963), and its precursor, Betts v. Brady, 316 U.S. 455 (1942), does not apply to tribal courts because they are not subject to the Federal Constitution. As the Court noted in Talton v. Mayes, 163 U.S. 376 (1896), "the provisions of the Constitution of the United States ... had no reference to the government of the Indian tribes." Id. at 384. Similarly, in United States v. Kagama, 118 U.S. 375 (1886), the Court observed that "the Indian tribes are not within the provisions of the Constitution" and that "the provisions of that instrument ... do not apply to them." Id. at 379, 385.

The respondent's tribal court convictions, although obtained without counsel, are valid under the Indian Civil Rights Act, which governs tribal court proceedings. The use of these convictions as predicate offenses under 18 U.S.C. 117(a) is consistent with the principles established in the aforementioned cases. Therefore, the judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
