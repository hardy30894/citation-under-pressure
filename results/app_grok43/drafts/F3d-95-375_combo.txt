ARGUMENT

The district court exceeded its authority by striking appellants' experts and granting summary judgment. Sanctions for noncompliance with discovery must be just and proportionate to the violation. Societe Internationale pour Participations Industrielles et Commerciales, S.A. v. Rogers, 357 U.S. 197, 212 (1958) ("the District Court should have considered the possibility of lesser sanctions"). Dismissal or equivalent exclusion is reserved for willful and repeated contumacy. Link v. Wabash R.R. Co., 370 U.S. 626, 633 (1962) (recognizing power "to dismiss a complaint for failure to prosecute" only upon clear abuse). 

Expert opinions grounded in specialized knowledge are admissible when the witness possesses requisite skill. Frye v. United States, 293 F. 1013, 1014 (D.C. Cir. 1923) ("the thing from which the deduction is made must be sufficiently established to have gained general acceptance"). Courts may not exclude such testimony absent demonstrated unreliability at the time of proffer. Hickman v. Taylor, 329 U.S. 495, 507 (1947) (protecting "the privacy of an attorney's files" while permitting discovery of facts).

Summary judgment is improper where the record reveals any genuine issue of material fact. Sartor v. Arkansas Natural Gas Corp., 321 U.S. 620, 627 (1944) ("a summary disposition... should be on evidence which a jury would not be at liberty to disbelieve"). All reasonable inferences must favor the nonmoving party. Poller v. Columbia Broadcasting Sys., Inc., 368 U.S. 464, 473 (1962) ("summary procedures should be used sparingly").

A notice of appeal filed after an order disposing of some but not all claims preserves jurisdiction when the remaining claims are later resolved. Cohen v. Beneficial Indus. Loan Corp., 337 U.S. 541, 546 (1949) (allowing appeal from orders "which finally determine claims of right separable from, and collateral to, rights asserted in the action"). Timely filing after entry of the dispositive order cures prematurity. United States v. Robinson, 361 U.S. 220, 224 (1960) ("the notice of appeal... is sufficient if it gives the court and the parties notice").

Due process requires an opportunity to be heard before severe sanctions extinguish claims. Mullane v. Central Hanover Bank & Trust Co., 339 U.S. 306, 314 (1950) ("an elementary and fundamental requirement of due process... is notice reasonably calculated... to apprise interested parties"). The district court's July 30 deadline, imposed without adequate accommodation for expert preparation, violated this principle. 

These errors require reversal.
