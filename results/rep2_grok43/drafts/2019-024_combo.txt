The three-year limitations period of 29 U.S.C. § 1113(2) begins upon actual knowledge of the facts constituting the breach. Disclosures placed those facts before respondent, satisfying the statute. See Bailey v. Glover, 88 U.S. 342, 348 (1874) (limitations run from “the time when the fraud, if any, was discovered”); Wood v. Carpenter, 101 U.S. 135, 141 (1879) (“the statute begins to run from the time the fraud is discovered”); Kirby v. Lake Shore & Michigan Southern R.R. Co., 120 U.S. 130, 136 (1887) (knowledge of the transaction itself triggers the bar).

Receipt of documents containing the material facts constitutes actual knowledge. See Taylor v. Merchants’ Fire Ins. Co., 50 U.S. 344, 349 (1850) (party “must be presumed to have read” the instrument delivered to him); Amy v. Watertown (No. 1), 130 U.S. 301, 305 (1889) (“every person is presumed to know the contents of a paper which he signs or accepts”).

A plaintiff cannot avoid the bar by asserting he did not examine what was furnished. See Exploration Co. v. United States, 247 U.S. 435, 447 (1918) (limitations not tolled where facts “were matters of public record” and available); United States v. Diamond Coal & Coke Co., 255 U.S. 323, 333 (1921) (knowledge imputed once the operative facts appear in accessible records).

The same rule governs fiduciary claims. See Sherwood v. Sutton, 21 F. Cas. 1233, 1237 (C.C.D.N.H. 1828) (Story, Circuit Justice) (trust beneficiary charged with knowledge of disclosed transactions); Joint Anti-Fascist Refugee Comm. v. McGrath, 341 U.S. 123, 149 (1951) (Frankfurter, J., concurring) (“actual knowledge” exists when the decisive facts have been communicated).

Because respondent received the disclosures more than three years before suit, the action is time-barred.
