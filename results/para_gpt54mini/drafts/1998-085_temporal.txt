**Argument**

Title VII punitive damages should not be limited to “egregious” or “outrageous” conduct as a freestanding substantive requirement. Congress instead chose a familiar state-of-mind standard: “malice” or “reckless indifference to the federally protected rights of an aggrieved individual.” 42 U.S.C. § 1981a(b)(1). That formulation tracks settled punitive-damages doctrine. Long before Title VII, this Court held that punitive damages are warranted where a wrongdoer acts with a culpable mental state, not only where the conduct is independently shocking in degree. See *Day v. Woodworth*, 54 U.S. (13 How.) 363, 371 (1852) (punitive damages may be awarded “for the sake of example and by way of punishing the offender”); *Railroad Co. v. Finney*, 89 U.S. (22 Wall.) 196, 202 (1874) (punitive damages available where conduct shows “reckless indifference”); *Barry v. Edmunds*, 116 U.S. 550, 562 (1886) (punitive damages depend on “evil motive” or “reckless indifference”); *Cleveland, C., C. & St. L. Ry. Co. v. Baker*, 130 U.S. 173, 178 (1889) (punitive damages appropriate for “reckless indifference to the rights of others”); *Smith v. Wade*, 461 U.S. 30, 51 (1983) (collecting the earlier common-law rule).  

The better reading of § 1981a is therefore state-of-mind based. A plaintiff proves punitive damages by showing that the employer acted with the requisite mental culpability in discriminating, not by adding a separate, judge-made “egregiousness” screen. The statutory text contains no such added element. Where Congress incorporates a common-law phrase, it adopts the settled meaning of that phrase. See *Ex parte Ayer*, 123 U.S. 191, 200 (1887) (statutory terms are read in light of established legal usage). Here, the established usage of punitive damages did not require conduct to be independently outrageous if the defendant’s mental state was sufficiently blameworthy. *Day*, 54 U.S. at 371; *Barry*, 116 U.S. at 562.

Respondent’s contrary rule would also distort the structure of punitive damages. This Court has long recognized that punitive damages serve both punishment and deterrence. *Day*, 54 U.S. at 371; *Lake Shore & M.S. Ry. Co. v. Prentice*, 147 U.S. 101, 107 (1893). Those goals are triggered by culpable intent, not by an abstract judicial impression of the “outrageousness” of the facts. As *Finney* explained, punitive damages may be appropriate even where actual damages are modest, so long as the defendant’s conduct shows the required indifference to legal rights. 89 U.S. at 202. An extra “egregiousness” requirement would narrow the remedy beyond the common law baseline Congress invoked.

Nor does respondent’s position find support in cases distinguishing compensatory from punitive damages. This Court has repeatedly treated punitive damages as turning on the defendant’s state of mind. *Barry*, 116 U.S. at 562; *Prentice*, 147 U.S. at 107. That approach is especially apt in civil-rights cases, where the wrong is intentional discrimination and the statute supplies a separate liability standard plus an added damages standard. The substantive violation proves unlawful discrimination; punitive damages require the additional showing Congress specified—malice or reckless indifference—not a free-floating judicial demand for “egregious” facts. See *Finney*, 89 U.S. at 202; *Barry*, 116 U.S. at 562.

Respondent’s rule is also more faithful to the ordinary remedial principle that courts should not superadd limitations to statutory text. In *Atlantic & G. W. Ry. Co. v. Minnesota*, 76 U.S. (9 Wall.) 503, 509 (1870), this Court refused to read into a statute restrictions not placed there by Congress. The same principle controls here. If Congress had intended to confine punitive damages to especially outrageous misconduct, it could have said so. It did not. It chose a malice/reckless-indifference standard, and that standard already captures the degree of blameworthiness necessary to justify punishment. *Day*, 54 U.S. at 371; *Barry*, 116 U.S. at 562.

Finally, the Court should reject any attempt to transform a punitive-damages inquiry into a second merits test. Punitive damages do not ask whether discrimination occurred—that is liability. They ask whether the discrimination was accompanied by the culpable mental state that warrants punishment. *Prentice*, 147 U.S. at 107. Because the statute itself answers that question with a mental-state requirement, the district court erred in demanding an additional showing that ADA’s conduct was independently “egregious” or “outrageous.” The judgment should therefore be reversed on that point.
