# ARGUMENT

## I. Standard of Review

This Court reviews a grant of summary judgment de novo, drawing all reasonable inferences in favor of the non-movant, PowerOasis. *Anderson Corp. v. Fiber Composites, LLC*, 474 F.3d 1361, 1366 (Fed. Cir. 2007). Whether a claim is entitled to the benefit of an earlier filing date under 35 U.S.C. § 120 turns on whether the earlier application's disclosure satisfies the written description requirement of 35 U.S.C. § 112, ¶ 1—a question that, while ultimately factual, cannot be resolved against the patentee on summary judgment where a reasonable factfinder could find adequate support in the original disclosure. *Vas-Cath, Inc. v. Mahurkar*, 935 F.2d 1555, 1560–61 (Fed. Cir. 1991).

## II. The District Court Erred by Demanding Literal Antecedent Support for "Customer Interface" Located on the Customer's Device

Under § 120, "a claim in a subsequent application is entitled to the benefit of the filing date of an earlier application" if "the earlier application discloses the invention claimed in the later application in the manner required by § 112." *Lockwood v. American Airlines, Inc.*, 107 F.3d 1565, 1571–72 (Fed. Cir. 1997). But that inquiry does not require the earlier specification to use the identical claim language later adopted. This Court has repeatedly held that the test for written description support "is whether the disclosure of the application relied upon reasonably conveys to the artisan that the inventor had possession" of the later-claimed subject matter—not whether the specification contains verbatim antecedent language. *Vas-Cath*, 935 F.2d at 1563. The district court's analysis inverted this standard: rather than asking whether the Original Application conveyed possession of a customer interface located on the customer's device, the court asked only whether the phrase "customer interface"—or language specifically describing its location on a laptop—appeared for the first time in the 2000 CIP Application. *PowerOasis, Inc. v. T-Mobile USA, Inc.*, No. 05-cv-42-PB, 2007 WL 962937, at *2 (D.N.H. Mar. 30, 2007). That approach conflicts with this Court's admonition that written description support need not be provided *in haec verba*. *See In re Wertheim*, 646 F.2d 527, 537 (C.C.P.A. 1981) (support may exist even without express, verbatim disclosure of the later claim language, so long as the earlier disclosure conveys the same concept to a person of skill in the art).

## III. The Original Application Conveys Possession of a Customer Interface Located on the Customer's External Device

The Original Application discloses a vending machine that connects to "an external telecommunications device of the customer" and enables a customer, through that connection, to access a telecommunications channel. This disclosure necessarily conveys that the customer interacts with—and receives status information through—the very external device to which the vending machine connects, because the specification describes no other mechanism by which the customer could monitor or control the transaction once connected. A person of ordinary skill in the art reading the Original Application would understand that the customer's own laptop, once linked to the telecommunications channel access connector, functions as the point of interaction contemplated by the invention. That is sufficient under this Court's precedent, which asks only whether the earlier disclosure "reasonably conveys" the later-claimed concept to the art, not whether it anticipates the precise terminology later chosen by claim drafters. *Vas-Cath*, 935 F.2d at 1563; *In re Wertheim*, 646 F.2d at 537.

The district court's reliance "entirely on new language added to the 2000 CIP application" to construe "customer interface" does not establish, as a matter of law, that the concept was *new matter* rather than a more explicit articulation of subject matter already disclosed. This Court has recognized that later applications may use "different language to describe the same invention" without introducing new matter, so long as the underlying concept was already conveyed. *Purdue Pharma L.P. v. Faulding Inc.*, 230 F.3d 1320, 1323 (Fed. Cir. 2000) (written description inquiry examines whether the "invention" — not particular phrasing — was disclosed). At minimum, whether the Original Application conveyed possession of a laptop-based customer interface, given its disclosure of a customer's external device connected to the telecommunications channel, presents a genuine issue of material fact improper for resolution on summary judgment. *Vas-Cath*, 935 F.2d at 1560–61.

## IV. Because the Asserted Claims Are Entitled to the Original Application's Filing Date, the MobileStar Network Cannot Anticipate Under § 102(b)

Section 102(b) bars patentability only where the claimed invention was in public use or on sale "more than one year prior to the date of the application for patent in the United States." 35 U.S.C. § 102(b). Where, as here, the asserted claims are entitled to the benefit of the Original Application's February 6, 1997 filing date under § 120, the relevant critical date precedes—by more than two years—the June 1999 public use and sale of the MobileStar Network. *See Tronzo v. Biomet, Inc.*, 156 F.3d 1154, 1158–59 (Fed. Cir. 1998) (claims properly entitled to priority date are shielded from intervening prior art). The district court's contrary holding rested entirely on its erroneous written description analysis. Because that analysis was legally flawed and, at a minimum, left genuine factual disputes unresolved, summary judgment of anticipation cannot stand.

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should reverse the grant of summary judgment and remand for further proceedings.
