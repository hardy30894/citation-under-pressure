**ARGUMENT**

I. The Double Jeopardy Clause bars retrial of a prior conviction allegation in noncapital sentencing proceedings.

The Double Jeopardy Clause of the Fifth Amendment provides that no person shall “be subject for the same offence to be twice put in jeopardy of life or limb.” U.S. Const. amend. V. The Supreme Court has long held that this Clause protects against multiple punishments for the same offense. *North Carolina v. Pearce*, 395 U.S. 711, 717 (1969). In *Bullington v. Missouri*, 451 U.S. 430 (1981), the Court extended this protection to capital sentencing proceedings, holding that a jury’s determination that the State had failed to prove the existence of aggravating factors beyond a reasonable doubt barred retrial of those factors. *Id.* at 446. The Court reasoned that such sentencing proceedings possess “the hallmarks of the trial on guilt or innocence,” including the requirement that the State prove its case beyond a reasonable doubt. *Id.* at 438-39.

The Court reaffirmed this principle in *Arizona v. Rumsey*, 467 U.S. 203 (1984), where it held that a jury’s rejection of an aggravating factor in a capital sentencing proceeding precluded the State from relitigating that factor on retrial. *Id.* at 210. The Court explained that the sentencing proceeding in *Bullington* “resembled a trial on guilt or innocence” because it required the State to prove its case beyond a reasonable doubt and provided the defendant with procedural protections akin to those at trial. *Id.* at 209-10.

The Court’s reasoning in *Bullington* and *Rumsey* is not limited to capital cases. In *Monge v. California*, 524 U.S. 721 (1998), the Supreme Court expressly declined to limit *Bullington* to capital sentencing proceedings. *Id.* at 734 (plurality opinion). The Court noted that the “hallmarks of the trial on guilt or innocence” identified in *Bullington*—proof beyond a reasonable doubt and procedural protections—are present in noncapital sentencing proceedings as well. *Id.* at 734-35. The Court further observed that the Double Jeopardy Clause’s protections are not confined to capital cases but apply broadly to all sentencing proceedings where the State seeks to impose additional punishment based on prior convictions. *Id.* at 735.

The California Supreme Court’s contrary holding in this case is inconsistent with the Supreme Court’s precedents. The California court erroneously concluded that the Double Jeopardy Clause does not apply to noncapital sentencing proceedings, relying on *Williams v. New York*, 337 U.S. 241 (1949), which held that the Due Process Clause does not require formal sentencing procedures in noncapital cases. *Monge*, 524 U.S. at 732 (plurality opinion). However, *Williams* does not address the Double Jeopardy Clause and is therefore inapplicable to the issue presented here. *Id.* at 733. The California court also misapplied *Stroud v. United States*, 251 U.S. 15 (1919), which held that the Double Jeopardy Clause does not bar retrial of a prior conviction allegation where the defendant’s conviction was reversed on appeal. *Id.* at 18. *Stroud* is distinguishable because it involved a reversal for trial error, not a determination that the State failed to prove its case beyond a reasonable doubt. *Id.* at 734.

The Supreme Court’s decision in *United States v. Watts*, 519 U.S. 148 (1997), further supports the application of the Double Jeopardy Clause to noncapital sentencing proceedings. In *Watts*, the Court held that sentencing courts may consider uncharged conduct when determining a defendant’s sentence, but it emphasized that such conduct must be proven to a jury beyond a reasonable doubt or admitted by the defendant. *Id.* at 157. The Court’s reasoning underscores the importance of the reasonable doubt standard in sentencing proceedings, a hallmark of the protections afforded by the Double Jeopardy Clause.

II. The California Supreme Court’s holding conflicts with the Supreme Court’s precedents and undermines the fundamental protections of the Double Jeopardy Clause.

The California Supreme Court’s decision in this case creates a circuit split and undermines the uniformity of federal law. The Supreme Court has repeatedly emphasized the importance of the Double Jeopardy Clause’s protections in sentencing proceedings, and its decisions in *Bullington*, *Rumsey*, and *Watts* make clear that these protections apply regardless of whether the sentencing proceeding is capital or noncapital. The California court’s contrary holding is unsupported by precedent and should be reversed.

Moreover, the California court’s decision frustrates the purpose of the Double Jeopardy Clause by allowing the State to relitigate prior conviction allegations where the evidence was insufficient to support the enhancement. This result is inconsistent with the Clause’s text and purpose, which is to protect defendants from the burdens and risks of multiple prosecutions. *Green v. United States*, 355 U.S. 184, 187-88 (1957). The Supreme Court has recognized that the Double Jeopardy Clause’s protections are not limited to acquittals but extend to any determination that the State has failed to prove its case. *Burks v. United States*, 437 U.S. 1, 16 (1978).

The California Supreme Court’s holding also creates uncertainty in sentencing proceedings, as defendants cannot rely on the finality of a court’s determination that the State has failed to prove a prior conviction allegation. This uncertainty undermines the legitimacy of the sentencing process and the public’s confidence in the justice system. The Supreme Court has repeatedly emphasized the importance of finality in criminal proceedings, and the Double Jeopardy Clause is a critical safeguard against the State’s ability to relitigate issues that have already been decided. *United States v. DiFrancesco*, 449 U.S. 117, 130 (1980).

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the Court should reverse the judgment of the California Supreme Court and hold that the Double Jeopardy Clause precludes retrial of a prior conviction allegation in noncapital sentencing proceedings.
