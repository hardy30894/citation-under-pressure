# ARGUMENT

## I. The Text and Structure of the INA Establish That a Grant of TPS Constitutes an "Admission" Under § 1255

Congress provided that once an alien receives TPS, "the alien shall be considered as being in, and maintaining, lawful status as a nonimmigrant" for purposes of adjustment of status under § 1255. 8 U.S.C. § 1254a(f)(4). That deeming clause must be given real effect. It is "our duty to give effect, if possible, to every clause and word of a statute." *United States v. Menasche*, 348 U.S. 528, 538–39 (1955). Nonimmigrant status, by its very nature, is a category of lawful presence obtained only through inspection and authorization by an immigration officer—precisely what § 1255(a) requires when it speaks of an alien "inspected and admitted." An alien cannot occupy "lawful status as a nonimmigrant" in any authentic sense without having been admitted in that status. Congress's instruction that TPS recipients be "considered as being" nonimmigrants therefore necessarily carries with it the admission that nonimmigrant status presupposes. To read § 1254a(f)(4) as conferring lawful status while withholding the admission that status logically entails empties the provision of meaning for the very population—aliens who entered without inspection—for whom the distinction matters. Such a reading violates the settled principle that statutes should not be construed so that "a part will be inoperative or superfluous." *Menasche*, 348 U.S. at 538–39.

## II. Canons of Construction Governing Immigration Statutes Confirm That § 1254a(f)(4) Satisfies § 1255(a)'s Admission Requirement

### A. Doubts About the Meaning of Immigration Statutes Affecting an Alien's Ability to Remain Are Resolved in the Alien's Favor

This Court has long recognized that where the consequences to the individual are severe, ambiguous immigration statutes are construed against the harsher result. In *Fong Haw Tan v. Phelan*, 333 U.S. 6, 10 (1948), the Court explained that "we resolve the doubts in favor of that construction because deportation is a drastic measure and at times the equivalent of banishment or exile." The Court reaffirmed that principle in *Barber v. Gonzales*, 347 U.S. 637, 642 (1954), holding that "since the stakes are considerable for the individual, we will not assume that Congress meant to trench on his freedom beyond that which is required by the narrowest of several possible meanings of the words used." Although those cases addressed deportation rather than adjustment of status, the same solicitude applies here: denying adjustment condemns TPS holders—many of whom have lived lawfully in the United States for years under the government's own protection—to permanent ineligibility for the very immigration benefit Congress made available to nonimmigrants generally. Where, as here, the statutory text permits a construction that avoids this harsh and anomalous result, that construction should prevail.

### B. Immigration Law Recognizes Functional, Purpose-Driven Constructions of Technical Admission and Entry Requirements

This Court has repeatedly declined to apply the concepts of "entry" and "admission" mechanically where a rigid literal application would defeat the purposes those concepts serve. In *Rosenberg v. Fleuti*, 374 U.S. 449 (1963), the Court held that an "innocent, casual, and brief excursion" by a lawful permanent resident should not be treated as a new "entry" triggering the immigration laws' exclusion provisions, notwithstanding the literal statutory definition of entry. The Court reasoned that Congress could not have intended so technical a construction to produce such an unwarranted result for the individual alien. Similarly, in *Delgadillo v. Carmichael*, 332 U.S. 388 (1947), the Court held that a seaman rescued after his torpedoed ship sank, who was briefly taken to Cuba before returning to the United States, had not made a new "entry" that would disrupt his continuous residence for deportation purposes, explaining that the Court was "not able to find in the...Act the harsh result for which the government contends." And in *Kwong Hai Chew v. Colding*, 344 U.S. 590, 596 (1953), the Court treated a returning permanent resident alien "as if he had never left the United States," notwithstanding his physical departure and return, in order to accord him the statutory and constitutional protections Congress intended him to retain.

These cases establish that this Court does not read admission-related terms so literally that they defeat the evident congressional design. Here, Congress designed the TPS program to allow aliens fleeing disaster or conflict to remain lawfully and to enjoy the incidents of nonimmigrant status, including eligibility to seek adjustment under § 1255. Interpreting § 1254a(f)(4)'s deeming language as petitioners urge—treating the conferral of nonimmigrant status as satisfying § 1255(a)'s admission requirement—honors that purpose. The Third Circuit's contrary reading instead imports into the statute a technical distinction between "status" and "admission" that serves no purpose Congress articulated and that this Court's precedents counsel against imposing on humanitarian immigration provisions.

## CONCLUSION

For these reasons, the judgment of the Third Circuit should be reversed.
