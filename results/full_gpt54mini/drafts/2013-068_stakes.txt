**ARGUMENT**

I. The First Circuit correctly upheld the Massachusetts statute as a valid content-neutral time, place, and manner regulation.

The First Amendment permits reasonable restrictions on speech in a traditional public forum if the regulation is content neutral, narrowly tailored to serve a significant governmental interest, and leaves open ample alternative channels for communication. *Ward v. Rock Against Racism*, 491 U.S. 781, 791-92 (1989). The Massachusetts statute satisfies that standard. Its operative command applies to all persons who “knowingly enter or remain” within a marked 35-foot buffer zone adjacent to reproductive-health facilities, regardless of viewpoint. *McCullen v. Coakley*, 573 U.S. 464, 490-91 (2014); *Hill v. Colorado*, 530 U.S. 703, 719-20 (2000).

This Court has long recognized that the Commonwealth has a substantial interest in ensuring access to health-care facilities, preserving public safety, and protecting the health and privacy of patients. *Schenck v. Pro-Choice Network of Western New York*, 519 U.S. 357, 376-77 (1997); *Madsen v. Women’s Health Center, Inc.*, 512 U.S. 753, 768 (1994). Massachusetts enacted the statute against the backdrop of repeated disruption at clinic entrances, and the law is designed to prevent obstruction and harassment at the precise locations where patients must pass. *McCullen*, 573 U.S. at 490-91. That is a classic time, place, and manner objective. *Ward*, 491 U.S. at 791.

Petitioners insist the law is invalid because it burdens their preferred method of close conversation. But the Constitution does not require the State to adopt the least restrictive means when evaluating a generally applicable time, place, and manner restriction. *Ward*, 491 U.S. at 798-99. Rather, the question is whether the regulation is narrowly tailored to serve the government’s legitimate interests without burdening substantially more speech than necessary. *Id.* at 799. The Court in *McCullen* reaffirmed that principle while explaining that Massachusetts had not shown the statute was narrowly tailored on the particular record before it. *McCullen*, 573 U.S. at 496-97. But the First Circuit’s judgment here was correct under the law as it then stood, especially because *Hill* had already upheld a closely analogous buffer-zone law. *Hill*, 530 U.S. at 729-30.

The petitioners also cannot show that the statute forecloses adequate alternative channels. They remain free to engage in conversation outside the buffer zone, to distribute literature, to display signs, to pray, and to speak at varying volumes from nearby public sidewalks. *Ward*, 491 U.S. at 802; *McCullen*, 573 U.S. at 494-95. The First Circuit properly relied on the record showing that these alternatives were meaningful, even if not identical to the petitioners’ preferred mode of personal approach. See *Heffron v. International Society for Krishna Consciousness, Inc.*, 452 U.S. 640, 654-55 (1981). The First Amendment guarantees effective communication, not the most effective communication. *Id.* at 654.

II. *Hill v. Colorado* remains binding precedent and should not be overruled.

If the Court reaches the second question, stare decisis strongly favors retaining *Hill*. “Stare decisis is the preferred course because it promotes the evenhanded, predictable, and consistent development of legal principles.” *Payne v. Tennessee*, 501 U.S. 808, 827 (1991). The Court does not overrule precedent lightly, especially in the First Amendment context where legislatures and courts have relied on settled doctrine. *Dickerson v. United States*, 530 U.S. 428, 443-44 (2000).

*Hill* was correctly decided. It upheld a Colorado statute that limited approaches within 100 feet of a health-care facility and found the law content neutral because it regulated conduct, not the content of speech. *Hill*, 530 U.S. at 719-20, 725-26. The Court emphasized that the State may protect access to medical facilities and the privacy of unwilling listeners. *Id.* at 715, 729-30. Those concerns are present here as well.

At minimum, nothing in the intervening cases compels overruling *Hill*. The Court has repeatedly recognized that the First Amendment allows reasonable access regulations in sensitive physical settings. *Schenck*, 519 U.S. at 376-77; *Madsen*, 512 U.S. at 768; *McCullen*, 573 U.S. at 490-91. Petitioners’ request to cast aside *Hill* would destabilize a settled line of cases and unsettle longstanding reliance interests. *Payne*, 501 U.S. at 827-28.

III. The Massachusetts law may be distinguished from broader restrictions, but its validity does not depend on overruling *Hill*.

Even if the Court were to clarify the limits of *Hill*, that would not aid petitioners. The Massachusetts statute is narrower than the Colorado law in *Hill* and more directly tied to access points at clinics. *McCullen*, 573 U.S. at 490-91. The First Circuit therefore did not err in upholding it. The judgment should be affirmed.
