**ARGUMENT**

I. The Lodestar Method Is a Presumptively Correct Calculation of Reasonable Attorneys’ Fees.

The lodestar approach—calculating fees by multiplying the number of hours reasonably expended by a reasonable hourly rate—has long been recognized as the starting point for determining reasonable attorneys’ fees under federal fee-shifting statutes. *See* *Lindy Bros. Builders, Inc. v. Am. Radiator & Standard Sanitary Corp.*, 487 F.2d 161, 167 (3d Cir. 1973) (en banc) (adopting the lodestar method as the "most useful" starting point for fee calculations); *Farrar v. Hobby*, 506 U.S. 103, 114 (1992) (recognizing the lodestar as the "calculation of the attorney's hourly rate multiplied by the number of hours reasonably worked"). This Court has repeatedly emphasized that the lodestar figure is "presumptively the reasonable fee." *Blum v. Stenson*, 465 U.S. 886, 897 (1984) (quoting *Hensley v. Eckerhart*, 461 U.S. 424, 433 (1983)). The lodestar is not merely a floor or a ceiling; it is the "most critical factor" in the fee analysis. *Lindy Bros.*, 487 F.2d at 167.

The presumption that the lodestar is reasonable reflects the sound principle that hourly rates and hours worked are the most objective indicia of the value of legal services. *See id.* at 168 (noting that the lodestar "provides a reasonably objective basis on which to make that determination"). This Court has cautioned against deviations from the lodestar absent "specific evidence" that the lodestar does not fully compensate the prevailing party for the quality of representation and results obtained. *Blum*, 465 U.S. at 899. The lodestar thus serves as the baseline against which any enhancement must be justified.

II. Enhancements Based on Quality of Performance and Results Obtained Are Permissible in Extraordinary Cases.

This Court has never held that enhancements are categorically prohibited. To the contrary, it has repeatedly acknowledged that in "rare" and "exceptional" cases, an upward adjustment may be warranted where the lodestar fails to reflect the true value of the services rendered. *Delaware Valley Citizens’ Council for Clean Air v. Pennsylvania ("Delaware Valley I")*, 478 U.S. 546, 565 (1986) (plurality opinion) (upholding enhancements where "the lodestar does not fully reflect the attorney's work in the extraordinary circumstances of this case"). The Court has emphasized that such enhancements are permissible where the applicant offers "specific evidence" that the quality of service was "superior" and the results "exceptional." *Blum*, 465 U.S. at 899.

This principle is deeply rooted in the equitable tradition of fee-shifting statutes, which aim to ensure that prevailing parties are not deterred from vindicating their rights due to financial constraints. *See* *Alyeska Pipeline Serv. Co. v. Wilderness Soc’y*, 421 U.S. 240, 257 (1975) (recognizing that fee-shifting is an equitable remedy designed to encourage private enforcement of federal rights). The lodestar presumption ensures that fees are reasonable, but it does not foreclose adjustments where the circumstances warrant it. *See* *Hensley*, 461 U.S. at 433 n.7 (noting that the lodestar may be adjusted in "rare circumstances").

III. The District Court Did Not Abuse Its Discretion in Enhancing the Lodestar.

The District Court’s enhancement was supported by detailed findings that this case presented precisely the "rare" and "exceptional" circumstances justifying an upward adjustment. The Court found that plaintiffs’ counsel achieved "exceptional success" in securing comprehensive reforms for Georgia’s foster care system, including systemic changes that will benefit thousands of children. *See Kenny A. ex rel. Winn v. Perdue*, 454 F. Supp. 2d 1260, 1286 (N.D. Ga. 2006). The Court also observed that counsel’s performance was "stellar," requiring extraordinary skill, dedication, and perseverance over a prolonged and complex litigation. *Id.* at 1290. Such findings are entitled to substantial deference. *See* *Hensley*, 461 U.S. at 437 (affording district courts "wide discretion" in fee determinations).

This case is analogous to *Farrar*, where the Court recognized that enhancements may be appropriate where the results obtained are "exceptional" and the representation is "superior." 506 U.S. at 115. The District Court’s enhancement was similarly justified by the "exceptional" nature of the relief obtained and the "superior" quality of the representation. *See also* *Lindy Bros.*, 487 F.2d at 167 (upholding enhancements where counsel’s performance and results were "exceptional").

IV. The Eleventh Circuit Properly Affirmed the District Court’s Discretionary Decision.

The Eleventh Circuit correctly held that the District Court did not abuse its discretion in enhancing the lodestar. *Kenny A. ex rel. Winn v. Perdue*, 532 F.3d 1209, 1242 (11th Cir. 2008). The Court relied on well-established precedent recognizing that enhancements are permissible in "rare" and "exceptional" cases where the lodestar fails to reflect the true value of the services. *Id.* (citing *Delaware Valley I*, 478 U.S. at 565; *Blum*, 465 U.S. at 899). The Eleventh Circuit’s decision is consistent with this Court’s guidance and reflects the proper deference owed to district courts in fee determinations.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the Court should affirm the judgment of the Eleventh Circuit. The District Court did not abuse its discretion in enhancing the lodestar to reflect the exceptional quality of representation and results obtained in this case.
