ARGUMENT

The Massachusetts statute creates a content-neutral time, place, and manner restriction that satisfies the First Amendment. A regulation of speech in a traditional public forum is valid if it is “justified without reference to the content of the regulated speech,” “narrowly tailored to serve a significant governmental interest,” and “leave[s] open ample alternative channels for communication.” Ward v. Rock Against Racism, 491 U.S. 781, 791 (1989); Clark v. Community for Creative Non-Violence, 468 U.S. 288, 293 (1984). Sidewalks remain quintessential public forums, Perry Educ. Ass’n v. Perry Local Educators’ Ass’n, 460 U.S. 37, 45 (1983), yet the State may impose reasonable restrictions to protect access to medical facilities. Hill v. Colorado, 530 U.S. 703, 715-17 (2000).

The Act advances the significant interest in ensuring safe and unimpeded access to reproductive health care facilities. See Madsen v. Women’s Health Ctr., Inc., 512 U.S. 753, 767-68 (1994); Schenck v. Pro-Choice Network of W. N.Y., 519 U.S. 357, 376 (1997). Its fixed 35-foot zones are content-neutral on their face and as applied, distinguishing only by location rather than by the speaker’s message. Hill, 530 U.S. at 719-20. The zones are narrowly tailored because they address the documented problem of congestion and obstruction at clinic entrances without prohibiting all speech within the regulated area. Id. at 726-27; Burson v. Freeman, 504 U.S. 191, 210-11 (1992).

The statute leaves open ample alternative channels. Petitioners may speak at normal conversational volume, display signs, distribute literature, and pray outside the zones. Frisby v. Schultz, 487 U.S. 474, 483-84 (1988). The record confirms that these avenues remain effective at each clinic location.

Hill v. Colorado directly controls. Stare decisis requires adherence absent a showing that the precedent has become unworkable or that intervening authority has eroded its foundations. Payne v. Tennessee, 501 U.S. 808, 827-28 (1991). No such showing exists here. The decision in Hill remains workable, and subsequent cases have reaffirmed the distinction between content-based and content-neutral restrictions. The Court should decline petitioners’ invitation to overrule it.

The judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
