**ARGUMENT**

**I. The District Court Erred in Dismissing Buckles’s §2255 Motion as Untimely Because the Ninth Circuit’s Order Recalling the Mandate Restarted the 90-Day Period for Filing a Certiorari Petition.**

The Supreme Court’s Rules provide that a petition for a writ of certiorari must be filed within 90 days “after the entry of the judgment” or “after the entry of the order denying rehearing.” Sup. Ct. R. 13(1), (3). An order recalling the mandate and thereby vacating the prior mandate constitutes a new “entry” that resets the certiorari clock. *See United States v. DeLeo*, 644 F.2d 300, 303 (3d Cir. 1981) (holding that recall of a mandate “restores the case to its former status” and “the time for filing certiorari runs from the new mandate”). This principle flows from the recognition that a recalled mandate nullifies the finality of the prior judgment. *See* 9th Cir. R. 27-1 advisory committee note (explaining that recall of mandate “vacates the mandate and restores the court’s jurisdiction”).

Here, on June 12, 2007, this Court issued an order recalling the mandate to consider Buckles’s motion for appointment of certiorari counsel. The mandate reissued on June 20, 2007. That recall vacated the prior mandate—and the prior denial of rehearing—thereby creating a new triggering event for the 90-day certiorari period. *Cf. United States v. Smith*, 433 F.3d 804, 807 (11th Cir. 2005) (per curiam) (explaining that when mandate is recalled and later reissued, the time for filing certiorari runs from the reissuance). Buckles filed his certiorari petition on September 5, 2007—within 90 days of the mandate’s reissuance on June 20. Thus, his petition was timely. *See* Sup. Ct. R. 13(1); *see also* *Kramer v. United States*, 755 F.2d 909, 910 (8th Cir. 1985) (per curiam) (applying same logic of new mandate restarting period).

The district court’s contrary conclusion—adopting the Supreme Court Clerk’s notation that the petition was due July 2, 2007—erroneously ignored the legal effect of this Court’s recall order. Because the certiorari petition was timely, Buckles’s subsequent §2255 motion—filed within one year of the Supreme Court’s denial of certiorari—was also timely. *See* 28 U.S.C. §2255(f)(1).

**II. In the Alternative, Buckles Is Entitled to Equitable Tolling Because the Ninth Circuit Clerk’s Misinformation Reasonably Misled Him.**

Even if the recall order did not restart the certiorari period, Buckles is entitled to equitable tolling of the §2255 deadline. Equitable tolling is appropriate where a petitioner has been “actively misled” or “prevented in some extraordinary way from asserting his rights.” *Pace v. DiGuglielmo*, 544 U.S. 408, 418 (2005). This Court has held that “affirmative misconduct” by a court clerk may constitute an extraordinary circumstance warranting tolling. *See Pliler v. Ford*, 542 U.S. 225, 234 (2004) (recognizing that “a court’s erroneous advice to a pro se litigant” may justify equitable relief); *see also United States v. Guerrero*, 488 F.3d 1134, 1137 (9th Cir. 2007) (per curiam) (tolling §2255 deadline where court-appointed counsel failed to file petition despite promising to do so).

Buckles’s sister, Bonnie Weinberger, contacted the Ninth Circuit Clerk’s office on June 4, 2007, to inquire about the status of Buckles’s motion for appointment of counsel. According to Buckles’s sworn declaration, the Clerk’s office told her that “the timeline for filing the Writ of Certiorari would be stayed until a ruling was made on the Petition for Appointment of Counsel and a new mandate date would be issued.” This statement directly misrepresented the law—no such stay exists under the Rules. Buckles reasonably relied on that misinformation, believing his certiorari deadline ran from the new mandate date. *See* *Holland v. Florida*, 560 U.S. 631, 649 (2010) (equitable tolling “may be appropriate when a litigant is abandoned by his attorney or misled by a state official”). The Clerk’s office is an authoritative source; a pro se prisoner cannot be expected to second-guess such official guidance. *Cf. Whalem/Hunt v. Early*, 233 F.3d 1146, 1148 (9th Cir. 2000) (en banc) (tolling due to misleading information from court personnel).

Moreover, Buckles diligently pursued his rights. He wrote to his attorney on the 21st day after rehearing denial, as required by Ninth Circuit Rule 4-1(e), and promptly filed his pro se supplemental motion on May 3. He then waited for this Court’s ruling before filing his certiorari petition on September 5—well within the 90-day period from the reissued mandate he had been told to expect. This diligence satisfies the second prong of equitable tolling. *See Pace*, 544 U.S. at 418. The district court therefore abused its discretion by failing to consider the declaration and by dismissing the §2255 motion without an evidentiary hearing on the tolling issue.

**Conclusion**

For the foregoing reasons, the district court’s dismissal of Buckles’s §2255 motion should be reversed. The case should be remanded with instructions that the motion is timely, or, in the alternative, for an evidentiary hearing on equitable tolling.
