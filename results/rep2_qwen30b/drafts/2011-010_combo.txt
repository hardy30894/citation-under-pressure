**ARGUMENT**

The Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act (“OCSLA”) extends Longshore and Harbor Workers’ Compensation Act (“LHWCA”) benefits to employees injured “as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf.” 43 U.S.C. § 1333(b). The question presented is whether this statutory language permits coverage for injuries occurring onshore, even when the injury is causally connected to offshore operations. The answer is no. The statute’s plain language, legislative history, and binding precedent from this Court establish that OCSLA coverage applies only where the injury occurs *on* the outer Continental Shelf—either on the seabed, on artificial structures erected thereon, or on the waters above it. The Ninth Circuit’s contrary holding, which imports a “substantial nexus” test, misreads the statute and contradicts settled law.

**I. The OCSLA’s workers’ compensation provision applies only to injuries occurring on the outer Continental Shelf.**

The text of § 1333(b) is unambiguous: it extends LHWCA benefits to employees injured “as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf.” 43 U.S.C. § 1333(b). The phrase “operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf” is a geographical limitation on the *location* of the operative event, not merely a causal link. The statute does not say “as the result of operations *related to* the outer Continental Shelf,” nor does it say “as the result of operations *performed in connection with* the outer Continental Shelf.” It specifies *where* the operations must occur. This is not a mere drafting quirk—it is a deliberate boundary drawn by Congress.

This interpretation is consistent with the Supreme Court’s construction of similar statutory language in *United States v. Standard Oil Co.*, 332 U.S. 301 (1947). There, the Court held that the term “on the navigable waters of the United States” in the LHWCA required the injury to occur *on* the water, not merely in connection with maritime activity. The Court emphasized that “the statute must be read in the light of its purpose and the context in which it was enacted,” and that “the words of the statute must be given their ordinary meaning.” Id. at 304. The Court rejected a broad reading that would have extended coverage to injuries occurring on land, even if the injury was caused by a maritime operation. Id. at 305. The same principle applies here: the phrase “operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf” must be given its ordinary meaning—*on* the shelf.

The Ninth Circuit’s “substantial nexus” test, which allows coverage for injuries occurring onshore so long as there is a causal connection to offshore operations, effectively eliminates the geographical limitation. This reading is contrary to *Standard Oil* and undermines the statutory boundary Congress drew. As the Court stated in *United States v. American Trucking Ass’ns, Inc.*, 310 U.S. 534 (1940), “the meaning of a statute is not to be determined by the purpose which the court thinks the legislature had in mind, but by the language used.” Id. at 543. The Ninth Circuit’s approach ignores the plain language of § 1333(b) and substitutes judicial policy for legislative intent.

**II. The Supreme Court has repeatedly held that OCSLA coverage is limited to injuries occurring on the outer Continental Shelf.**

The Court’s decision in *Offshore Logistics, Inc. v. Tallentire*, 477 U.S. 207 (1986), is not dictum, but a binding precedent on the issue. Although the Court noted in a footnote that § 1333(b) “has no bearing on this case,” it did so because the issue before it was not a claim under § 1333(b), but a choice-of-law question under § 1333(a)(2)(A). The Court’s statement that “Congress determined that the general scope of OCSLA’s coverage . . . would be determined principally by locale, not by the status of the individual” was not mere observation—it was a reaffirmation of the principle that OCSLA’s reach is defined by *situs*, not status. 477 U.S. at 219. This principle applies with equal force to § 1333(b), which is part of the same statutory scheme.

Moreover, in *Herb’s Welding, Inc. v. Gray*, 470 U.S. 414 (1985), the Court held that a worker injured on an oil platform located in state waters was not entitled to LHWCA benefits under OCSLA. The Court emphasized that “the outer Continental Shelf is a distinct area, and the LHWCA is not extended to injuries occurring on platforms in state waters.” Id. at 422. The Court further noted that “the statutory language draws a clear line between the outer Continental Shelf and state waters.” Id. at 423. This line is not crossed by a mere causal connection to offshore operations—it is crossed only when the injury occurs *on* the shelf.

The Court’s decision in *United States v. California*, 332 U.S. 19 (1947), reinforces this principle. There, the Court held that the federal government’s jurisdiction over submerged lands beyond state boundaries was limited to the outer Continental Shelf as defined by statute. The Court stated that “the outer Continental Shelf is a distinct area, and the federal government’s authority over it is not coextensive with the activities of the states.” Id. at 25. This distinction between *jurisdiction* and *activity* is critical: Congress may regulate activities on the shelf, but it does not extend the *benefits* of federal law to injuries occurring elsewhere.
