**ARGUMENT**

**I. The plain text of ANILCA § 103(c) does not withdraw federal regulatory authority over non-federal lands within conservation system units.**

The Ninth Circuit correctly held that Sturgeon’s interpretation of ANILCA § 103(c) is foreclosed by the statute’s plain text. *See United States v. 37 Photographs*, 402 U.S. 363, 369 (1970) (“The starting point in every case involving construction of a statute is the language itself.”). Section 103(c) provides that, with limited exceptions not implicated here, “the management of the [conservation system units] shall be in accordance with the laws and regulations applicable to the National Park System.” 16 U.S.C. § 3103(c) (1976). The statute does not purport to exempt state, Native, corporate, or private lands from federal regulation merely because they lie within the boundaries of a conservation system unit. *See id.*; *United States v. Alford*, 274 U.S. 264, 267 (1927) (“Where the language of a statute is clear, courts must give effect to its plain meaning.”).

Sturgeon’s contrary reading would render the phrase “applicable to the National Park System” meaningless. *See United States v. Mescall*, 215 U.S. 268, 273 (1909) (“A statute should not be construed so as to render any part of it meaningless or inoperative.”). The Ninth Circuit properly rejected Sturgeon’s argument that § 103(c) limits federal authority to federally owned lands. *See Sturgeon v. Frost*, 841 F.3d 1041, 1048 (9th Cir. 2016) (citing *Alford*, 274 U.S. at 267). This Court should do likewise.

**II. The National Park Service’s hovercraft ban is a valid exercise of federal authority under the Property Clause.**

The hovercraft ban is a permissible regulation of conduct occurring on federal lands. *See Kleppe v. New Mexico*, 426 U.S. 529, 540 (1976) (“The power over the public land ... necessarily includes the power to regulate and protect the use of the land for such purposes.”). The Property Clause grants Congress “power to dispose of and make all needful Rules and Regulations respecting the Territory or other Property belonging to the United States.” U.S. Const. art. IV, § 3, cl. 2. This authority is “without limitations.” *United States v. Gratiot*, 39 U.S. 526, 537 (1840). The Court has repeatedly upheld federal regulations governing activities on federal lands, even where those activities occur on non-federal enclaves. *See Camfield v. United States*, 167 U.S. 518, 525 (1897) (upholding federal regulation of fences on private land within federal grazing district); *Light v. United States*, 220 U.S. 523, 537 (1911) (upholding federal regulation of grazing on private land within forest reserve).

Sturgeon’s reliance on *Katie John v. United States*, 720 F.3d 1214 (9th Cir. 2013), is misplaced. *Katie John* did not address the Property Clause or ANILCA § 103(c). *See id.* at 1220. The Ninth Circuit’s decision in *Katie John* is not binding on this Court and should not be followed. *See Cohens v. Virginia*, 19 U.S. 264, 404 (1821) (“[T]he judicial power of the United States ... extend[s] to all cases arising under the constitution and laws of the United States ... [and] this court must decide such questions for itself.”).

**III. The hovercraft ban does not violate Sturgeon’s rights under the Commerce Clause or the Privileges and Immunities Clause.**

Sturgeon’s claim that the hovercraft ban infringes his right to navigate navigable waters is meritless. *See United States v. Appalachian Elec. Power Co.*, 311 U.S. 377, 426 (1940) (“The navigable waters of the United States are subject to national control in the public interest.”). The Commerce Clause empowers Congress to regulate activities affecting interstate commerce, including navigation on navigable waters. *See id.* at 424-25. The hovercraft ban is a reasonable exercise of this authority. *See id.* at 426 (“The power to regulate navigation is the power to prescribe rules for the proper management of the water.”).

Sturgeon’s reliance on *Slaughter-House Cases*, 83 U.S. 36 (1872), is unavailing. *Slaughter-House* did not address federal regulatory authority over navigable waters. *See id.* at 79. The Court has since clarified that the federal government’s authority over navigable waters is plenary. *See Appalachian Elec. Power Co.*, 311 U.S. at 426. The hovercraft ban does not violate the Privileges and Immunities Clause. *See id.*

**IV. The National Park Service’s interpretation of ANILCA § 103(c) is entitled to deference.**

The National Park Service’s interpretation of ANILCA § 103(c) is entitled to deference under *Bowles v. Seminole Rock & Sand Co.*, 325 U.S. 410, 414 (1945). The Court has repeatedly deferred to agency interpretations of statutes they administer. *See id.* The Ninth Circuit properly applied *Seminole Rock* in this case. *See Sturgeon*, 841 F.3d at 1048. This Court should do likewise.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Ninth Circuit should be affirmed.
