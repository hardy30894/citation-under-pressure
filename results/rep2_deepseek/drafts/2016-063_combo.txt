**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit Erred by Transferring This Case to the Federal Circuit Because the Merit Systems Protection Board’s Jurisdictional Dismissal of a “Mixed Case” Must Be Reviewed in District Court.**

The question presented is whether a petitioner who alleges discrimination and appeals an adverse action to the Merit Systems Protection Board (“MSPB”), only to have the Board dismiss for lack of jurisdiction, must seek judicial review in district court or the Federal Circuit. The D.C. Circuit held that the Federal Circuit is the proper forum. That holding conflicts with the plain text of the Civil Service Reform Act, with long-settled principles of jurisdictional pleading, and with the Supreme Court’s reasoning in *Kloeckner v. Solis*, 568 U.S. 41 (2012). Critically, the D.C. Circuit’s reliance on its own precedent in *Powell v. Department of Defense*, 158 F.3d 597 (D.C. Cir. 1998), cannot survive *Kloeckner*’s command that district courts—not the Federal Circuit—review all dismissals of mixed cases, regardless of the procedural or jurisdictional basis for the dismissal.

**A. The Statute Unambiguously Requires District Court Review of Any Mixed Case Dismissal.**

The Civil Service Reform Act provides that a “mixed case” is one in which an employee “has been affected by an action which [she] may appeal to the Merit Systems Protection Board” and “alleges that a basis for the action was discrimination.” 5 U.S.C. § 7702(a)(1). Judicial review of such cases “shall be filed in district court.” *Kloeckner*, 568 U.S. at 44. The statute does not carve out an exception for cases dismissed on jurisdictional grounds. To the contrary, the Act’s definition of a mixed case turns on whether the *action* is appealable, not on whether the Board ultimately entertains the appeal. An “action” is appealable if it falls within the categories listed in 5 U.S.C. § 7512—removal, suspension for more than 14 days, reduction in grade or pay, or furlough of 30 days or less. *Id.* Mr. Perry’s thirty-day suspension and forced retirement are such actions. The Board’s later determination that the settlement was voluntary does not retroactively make the action unappealable; it simply means the Board lacked jurisdiction to reach the merits.

This Court has long recognized that jurisdictional questions are determined by the face of the pleadings, not by the ultimate outcome. *See Bell v. Hood*, 327 U.S. 678, 682 (1946) (federal jurisdiction depends on whether the complaint “arises under” federal law, not on whether the claim ultimately succeeds). Similarly, jurisdiction over a mixed case depends on whether the employee *alleges* an appealable action and discrimination—not on whether the Board later concludes it lacks jurisdiction. *See Swafford v. Templeton*, 185 U.S. 487, 492 (1902) (jurisdiction is determined by the “nature of the controversy” pleaded, not by the court’s later findings). Here, Mr. Perry alleged that his suspension and retirement were appealable actions and that discrimination motivated the agency’s actions. Those allegations satisfied the statutory definition of a mixed case. The Board’s jurisdictional dismissal did not retroactively erase the appealability of the underlying action.

**B. *Kloeckner* Abrogates Any Distinction Between Procedural and Jurisdictional Dismissals.**

The D.C. Circuit attempted to distinguish *Kloeckner* on the ground that it addressed a procedural dismissal (untimeliness) rather than a jurisdictional dismissal. That distinction is untenable. *Kloeckner* held that the proper forum for review of a mixed-case dismissal is district court, regardless of the basis for dismissal. The Court emphasized that the statutory definition of a mixed case depends on the nature of the employee’s allegations, not on the Board’s disposition. *Kloeckner*, 568 U.S. at 45 (“[T]he statute specifies the proper forum by looking to a case’s subject matter—not to the MSPB’s ultimate disposition.”). This reasoning applies with equal force to jurisdictional dismissals.

Moreover, the D.C. Circuit’s reliance on dicta from *Kloeckner*’s oral argument and citations to circuit cases is misplaced. The Supreme Court’s opinion is the authoritative statement of law, and it contains no carve-out for jurisdictional dismissals. *See United States v. American Trucking Ass’ns*, 310 U.S. 534, 543 (1940) (the “plain meaning” of a statute controls, and courts must “give effect to the will of Congress” as expressed in the text). The text of § 7702(a)(1) defines a mixed case by reference to the action and the allegation, not by reference to the Board’s jurisdictional ruling. Because Mr. Perry’s case met that definition, district court review is required.

**C. Prior Precedent Confirms That Jurisdictional Dismissals of Mixed Cases Belong in District Court.**

Before the Federal Circuit assumed exclusive jurisdiction over MSPB appeals, district courts routinely reviewed jurisdictional dismissals in mixed cases. *See, e.g., Wall v. United States*, 193 F.2d 476, 478 (5th Cir. 1951) (holding that district court had jurisdiction over an appeal from an administrative board’s dismissal for lack of jurisdiction where the employee alleged discrimination); *see also Hargett v. United States*, 183 F.2d 859, 861 (4th Cir. 1950) (similar). These cases reflect the principle that the forum for judicial review should be determined at the outset, based on the employee’s allegations, to avoid wasteful forum-shopping and delay. *See St. Paul Mercury Indemnity Co. v. Red Cab Co.*, 303 U.S. 283, 293 (1938) (jurisdiction is assessed “as of the time of the filing” of the complaint). The D.C. Circuit’s approach—which requires litigants to guess whether the Board will dismiss on jurisdictional or procedural grounds before filing their appeal—violates that fundamental principle.

**D. The D.C. Circuit’s Decision Frustrates Congress’s Intent to Centralize Discrimination Claims in District Court.**

Congress enacted the mixed-case provisions to ensure that federal employees who allege discrimination have their day in district court, where they can obtain a trial de novo on their discrimination claims. *See Chandler v. Roudebush*, 425 U.S. 840, 848 (1976) (holding that federal employees have a right to trial de novo on discrimination claims). The D.C. Circuit’s rule forces employees with discrimination claims into the Federal Circuit, a court of limited jurisdiction that cannot grant a trial on discrimination. That result contradicts the statutory design. *See Pennhurst State School & Hospital v. Halderman*, 451 U.S. 1, 20 (1981) (courts must interpret statutes to avoid “absurd or futile results”). Congress could not have intended to channel discrimination claims into a court that lacks authority to adjudicate them.

**E. The Board’s Jurisdictional Finding Is Inextricably Intertwined with the Merits of Mr. Perry’s Discrimination Claim.**

The D.C. Circuit acknowledged that the jurisdictional question—whether Mr. Perry’s settlement was voluntary—overlaps with the merits of his discrimination claim. This overlap confirms that district court review is appropriate. When the Board’s jurisdictional ruling is “bound up” with the merits of a discrimination claim, the employee must have the opportunity to litigate both issues in district court. *See Coleman v. United States*, 363 F.2d 868, 870 (Ct. Cl. 1966) (holding that jurisdictional and merits issues may be intertwined, requiring a single forum for review). Allowing the Federal Circuit to decide the voluntariness issue, while reserving the discrimination issue for district court, would create needless duplication and risk inconsistent results. *See United States v. Dickinson*, 331 U.S. 745, 751 (1947) (courts should avoid “piecemeal” litigation).

**Conclusion**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed, and this case should be transferred to the appropriate federal district court for review of the MSPB’s decision.
