# ARGUMENT

## A CLAIM FOR NOMINAL DAMAGES, UNACCOMPANIED BY ANY PROVABLE INJURY, CANNOT REDRESS PAST CONSTITUTIONAL VIOLATIONS AND THEREFORE CANNOT SAVE AN OTHERWISE MOOT CASE

### I. Article III Requires a Live Controversy Redressable by Actual Relief, and Petitioners Have None

Petitioners' claims for declaratory and injunctive relief are moot. Georgia Gwinnett College revised the challenged policies before this litigation proceeded, and Petitioners have graduated. What remains is a bare request for nominal damages, wholly divorced from any allegation of actual injury. This Court has long insisted that a live controversy must persist throughout litigation, and that relief sought must be capable of redressing a genuine injury rather than vindicating an abstract grievance.

The Eleventh Circuit correctly recognized that a claim for nominal damages, standing alone, does not supply the redressability that Article III demands. The court below observed that Petitioners' complaint rested "on the abstract injury suffered as the result of the violation of their constitutional rights," rather than on any "actual injury." That distinction is constitutionally significant. As this Court has held in analogous First Amendment campus-speech contexts, plaintiffs must demonstrate concrete stakes—not merely the assertion that a right was infringed in the abstract. Compare *Healy v. James*, 408 U.S. 169 (1972), where the plaintiffs sought "reversed and remanded for reconsideration" concrete recognition rights denied to their organization—a live, ongoing deprivation—with this case, where no such ongoing deprivation exists once the offending policies were rescinded.

### II. Nominal Damages Divorced From Actual Injury Do Not Constitute the Kind of Relief That Preserves a Case or Controversy

Petitioners ask this Court to hold that a request for one dollar in nominal damages keeps alive a constitutional challenge to a policy no longer in effect. But nominal damages serve a symbolic function only when tethered to a demonstrated, if unquantifiable, injury. The Eleventh Circuit's opinion below correctly applied the principle that "compensatory damages in a § 1983 suit [must] be based on actual injury caused by the defendant rather than on the 'abstract value' of the constitutional rights that may have been violated." *Slicker v. Jackson*, 215 F.3d 1225, 1230 (11th Cir. 2000). Petitioners' First Amended Complaint never alleged such actual injury; it invoked "irreparable harm" without specifying what that harm was.

This Court's decisions confirm that redress for constitutional violations on campus must be grounded in a genuine, ongoing or concretely suffered injury. In *Board of Regents of the University of Wisconsin System v. Southworth*, 529 U.S. 217 (2000), the students sought both injunctive and declaratory relief to remedy a continuing compelled-subsidy scheme, and this Court evaluated whether the fee program itself—the live mechanism of injury—violated the First Amendment. There was no suggestion that a symbolic damages request could substitute for a functioning constitutional violation. Similarly, in *Widmar v. Vincent*, 454 U.S. 263 (1981), the university's regulation was still being enforced at the time of adjudication; the controversy remained live because the challenged policy continued to operate.

Here, by contrast, GGC's superseded policies are gone. Petitioners identify no continuing restriction, no pending enforcement, and no quantifiable loss. Their claim is precisely the sort of freestanding vindication of abstract rights that Article III does not entertain through the vehicle of nominal damages alone.

### III. Allowing Nominal Damages to Perpetually Preserve Moot Claims Would Undermine the Doctrine of Constitutional Avoidance and Invite Advisory Opinions

Petitioners' rule would effectively eliminate mootness doctrine in constitutional cases: any plaintiff need only tack on a request for a token dollar to insulate a claim from intervening policy changes, no matter how genuinely and completely the defendant has remedied the underlying conduct. That result cannot be reconciled with this Court's consistent recognition that federal courts must avoid rendering advisory opinions on withdrawn or superseded policies. Even in the student-speech context, where First Amendment interests are weighty, this Court has been careful to resolve only live disputes tied to operative deprivations. See *Papish v. Board of Curators of the University of Missouri*, 410 U.S. 667 (1973) (addressing ongoing expulsion and its concrete consequences, including denial of course credit).

The Eleventh Circuit properly limited its holding to the application of *Flanigan's Enterprises, Inc. of Georgia v. City of Sandy Springs*, 868 F.3d 1248 (11th Cir. 2017) (en banc), which forecloses using nominal damages to rescue challenges to policies no longer in force. Petitioners never adequately pled compensatory damages, and their belated attempt to inject such a claim through a response brief was properly rejected as procedurally improper. The court below correctly noted that "[w]here a request for leave to file an amended complaint simply is imbedded within an opposition memorandum, the issue has not been raised properly."

### CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should affirm the judgment of the Eleventh Circuit.
