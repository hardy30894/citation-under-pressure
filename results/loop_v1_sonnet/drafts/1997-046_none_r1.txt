**ARGUMENT**

**I. Woodard Has No Life or Liberty Interest in Clemency Proceedings Protected by the Due Process Clause.**

Executive clemency has never been treated as an extension of the judicial process to which due process attaches. From the founding, this Court has described clemency as an act of sovereign grace, categorically distinct from the adjudication of guilt or the imposition of sentence. In *United States v. Wilson*, 32 U.S. (7 Pet.) 150, 160 (1833), Chief Justice Marshall explained that "[a] pardon is an act of grace, proceeding from the power entrusted with the execution of the laws, which exempts the individual on whom it is bestowed from the punishment the law inflicts for a crime he has committed." That characterization has never wavered. In *Ex parte Garland*, 71 U.S. (4 Wall.) 333, 380 (1866), the Court confirmed that the clemency power "is unlimited, with the exception stated," and may be exercised "at any time after [the offense's] commission, either before legal proceedings are taken, or during their pendency, or after conviction and judgment"—precisely because it operates outside, not within, the ordinary sequence of trial and appeal that due process governs.

The discretionary, non-adjudicatory character of clemency was reaffirmed in *Biddle v. Perovich*, 274 U.S. 480, 486 (1927), where Justice Holmes wrote that a pardon "is not a private act of grace from an individual happening to possess power" but "a part of the Constitutional scheme," reflecting "the determination of the ultimate authority that the public welfare will be better served" by leniency than by the judgment as entered. Nothing in that description casts clemency as a further stage of adjudication subject to the procedural safeguards attending trial or direct appeal.

The Sixth Circuit's reliance on the appellate-due-process line of cases is misplaced. Even direct appeal—far closer to the adjudicatory core than clemency—is not a matter of constitutional right at all. As the Court held in *McKane v. Durston*, 153 U.S. 684, 687 (1894), "[a]n appeal from a judgment of conviction is not a matter of absolute right, independently of constitutional or statutory provisions allowing such appeal," and a State "may . . . make it dependent upon conditions." If a State may condition or withhold appellate review itself without offending due process, it follows a fortiori that a State may structure executive clemency—an act of grace lying still further from the trial—without triggering the same constitutional scrutiny. *Griffin v. Illinois*, 351 U.S. 12 (1956), on which the court below drew by analogy, addressed only whether a State that creates a right of appeal may condition it on ability to pay; it says nothing about the wholly discretionary act of clemency, which creates no comparable statutory entitlement in the first instance.

Due process protections attach only where government action threatens an interest to which the individual is already entitled. See *Cafeteria & Restaurant Workers Union v. McElroy*, 367 U.S. 886, 895 (1961) (the process due depends on "the precise nature of the government function involved as well as . . . the private interest that has been affected by governmental action"). A capital prisoner's hope for clemency is not such an entitlement; it depends entirely on executive grace, as *Wilson*, *Garland*, and *Biddle* establish. Because clemency does not redetermine guilt or reopen a lawfully imposed sentence, but merely asks whether mercy should mitigate a judgment due process has already validated, no protected liberty interest arises from the clemency process itself. The Sixth Circuit erred in extending due process scrutiny—however "minimal" or "barely perceptible"—to a proceeding this Court has consistently placed outside the adjudicatory system.

**II. Because Participation in the Clemency Interview Is Voluntary, It Does Not Compel Self-Incrimination in Violation of the Fifth Amendment.**

The Fifth Amendment forbids only *compelled* self-incrimination. As the Court held in *Malloy v. Hogan*, 378 U.S. 1, 8 (1964), the privilege "is fulfilled only when the person is guaranteed the right 'to remain silent unless he chooses to speak in the unfettered exercise of his own will, and to suffer no penalty . . . for such silence.'" Ohio's regulation does exactly that. A death-sentenced inmate may request a personal interview or decline one; nothing compels him to answer any particular question, and no penalty attaches to silence at any point in the process.

Where disclosure is voluntary, no Fifth Amendment violation occurs. *Rogers v. United States*, 340 U.S. 367, 371 (1951) (privilege protects against compulsion, not voluntary disclosure); *Hoffman v. United States*, 341 U.S. 479, 486 (1951) (privilege applies only where compelled answers would themselves support conviction or "furnish a link in the chain of evidence"). The privilege belongs to the witness, who may invoke it selectively rather than refuse to appear at all. *Brown v. Walker*, 161 U.S. 591, 594–97 (1896); *United States v. Sullivan*, 274 U.S. 259, 263–64 (1927) (a person must claim the privilege as to particular questions, not decline participation entirely). Ohio's regulation preserves exactly that choice: an inmate who requests an interview may answer as much or as little as he wishes.

Nor may Woodard manufacture a constitutional violation from speculation about hypothetical future prosecutions. This Court has cautioned against extending the privilege to remote, contingent harms. *Ullmann v. United States*, 350 U.S. 422, 430–31 (1956) (privilege does not shield against speculative future risk absent a realistic threat of prosecution). Woodard's own complaint disclaims any effect on his existing convictions or sentence, and no adverse consequence follows from declining the interview or limiting his answers. Any risk of self-incrimination is thus wholly theoretical.

Because the clemency interview is optional and carries no penalty for silence, Ohio's regulation does not compel Woodard to be a witness against himself. The judgment below should be reversed.
