The Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act, 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(2)(A), declares applicable and non-inconsistent state laws to be federal law on fixed platforms. This assimilation occurs only to the extent federal statutes leave a deficiency. See Rodrigue v. Aetna Cas. & Sur. Co., 395 U.S. 352, 355 (1969) (state law supplies the rule “to the extent that” federal law is inadequate). The text mirrors earlier enclave statutes that operate solely when Congress has not provided a complete scheme. United States v. Sharpnack, 355 U.S. 286, 294 (1958); Howard v. Commissioners of Sinking Fund, 344 U.S. 624, 627 (1953).

Congress modeled the provision on the rule that, once the United States acquires exclusive jurisdiction, only those state laws existing at acquisition remain in force unless Congress later directs otherwise. James Stewart & Co. v. Sadrakula, 309 U.S. 94, 99 (1940); Collins v. Yosemite Park & Curry Co., 304 U.S. 518, 528 (1938). Subsequent state enactments do not automatically apply. Murray v. Joe Gerrick & Co., 291 U.S. 315, 318 (1934); Standard Oil Co. v. California, 291 U.S. 242, 244 (1934). The same limitation governs the OCSLA: California’s wage-and-hour statutes, enacted after 1953 and amended thereafter, cannot operate of their own force.

The Fair Labor Standards Act supplies a comprehensive regulatory regime for wages and hours, including an express savings clause for more protective state measures. Because that federal scheme is complete, no gap exists for state law to fill. Chicago, R.I. & P. Ry. Co. v. McGlinn, 114 U.S. 542, 546 (1885); Arlington Hotel Co. v. Fant, 278 U.S. 439, 445 (1929). Allowing California rules to apply whenever they are merely “not inconsistent” would convert the OCSLA’s limited borrowing into a general incorporation of all adjacent-state law, contrary to the statute’s text and the settled enclave doctrine.

The district court therefore correctly held that the FLSA leaves no room for the asserted California claims. The judgment on the pleadings should be affirmed.
