**ARGUMENT**

I. The Ohio Adult Parole Authority’s clemency process creates a constitutionally protected liberty interest under the Fourteenth Amendment.

The Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment protects individuals from the arbitrary deprivation of life or liberty without due process. *Palko v. Connecticut*, 302 U.S. 319, 325 (1937). This protection extends not only to life and liberty in their natural sense but also to interests created by state law where the state has made those interests “so bound up with the concept of ordered liberty that the deprivation of them would be a denial of due process.” *Moore v. Dempsey*, 261 U.S. 86, 97 (1923). In this case, Ohio has established a formal, structured clemency process that is not merely discretionary but is an integral part of the state’s capital punishment system. The state’s own regulations require the Ohio Adult Parole Authority (APA) to initiate a clemency review 45 days before a scheduled execution, *unless* the execution date has been stayed. This mandatory procedural trigger demonstrates that the state has not left clemency to pure whim but has embedded it within a predictable, time-sensitive framework. Such a system, when applied to a death row inmate, creates a liberty interest in the process itself.

The Supreme Court has long recognized that when a state creates a procedure that significantly affects a person’s liberty, due process protections attach. In *Meyer v. Nebraska*, 262 U.S. 390, 399 (1923), the Court held that the liberty protected by the Fourteenth Amendment includes “the right to engage in any of the common occupations of life.” Similarly, in *Twining v. New Jersey*, 211 U.S. 78, 98 (1908), the Court acknowledged that “liberty” is not limited to physical freedom but includes “the right to pursue one’s livelihood and to enjoy the fruits of one’s labor.” When a state establishes a mechanism through which a death sentence may be commuted, it creates a liberty interest in that mechanism. The state’s decision to make clemency a formalized, time-bound process—rather than a purely discretionary act—transforms what would otherwise be a mere administrative function into a matter of constitutional significance.

The APA’s regulation, which mandates a clemency review 45 days before execution, is not a mere formality. It is a procedural step that directly affects the outcome of a capital sentence. The state has thus created a structured opportunity for relief, and to deny due process protections to an inmate seeking that relief would be a violation of fundamental fairness. *Palko*, 302 U.S. at 325. The state’s own actions—by requiring the APA to act—have made the clemency process a necessary component of the capital punishment system. To hold otherwise would allow the state to create a process that is binding in form but immune from constitutional scrutiny, which is inconsistent with the principle that “no person shall be deprived of life, liberty, or property, without due process of law.” *Fifth Amendment*.

II. The option to participate in a clemency interview, while technically voluntary, creates an unconstitutional condition that coerces a waiver of the Fifth Amendment privilege against self-incrimination.

The Fifth Amendment protects individuals from being compelled to be witnesses against themselves. *Cummings v. Missouri*, 44 U.S. (3 How.) 277, 322 (1845). This right is not limited to criminal trials; it applies to any situation where a person may be compelled to provide testimony that could be used against them. *Coffin v. United States*, 156 U.S. 432, 453 (1895). The Supreme Court has repeatedly emphasized that the privilege is “a fundamental personal right and privilege” that “cannot be lightly overridden.” *Hale v. Henkel*, 201 U.S. 43, 65 (1906).

In this case, the APA offers a clemency interview as a voluntary option, but the structure of the process creates a coercive environment. The inmate is informed that any statement made during the interview will be considered at the clemency hearing. The state, through its regulation, effectively conditions the opportunity for clemency on the inmate’s willingness to speak. While the regulation does not *require* the inmate to speak, the practical effect is that the inmate must choose between silence—potentially forfeiting any chance of clemency—or speaking, risking self-incrimination. This is precisely the kind of “unconstitutional condition” the Court has condemned. *United States v. Jackson*, 390 U.S. 570, 583 (1968) (plurality opinion); *Cummings*, 44 U.S. at 322.

The Supreme Court has long recognized that a state cannot force a person to choose between a constitutional right and a benefit. In *Palko*, 302 U.S. at 325, the Court held that “the Fourteenth Amendment protects the individual against arbitrary or oppressive action by the state.” To compel a death row inmate to waive his Fifth Amendment right in order to obtain a chance at clemency is an arbitrary and oppressive act. The state may not use the threat of losing a chance at mercy as a tool to extract self-incriminating statements. *Coffin*, 156 U.S. at 453.

Moreover, the state’s own interest in clemency—its interest in making informed decisions—does not justify compelling a waiver of constitutional rights. The state may obtain information through other means, such as reviewing written submissions, affidavits, or records. The mere fact that the state may benefit from oral testimony does not justify violating a fundamental constitutional right. *Hale*, 201 U.S. at 65. The Constitution does not permit the state to trade liberty for silence.
