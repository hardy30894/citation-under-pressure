# ARGUMENT

## I. The Post-Removal-Period Statute Must Be Construed to Avoid the Serious Constitutional Doubt That Indefinite Civil Detention Would Raise

It is a "cardinal principle" of statutory construction that when a statute is susceptible of two constructions, courts must adopt the one that avoids serious constitutional doubt. *Crowell v. Benson*, 285 U.S. 22, 62 (1932). This Court applied that canon to detention of an American citizen in *Ex parte Endo*, 323 U.S. 283 (1944), holding that authority to detain must be read consistently with the purposes for which detention was authorized, and that detention could not continue once its underlying justification had evaporated. The same principle governs construction of immigration-detention statutes. In *United States v. Witkovich*, 353 U.S. 194 (1957), this Court confronted a statute permitting the Attorney General to require deportable aliens to answer questions "for such period as may be necessary" and, rather than read the provision to authorize open-ended inquiry unrelated to effecting deportation, construed it narrowly to extend only to matters relevant to the practicability of removal. The Court reasoned that a broader reading would raise "the gravest doubts" concerning its constitutionality, id. at 202, and chose instead the construction that kept the statute within constitutional bounds.

Section 1231(a)(6) should receive the same treatment. Nothing in its text compels a reading that authorizes detention with no temporal limit whatsoever. Where, as here, the competing construction—detention limited to the period reasonably necessary to accomplish removal—is equally available and avoids constitutional difficulty, that construction must control.

## II. Indefinite Civil Detention of a Removable Alien Who Cannot Be Removed Violates Due Process

The Fifth Amendment's Due Process Clause applies to all "persons" within the United States, not merely to citizens. *Yick Wo v. Hopkins*, 118 U.S. 356, 369 (1886) (the Fourteenth Amendment's protections "are universal in their application, to all persons within the territorial jurisdiction," without regard to citizenship). This Court has repeatedly recognized that aliens who have been admitted to and lawfully reside within the United States possess substantial due process protections that do not attach to aliens seeking initial entry. In *Kwong Hai Chew v. Colding*, 344 U.S. 590 (1953), the Court held that a lawful permanent resident returning from a voyage retained the due process protections of one "within the United States," because he had "a continuous residence and presence" here, and could not be treated as if he were an entering alien seeking admission for the first time. Similarly, in *Ng Fung Ho v. White*, 259 U.S. 276 (1922), the Court held that a resident alien facing deportation was entitled to a meaningful opportunity to be heard before being deprived of liberty, explaining that "to deport one who so claims to be a citizen, obviously deprives him of liberty," and such deprivation demands the protection of due process. Id. at 284-85.

This constitutional distinction between entering aliens and admitted resident aliens is decisive here. *Shaughnessy v. United States ex rel. Mezei*, 345 U.S. 206 (1953), upheld detention of an alien excluded at the border who had never effected entry, because such an alien "stopped at the border" is treated, for constitutional purposes, "as if he had remained outside." Id. at 215. Mezei's rationale rested squarely on the alien's status as one seeking admission, not on any general principle that the government may detain any removable alien without temporal limit. Petitioner, by contrast, was lawfully admitted to the United States decades ago and has resided here continuously. Under *Kwong Hai Chew* and *Ng Fung Ho*, he is entitled to the due process protections applicable to persons within the United States, protections *Mezei* never purported to withdraw from admitted resident aliens.

Civil detention, even when constitutionally permissible, cannot be untethered from its statutory purpose. In *Carlson v. Landon*, 342 U.S. 524 (1952), this Court sustained detention of aliens pending deportation proceedings, but did so only because detention there bore a direct and reasonable relationship to the government's interest in ensuring the aliens' presence for deportation once proceedings concluded. Id. at 538. Where, as in petitioner's case, deportation is not reasonably foreseeable because no receiving country will accept him, continued detention no longer serves that purpose; it becomes indistinguishable from permanent, purposeless confinement. Even Congress's plenary power over deportation, recognized in *Fong Yue Ting v. United States*, 149 U.S. 698 (1893), has never been understood to encompass authority to imprison indefinitely a person whose only "violation" is presence in the United States after a removal order that cannot practically be executed. And this Court held in *Wong Wing v. United States*, 163 U.S. 228 (1896), that even Congress's broad authority over the exclusion and expulsion of aliens does not permit the imposition of imprisonment as punishment without the safeguards the Constitution demands. Id. at 237-38.

## III. Conclusion

The statute should be construed, consistent with *Crowell*, *Endo*, and *Witkovich*, to limit post-removal detention to a period reasonably necessary to effect removal. So construed, it does not authorize petitioner's continued, indefinite confinement, which the Constitution—as illuminated by *Kwong Hai Chew*, *Ng Fung Ho*, *Yick Wo*, *Carlson*, and *Wong Wing*—will not tolerate.
