**ARGUMENT**

**I. Standard of Review and Statutory Framework**

The Tax Court’s denial of the QTIP marital deduction presents a question of law reviewed de novo. *Estate of Rapp v. Commissioner*, 140 F.3d 1211, 1214 (9th Cir. 1998). The interpretation of the trust instrument, and the legal rights it confers on the surviving spouse, are also questions of law subject to plenary review. *Id.*

Under I.R.C. § 2056(b)(7)(B)(ii), a surviving spouse has a “qualifying income interest for life” if she is “entitled to all the income from the property, payable annually or at more frequent intervals” and no person has a power to appoint any part of the property to anyone other than the spouse. The Treasury Regulation confirms that the spouse must have “the right, exercisable annually or at more frequent intervals, to receive all of the income from the property for life.” Treas. Reg. § 20.2056(b)-7(c)(1). This right is defeated only if the trustee has the power to withhold or accumulate income. *See Estate of Shelfer v. Commissioner*, 86 T.C. 805, 812-13 (1986).

**II. The Amendment Mandates Payment of All Net Income to the Surviving Spouse**

The Amendment provides that “the trustee shall pay to or apply for the benefit of the surviving spouse . . . all of the net income from the trust estate as the trustee, in the trustee’s reasonable discretion, shall determine to be proper” for enumerated purposes. The word “all” is controlling. The trust does not authorize the trustee to accumulate income or to distribute it to anyone other than Mrs. Davis for her lifetime. The discretionary phrase does not qualify the duty to pay “all of the net income”; it merely directs the trustee to determine the manner and timing of distributions consistent with the spouse’s accustomed manner of living. That is precisely the structure this Court approved in *Rapp*.

In *Rapp*, the trust directed the trustee to pay “such amount of the net income . . . as the trustee, in the trustee’s sole discretion, shall deem necessary for the health, support and maintenance” of the surviving spouse, with the power to accumulate any excess. 140 F.3d at 1212-13. This Court held the deduction was unavailable because the trustee could withhold and accumulate income. *Id.* at 1215. But the Court expressly distinguished a trust—like this one—that “requires the distribution of all income” from one that “permits the trustee to accumulate.” *Id.* Here, the Amendment states that the trustee “shall pay . . . all of the net income.” The trustee’s discretion is limited to deciding how much of that income is “proper” for the spouse’s welfare; she has no power to retain income in the trust. *Cf. Estate of Ellingson v. Commissioner*, 964 F.2d 959, 965 (9th Cir. 1992) (recognizing that a trustee’s unexercised discretionary power to withhold income defeats the spouse’s entitlement).

The Tax Court’s reliance on *Shelfer* is misplaced. In *Shelfer*, the trust authorized the trustee to pay “so much of the net income . . . as the trustee, in the trustee’s sole discretion, shall determine to be necessary” for support, and the trust expressly permitted accumulation of the balance. 86 T.C. at 807-08. The court found the spouse was not entitled to all income because the trustee could decide to pay only a portion. *Id.* at 812-13. Here, the Amendment contains no power to accumulate and, critically, begins with the imperative “shall pay . . . all of the net income.” When read as a whole, the spouse’s right to the entire income stream is unconditional.

**III. The “Other Sources” Provision Does Not Negative Entitlement to All Income**

The Commissioner’s argument that the trustee may reduce distributions because Mrs. Davis has other resources also fails. Paragraph 4 merely permits the trustee, “in making distributions,” to “consider any other income or resources of the beneficiary.” That factor informs the trustee’s exercise of discretion as to how much of the trust income is “proper” for the spouse’s support and comfort, but it does not authorize withholding income from the trust. The spouse remains entitled to all net income; the trustee cannot accumulate it or divert it to others. Had the decedent intended to condition Mrs. Davis’s income right on her other resources, he would have said the trustee “may pay” such sums as are needed, as in *Shelfer*. He did not.

The parallel provision on principal invasion reinforces this reading. The trustee may invade principal only after the spouse has exhausted her own separate trust. That narrow standard applies only to principal, not income. Its presence demonstrates that the decedent intended the surviving spouse to receive the trust income first and unconditionally, with principal invasion as a limited backstop.

**IV. Any Ambiguity Must Be Resolved in Favor of the Marital Deduction**

Finally, the decedent’s manifest intent was to obtain the marital deduction. The Amendment was executed specifically to provide for his new wife, and the estate elected to treat the trust as QTIP. A trust instrument should not be interpreted to defeat the marital deduction absent a clear expression of contrary intent. *See Estate of Mellinger v. Commissioner*, 112 T.C. 26, 37 (1999). The Tax Court’s cramped reading of the Amendment transforms a directive to pay “all of the net income” into a discretionary support trust, contrary to both the text and the decedent’s evident purpose.

The Amendment grants Mrs. Davis a qualifying income interest for life. The decision of the Tax Court should be reversed, and the estate’s QTIP deduction allowed.
