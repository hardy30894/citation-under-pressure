# ARGUMENT

## I. The plain, mandatory text of Section 1407(a) forecloses any transferee court's use of Section 1404(a) to retain a case for trial.

Section 1407(a) provides that the Panel "shall" remand each transferred action at or before the conclusion of pretrial proceedings. Congress's use of mandatory language is not without consequence; courts have long recognized that a legislative command must be given the effect its words require. *Escoe v. Zerbst*, 295 U.S. 490, 493 (1935). Congress's choice of mandatory phrasing must be given effect according to its terms. As this Court has held, "[i]t is elementary that the meaning of a statute must, in the first instance, be sought in the language in which the act is framed, and if that is plain, ... the sole function of the courts is to enforce it according to its terms." *Caminetti v. United States*, 242 U.S. 470, 485 (1917). Nothing in Section 1407(a) qualifies the remand duty for cases in which a transferee judge concludes, under the wholly separate discretionary standard of Section 1404(a), that retention would be convenient.

This Court has separately observed that "[t]here is no more persuasive evidence of the purpose of a statute than the words by which the legislature undertook to give expression to its wishes." *United States v. American Trucking Ass'ns*, 310 U.S. 534, 543 (1940). Here those words impose an unambiguous duty to remand, not a duty to remand unless the transferee court decides otherwise.

Respondent's countermotion asked the District of Arizona to convert a temporary pretrial assignment into a permanent one by invoking Section 1404(a)'s general convenience standard. But Congress did not leave the duration of a Panel transfer to the discretion of the transferee judge; it fixed that duration by statute, and courts have no license to nullify a mandatory command because they judge that departure serves efficiency.

## II. Because Congress specifically restricted multidistrict transfer to pretrial proceedings, the general transfer authority of Section 1404(a) cannot be used to override that specific limitation.

Sections 1404(a) and 1407(a) serve different purposes and were drafted with different scope. Section 1404(a) is a general venue-transfer provision applicable to ordinary civil litigation, designed to permit transfer for the convenience of parties and witnesses and in the interest of justice. *Van Dusen v. Barrack*, 376 U.S. 612, 616 (1964). It operates only where the transferee forum is one in which the action "might have been brought" by the plaintiff in the first instance—a limitation this Court has strictly enforced even against arguments grounded in convenience or party consent. *Hoffman v. Blaski*, 363 U.S. 335, 343–44 (1960). Section 1407(a), by contrast, is a specialized mechanism enacted for the narrow purpose of coordinating pretrial proceedings among related actions pending in different districts, with the Panel's authority expressly bounded by the remand command. *See Continental Grain Co. v. Barge FBL-585*, 364 U.S. 19, 26 (1960) (describing the individualized, case-specific standard governing ordinary venue transfer).

Where a specific statutory provision addresses a particular subject, it is not displaced by a general provision addressing the same general subject matter. As this Court has explained, "however inclusive may be the general language of a statute, it 'will not be held to apply to a matter specifically dealt with in another part of the same enactment.'" *Fourco Glass Co. v. Transmirra Products Corp.*, 353 U.S. 222, 228–29 (1957) (quoting *Rodgers v. United States*, 185 U.S. 83, 87–89 (1902)). Section 1407(a) specifically and exclusively governs the temporal scope of a Panel-ordered transfer; the general transfer authority of Section 1404(a)—a statute that says nothing about multidistrict litigation at all—cannot be read to swallow that specific limitation.

Moreover, Section 1404(a)'s general purpose has never been understood to authorize a court to alter the plaintiff's chosen forum for trial absent satisfaction of the statute's own criteria. This Court has treated Section 1404(a) as a provision governing the ordinary placement of litigation among federal courts, not as a vehicle by which a transferee court, sitting only by virtue of a limited pretrial assignment, may entrench itself in a case Congress committed to it for a different purpose. *Ex parte Collett*, 337 U.S. 55, 60–61 (1949). Lexecon selected the Northern District of Illinois as its forum. Nothing in Section 1404(a)'s convenience calculus permits a court sitting by virtue of the Panel's pretrial assignment to substitute its own trial-venue preference for the plaintiff's chosen forum, particularly when doing so directly contradicts the specific remand command of Section 1407(a).

## III. The Ninth Circuit erred in treating the conflict as unresolved.

The court below acknowledged "what appears to be a clear statutory mandate" yet declined to enforce it, resting instead on Multidistrict Litigation Rule 14(b) and non-binding circuit precedent. But an agency rule cannot rewrite the words Congress chose, and no court may relax a mandatory statutory command because compliance proves inconvenient. Under *American Trucking*, 310 U.S. at 543, and *Caminetti*, 242 U.S. at 485, the sole function of a court confronting plain statutory language is to enforce it—not to weigh policy trade-offs Congress itself resolved. Because Section 1407(a) required remand of this action to the Northern District of Illinois at or before the conclusion of pretrial proceedings, and because Section 1404(a) supplies no authority to override that specific command, the judgment below should be reversed.
