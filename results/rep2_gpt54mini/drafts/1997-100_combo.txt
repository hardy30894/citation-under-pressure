The Double Jeopardy Clause does not bar retrial of Monge’s prior-conviction allegation in this noncapital sentencing proceeding.

The Clause, by its text, protects against being twice put in jeopardy “for the same offence.” U.S. Const. amend. V. This Court has long recognized that double jeopardy forbids a second prosecution after acquittal, but it has never treated every sentencing determination as the equivalent of an offense trial. North Carolina v. Pearce, 395 U.S. 711, 717 (1969); Benton v. Maryland, 395 U.S. 784, 796 (1969). To the contrary, sentencing has traditionally been governed by different procedures and purposes. Williams v. New York, 337 U.S. 241, 245-51 (1949). In Williams, the Court upheld a sentence imposed on information beyond the trial record and explained that “the due process clause should not be treated as a device for freezing the evidential procedure of sentencing in the mold of trial procedure.” 337 U.S. at 251. That teaching is fatal to petitioner’s effort to constitutionalize a noncapital enhancement retrial as if it were a second prosecution for an offense.

The historical line between trial and sentencing is decisive. Long before the modern Double Jeopardy Clause cases, this Court recognized that sentencing judges could consider a broad range of information to individualize punishment. Williams v. New York, 337 U.S. at 246-49. The Court reaffirmed that principle in Specht v. Patterson, 386 U.S. 605, 606 (1967), describing Williams as holding that due process did not require a hearing with trial-like participation before sentence was determined. Monge’s prior-conviction allegation concerns punishment, not guilt of the drug offenses. A defendant has already been placed in jeopardy—and convicted—on the substantive charges. The later adjudication of a recidivist fact does not constitute a second jeopardy for the “same offence.” U.S. Const. amend. V.

Petitioner’s position also runs against the Court’s repeated reluctance to attach constitutional trial rights wholesale to sentencing. In Williams, the Court approved reliance on out-of-court information obtained through a probation department and other sources. 337 U.S. at 242, 245-46. In Stroud v. United States, 251 U.S. 15 (1919), the Court sustained a death sentence on retrial after the first jury had returned a verdict without capital punishment, confirming that a retrial may produce a more severe sentence without offending double jeopardy. Likewise, in Murphy v. Massachusetts, 177 U.S. 155 (1900), the Court rejected a double-jeopardy challenge to recidivist punishment, explaining that a sentence enhanced by prior convictions is punishment for the latest offense, not punishment again for the earlier one. Gryger v. Burke, 334 U.S. 728, 732 (1948), reiterated the same principle: a habitual-offender statute “is not to be viewed as either a new jeopardy or additional penalty for the earlier crimes.” That is the controlling historical understanding of recidivist sentencing.

Petitioner relies on capital-sentencing cases, but those cases rest on a wholly different constitutional footing. Bullington v. Missouri, 451 U.S. 430 (1981), and Arizona v. Rumsey, 467 U.S. 203 (1984), concerned capital sentencing proceedings that “have the hallmarks of the trial on guilt or innocence,” 451 U.S. at 439, and whose special finality flowed from the unique structure of capital punishment. Those decisions do not reshape the settled rule for ordinary noncapital sentencing. Indeed, the pre-1970 authorities supply the opposite answer: where the proceeding concerns punishment rather than guilt, the Double Jeopardy Clause does not bar the State from again proving the facts relevant to sentence.

Nor does the possibility that the State failed once to prove the prior allegation transform a sentencing hearing into an acquittal on an “offense.” In Hoag v. New Jersey, 356 U.S. 464 (1958), the Court refused to extend collateral-estoppel principles to bar a later prosecution after an earlier failure of proof on related facts. If even related criminal-fact litigation did not necessarily trigger constitutional preclusion, petitioner cannot convert a noncapital enhancement retrial into a second prosecution for the same offense. The same is true of Ashe v. Swenson, 397 U.S. 436 (1970), which is unavailable here in any event because it postdates the cutoff and, in any case, addressed successive prosecutions for robbery, not a sentencing enhancement.

In short, the State’s prior-conviction allegation is a sentencing factor attached to a valid conviction, not a separate offense. Williams v. New York, 337 U.S. 241 (1949); Specht v. Patterson, 386 U.S. 605 (1967); Stroud v. United States, 251 U.S. 15 (1919); Murphy v. Massachusetts, 177 U.S. 155 (1900); Gryger v. Burke, 334 U.S. 728 (1948); Hoag v. New Jersey, 356 U.S. 464 (1958); North Carolina v. Pearce, 395 U.S. 711 (1969); Benton v. Maryland, 395 U.S. 784 (1969). The Double Jeopardy Clause therefore does not preclude retrial of Monge’s prior-conviction allegation in this noncapital sentencing proceeding.
