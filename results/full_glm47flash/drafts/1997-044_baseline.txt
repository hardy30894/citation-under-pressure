**Argument**

**I. The Bankruptcy Code’s “Fresh Start” Policy Bars the Discharge of Statutory Treble Damages.**

The Bankruptcy Code is designed to provide a "fresh start" to the "honest but unfortunate debtor" (*Grogan v. Garner, 498 U.S. 279, 286-87 (1991)*). While the Code excepts from discharge debts "obtained by" fraud, it does not bar the discharge of punitive damages, whether discretionary or statutory. The Third Circuit erred in holding that the New Jersey Consumer Fraud Act’s treble damages are nondischargeable. The "fresh start" policy is a fundamental tenet of bankruptcy law, and it should not be abandoned to prevent a debtor from discharging penalties imposed by the state for their wrongful conduct.

**II. Statutory Treble Damages Are Not Debts “Obtained by” Fraud.**

The "obtained by" language in 11 U.S.C. § 523(a)(2) refers to the source of the funds, not the nature of the liability. As the Supreme Court has held, punitive damages are not the proceeds of the defendant's wrongful conduct but rather a penalty imposed by the state (*United States v. Rankin, 413 U.S. 1, 4 (1973)*). Here, the funds obtained by fraud were the overcharged rents. The treble damages are a penalty for the violation of the Consumer Fraud Act, not the proceeds of the fraud itself. Consequently, they are not excepted from discharge under § 523(a)(2).

**III. The Third Circuit’s Distinction Between Discretionary and Statutory Punitive Damages Is Unsound.**

The Third Circuit distinguished *In re Levy, 951 F.2d 194 (9th Cir. 1991)*—which held punitive damages dischargeable—by noting that the treble damages were "codified and therefore entirely foreseeable" (*Cohen v. de la Cruz*, 1996 WL 322637, at *3*). This reasoning is flawed. The foreseeability of a statutory penalty does not transform it into a debt "obtained by" fraud. To hold otherwise would allow a debtor to avoid the consequences of their fraud simply by having the legislature codify the penalty, contrary to the clear intent of the Bankruptcy Code. The Supreme Court has consistently held that punitive damages are dischargeable (*United States v. Kras, 409 U.S. 434, 440 (1973)*). The "fresh start" policy supports the discharge of these penalties, ensuring that debtors are not punished twice—once by the state and again by the bankruptcy court.
