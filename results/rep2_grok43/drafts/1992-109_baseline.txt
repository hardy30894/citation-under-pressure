ARGUMENT

The Due Process Clause does not require a trial court to apply a heightened “reasoned choice” standard when determining whether a defendant is competent to plead guilty or waive counsel. The single constitutional standard is the one set forth in Dusky v. United States, 362 U.S. 402, 402 (1960) (per curiam): whether the defendant has “sufficient present ability to consult with his lawyer with a reasonable degree of rational understanding” and “a rational as well as factual understanding of the proceedings against him.” See also Drope v. Missouri, 420 U.S. 162, 171 (1975); Pate v. Robinson, 383 U.S. 375, 387 (1966).

That standard governs every stage at which a defendant must decide whether to exercise or forgo constitutional rights. In Westbrook v. Arizona, 384 U.S. 150, 150 (1966) (per curiam), the Court required a competency determination before accepting a waiver of counsel, yet it applied the Dusky formulation without elaboration. Faretta v. California, 422 U.S. 806, 835 (1975), likewise held that a defendant who “knowingly and intelligently” waives counsel must be “literate, competent, and understanding,” but again invoked no standard distinct from Dusky. The same inquiry suffices for guilty pleas. See Boykin v. Alabama, 395 U.S. 238, 242 (1969) (requiring that the record affirmatively show the plea was made “voluntarily and understandingly”).

A heightened “reasoned choice” test is inconsistent with these precedents. It would demand that courts measure a defendant’s decisional capacity against an abstract ideal of optimal choice rather than the functional ability to understand the proceedings and consult with counsel. Nothing in the Due Process Clause or in this Court’s cases supports such a distinction. Competency to stand trial already requires a rational understanding of the charges, penalties, and available defenses; the same mental functioning permits a defendant to decide whether to forgo trial or representation.

The Ninth Circuit’s contrary rule, derived from Sieling v. Eyman, 478 F.2d 211 (9th Cir. 1973), rests on the erroneous premise that waiving rights necessarily requires greater mental capacity than standing trial. That premise finds no support in Westbrook, Faretta, or Dusky. Because the record demonstrates that Moran satisfied the Dusky standard at the time of his plea and waiver, the state courts’ competency determination is constitutionally adequate. The judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
