**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE DISTRICT COURT ABUSED ITS DISCRETION BY STRIKING THE TESTIMONY OF APPELLANTS’ EXPERTS AND GRANTING SUMMARY JUDGMENT.**

The district court’s exclusion of expert testimony under Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 37 and its grant of summary judgment were erroneous. A district court’s decision to exclude expert testimony under Rule 37 is reviewed for abuse of discretion. *See Nat’l Hockey League v. Metro. Hockey Club, Inc.*, 427 U.S. 639, 642 (1976) (per curiam). Summary judgment is also reviewed de novo, but the non-movant is entitled to all reasonable inferences in its favor. *See Celotex Corp. v. Catrett*, 477 U.S. 317, 322-23 (1986).

**A. The District Court Erred in Striking the Testimony of Appellants’ Experts Under Rule 37.**

Rule 37 permits a district court to impose sanctions, including exclusion of evidence, for failure to comply with a discovery order. *See* Fed. R. Civ. P. 37(b)(2)(A). However, "[s]anctions should be tailored to the particular circumstances of the case and should not be more severe than necessary to accomplish the desired result." *Nat’l Hockey League*, 427 U.S. at 643. Here, the district court struck the testimony of four experts without considering less severe alternatives, such as a continuance or monetary sanctions.

The district court’s sua sponte order striking the experts was particularly harsh given that the experts’ unavailability was not due to any fault of Appellants. *See* 8 Charles Alan Wright, *Federal Practice and Procedure* § 2284 (2d ed. 1994) ("[T]he court should consider whether the failure to comply was willful or in bad faith, the prejudice to the other party, and the existence of less drastic alternatives."). The court’s own scheduling orders contributed to the delay, and Appellants acted diligently once the experts were identified. *See id.* ("[T]he court should consider the reasons for the failure to comply.").

**B. The District Court Erred in Excluding the Testimony of Dr. Schroeder and Dr. Watson Under Daubert.**

The district court’s exclusion of Dr. Schroeder’s and Dr. Watson’s testimony under *Daubert v. Merrell Dow Pharmaceuticals*, 509 U.S. 579 (1993), was also an abuse of discretion. *Daubert* requires that expert testimony be both reliable and relevant. *Id.* at 589. However, the district court’s gatekeeping function must not usurp the jury’s role as the ultimate arbiter of credibility. *See id.* at 595-96.

Here, the district court improperly weighed the reliability of the experts’ methodologies rather than allowing the jury to consider any weaknesses in their testimony. *See id.* at 596 ("Vigorous cross-examination, presentation of contrary evidence, and careful instruction on the burden of proof are the traditional and appropriate means of attacking shaky but admissible evidence."). The court’s exclusion of Dr. Schroeder’s and Dr. Watson’s testimony was thus an abuse of discretion.

**II. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN GRANTING SUMMARY JUDGMENT WHERE APPROPRIATE FACTUAL DISPUTES REMAINED.**

Summary judgment is inappropriate where, as here, there are genuine disputes of material fact regarding causation and damages. *See Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, Inc.*, 477 U.S. 242, 248 (1986). The district court’s grant of summary judgment was erroneous because Appellants presented evidence sufficient to create a jury question on both personal injury and property damage claims.

**A. Appellants Presented Sufficient Evidence to Create a Genuine Dispute of Fact on Causation.**

To survive summary judgment, Appellants need not prove causation conclusively; they need only present evidence from which a reasonable jury could infer causation. *See id.* at 252. Here, Appellants submitted expert testimony and other evidence tending to show that exposure to hazardous chemicals at the superfund sites caused their injuries. *See id.* ("[T]he evidence of the non-movant is to be believed, and all justifiable inferences are to be drawn in his favor.").

The district court improperly weighed the credibility of Appellants’ experts rather than allowing the jury to do so. *See id.* at 255 ("Credibility determinations, the weighing of the evidence, and the drawing of legitimate inferences from the facts are jury functions, not those of a judge."). The court’s exclusion of key expert testimony thus deprived Appellants of their right to a jury trial on the issue of causation.

**B. Appellants Presented Sufficient Evidence to Create a Genuine Dispute of Fact on Damages.**

Similarly, Appellants presented evidence of property damage and personal injuries that, if believed, would entitle them to damages. *See id.* at 249. The district court’s exclusion of expert testimony on damages further undermined Appellants’ ability to prove their claims at trial. *See id.* ("[T]he judge’s function is not . . . to weigh the evidence and determine the truth of the matter but to determine whether there is a genuine issue for trial.").

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the district court abused its discretion in striking Appellants’ expert testimony and granting summary judgment. The judgment below should be reversed, and this case remanded for further proceedings consistent with this opinion.
