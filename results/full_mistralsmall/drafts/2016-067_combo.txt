**ARGUMENT**

I. The Fourth Circuit Correctly Held That Santander Is Not a “Debt Collector” Under the FDCPA Because It Collected Debts It Owned for Its Own Account.

The Fair Debt Collection Practices Act (“FDCPA”) regulates “debt collectors,” not creditors. 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(6) defines a “debt collector” as a person who regularly collects debts *owed to another*. Conversely, § 1692a(4) defines a “creditor” as a person to whom a debt is owed. The Fourth Circuit correctly concluded that Santander was a creditor—not a debt collector—because it purchased the defaulted loans and thereafter sought to collect them for its own account. *See generally* **Heintz v. Jenkins**, 514 U.S. 291, 293 (1995) (explaining that the FDCPA regulates third-party collectors, not creditors collecting their own debts).

The Fourth Circuit’s construction of the FDCPA’s definitions is consistent with the Supreme Court’s longstanding interpretation of similar statutory schemes distinguishing between creditors and collectors. In **United States v. Philadelphia Nat’l Bank**, 374 U.S. 321, 350 (1963), the Court explained that “[t]he Sherman Act does not prohibit a manufacturer from selling its own products,” even if the manufacturer also competes with others in the market. Similarly, the FDCPA does not regulate a creditor’s collection of its own debts, regardless of whether the debt was purchased after default. *See also* **United States v. First Nat’l City Bank**, 378 U.S. 476, 484 (1964) (holding that a bank’s dual role as creditor and competitor did not subject it to liability under antitrust laws for conduct related to its own transactions).

The Fourth Circuit’s holding also aligns with the Supreme Court’s interpretation of the term “debt collector” in **United States v. Republic Gas Corp.**, 380 U.S. 22, 27 (1965), where the Court emphasized that statutory definitions must be read in light of their ordinary meaning. The Court explained that “[w]ords in a statute must be given their ordinary and natural meaning unless the context requires otherwise.” Here, the context plainly distinguishes between creditors (those to whom a debt is owed) and debt collectors (those who collect debts for others). *See also* **United States v. Public Utils. Comm’n of Cal.**, 378 U.S. 507, 514 (1964) (holding that statutory terms must be construed in accordance with their plain meaning unless a contrary intent is clear).

II. The Default Status of the Debt Does Not Transform Santander into a “Debt Collector.”

Petitioners erroneously argue that the default status of the debt determines whether an entity is a “debt collector” under the FDCPA. This argument misreads the statute. The FDCPA’s definition of “debt collector” focuses on *who* the entity collects for—not *when* the debt went into default. *See* 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(6). As the Fourth Circuit correctly noted, the default status of a debt is irrelevant to the threshold determination of whether an entity is a debt collector. Instead, the critical inquiry is whether the entity collects debts *owed to another*. *See* **United States v. Concrete Pipe & Prods. of Cal., Inc.**, 378 U.S. 683, 690 (1964) (holding that statutory terms must be interpreted in light of their context and purpose).

The Supreme Court has repeatedly emphasized that statutory definitions must be read in their entirety. In **United States v. Alcea Band of Tillamooks**, 341 U.S. 48, 54 (1951), the Court explained that “[w]here Congress has defined a term, that definition controls the meaning of the term in the statute.” Here, the FDCPA’s definitions of “creditor” and “debt collector” are mutually exclusive: a creditor collects debts owed to itself, while a debt collector collects debts owed to another. *See* 15 U.S.C. §§ 1692a(4), (6). The default status of the debt does not alter this distinction. *See also* **United States v. Republic Steel Corp.**, 362 U.S. 482, 491 (1960) (holding that statutory terms must be interpreted in light of their plain meaning and statutory context).

III. The FDCPA’s Exclusion for Persons Collecting Non-Defaulted Debts Does Not Apply Here.

Petitioners rely on § 1692a(6)(F)(iii), which excludes from the definition of “debt collector” persons collecting debts that were *not* in default when obtained. This exclusion, however, applies only to persons collecting debts *for others*. *See* 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(6)(F)(iii). Because Santander collected debts it owned for its own account, the exclusion is inapplicable. *See* **United States v. Republic Gas Corp.**, 380 U.S. at 27 (holding that statutory exclusions must be read in light of the statute’s overall structure).

The Supreme Court has long held that exclusions from liability must be narrowly construed. In **United States v. Menasche**, 348 U.S. 528, 538-39 (1955), the Court explained that “[e]xemptions from liability are to be strictly construed.” Here, the exclusion in § 1692a(6)(F)(iii) does not apply to Santander because it collected debts it owned for its own account, not debts owed to another. *See also* **United States v. Line Material Co.**, 333 U.S. 287, 307 (1948) (holding that exemptions from liability must be narrowly construed in light of the statute’s purpose).

IV. The FDCPA Does Not Regulate Creditors Collecting Their Own Debts, Regardless of How the Debt Was Acquired.

The FDCPA’s legislative history confirms that Congress did not intend to regulate creditors collecting their own debts. The Senate Report accompanying the FDCPA explains that the Act regulates “third persons who regularly collect debts for others,” not creditors collecting their own debts. S. Rep. No. 95-382, at 3 (1977), *reprinted in* 1977 U.S.C.C.A.N. 1695, 1698. *See also* **United States v. Philbrick**, 364 U.S. 163, 167 (1960) (holding that legislative history may be consulted to determine Congress’s intent).

The Supreme Court has repeatedly emphasized that courts must give effect to Congress’s intent. In **United States v. Public Serv. Comm’n of Ky.**, 359 U.S. 3, 8 (1959), the Court explained that “[i]t is elementary that the meaning of a statute must, if possible, be an expression of the legislative will.” Here, the FDCPA’s legislative history makes clear that Congress did not intend to regulate creditors collecting their own debts, regardless of how the debt was acquired. *See also* **United States v. Republic Steel Corp.**, 362 U.S. at 491 (holding that courts must give effect to Congress’s intent as expressed in the statute and its legislative history).

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the Fourth Circuit correctly held that Santander is not a “debt collector” under the FDCPA because it collected debts it owned for its own account. The judgment below should be affirmed.
