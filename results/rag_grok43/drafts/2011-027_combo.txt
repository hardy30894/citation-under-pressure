The equal-footing doctrine vests a state with title to the beds of rivers that were navigable in fact at the time of its admission to the Union. United States v. Utah, 283 U.S. 801 (1931); United States v. West Virginia, 295 U.S. 463 (1935). The constitutional test therefore requires a trial court to determine navigability as of that historical date. Fox River Paper Co. v. Railroad Commission, 274 U.S. 651 (1927) (rights of the state in navigable waters “are matters of state law to be determined by the statutes and judicial decisions of the state” at the moment of statehood).

This Court has repeatedly held that navigability for title “is determined by the condition of the river at the time of statehood.” United States v. Appalachian Electric Power Co., 311 U.S. 377 (1941). Evidence of later use or non-use is relevant only insofar as it illuminates the river’s condition in 1889. Grand River Dam Authority v. Grand-Hydro, 335 U.S. 359 (1949). The Montana Supreme Court correctly applied this standard when it examined historical records, explorer accounts, and contemporaneous commercial activity on the Missouri, Madison, and Clark Fork Rivers.

The federal navigational servitude does not alter the title inquiry. United States v. Twin City Power Co., 350 U.S. 222 (1956); United States v. Rands, 389 U.S. 121 (1967). That servitude concerns federal regulatory power over commerce; it neither enlarges nor diminishes the property rights acquired by the State upon admission. Federal Power Commission v. Union Electric Co., 381 U.S. 90 (1965). Likewise, the Federal Power Act preserves rather than displaces state-law property interests in riverbeds. United States ex rel. Tennessee Valley Authority v. Powelson, 319 U.S. 266 (1943); Green Bay & Mississippi Canal Co. v. Patten Paper Co., 172 U.S. 58 (1898).

Because the navigability-for-title test is fixed at statehood, the trial court was required to—and did—make that determination. The judgment below should be affirmed.
