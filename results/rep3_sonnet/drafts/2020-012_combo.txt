ARGUMENT

I. Section 355(f) Authorizes Review Of The Board’s Refusal To Reopen Because Such A Refusal Is A “Final Decision” Under The Railroad Retirement Act.

The Railroad Retirement Act provides for judicial review of “final decisions” of the Board. 45 U.S.C. § 355(f). Nothing in that text, or in the structure of the Act, excludes decisions refusing to reopen a claim. The Second Circuit correctly recognized this in Szostak v. Railroad Retirement Board, 370 F.2d 253 (2d Cir. 1966), holding that a Board order denying reopening is reviewable because it finally disposes of the claimant’s asserted right to benefits and leaves nothing further for the agency to decide. Id. at 254–55. The Board’s refusal to reopen Mr. Salinas’s 2006 application was likewise the last word the agency will ever speak on that claim; it is “final” in every functional sense that this Court’s precedents have used the term.

This Court has long distinguished between administrative action that is merely a step in an ongoing process and action that terminates a claimant’s rights. In Chicago & Southern Air Lines v. Waterman S.S. Corp., 333 U.S. 103 (1948), the Court explained that orders having “the effect of final rejection of a claim” are the type of agency action subject to judicial oversight. Id. at 113. A denial of reopening does exactly that: it forecloses, once and for all, any possibility that the claimant will receive the benefit he sought. See also Federal Communications Comm’n v. Pottsville Broadcasting Co., 309 U.S. 134, 143 (1940) (discussing the practical finality required for judicial review of agency determinations).

The Fifth Circuit’s rule—drawn from its own decision in Roberts and cases predating 1970 in other circuits, such as the Sixth and Tenth Circuits—rests on the mistaken premise that a decision not to reopen is categorically distinct from a decision on the merits. But this Court rejected an analogous distinction in Interstate Commerce Commission v. Jersey City, 322 U.S. 503 (1944), where it recognized that when an agency’s disposition of a request to reconsider or reopen effectively terminates a party’s claim, the agency’s order is treated as final for purposes of review. Id. at 514–15. The same logic applies here: once the Board denied reopening, Mr. Salinas had no further administrative avenue to obtain his 2006 disability annuity. That denial was “final” in every sense that matters.

II. The Strong Presumption Favoring Judicial Review Of Final Agency Action Confirms That § 355(f) Reaches Reopening Denials.

Even if the statutory text were ambiguous, this Court’s settled interpretive principles resolve the ambiguity in Mr. Salinas’s favor. This Court has repeatedly held that federal statutes providing for review of agency action carry a presumption favoring judicial review, rebutted only by clear and convincing evidence that Congress intended to preclude it. Abbott Laboratories v. Gardner, 387 U.S. 136, 141 (1967) (review of final agency action is favored “unless there is persuasive reason to believe that such was the purpose of Congress”); Rusk v. Cort, 369 U.S. 367, 379–80 (1962) (applying the same presumption to preclude a reading of a statute that would deny all judicial review of an administrative denial). Nothing in the Railroad Retirement Act rebuts that presumption. Section 355(f) contains no language singling out reopening decisions for exclusion, and the legislative history reflects no intent to insulate the Board’s reopening determinations from review. Under Abbott Laboratories and Rusk, that silence must be construed in favor of review, not against it.

This principle has particular force where, as here, the agency’s decision leaves the claimant with no other means of vindicating a statutory entitlement. In Shaughnessy v. Pedreiro, 349 U.S. 48 (1955), this Court construed a comparable “final order” provision broadly, explaining that Congress does not lightly foreclose access to the courts for individuals seeking to vindicate rights created by federal statute. Id. at 51. The same reasoning governs the Railroad Retirement Act, a remedial statute enacted to secure income protection for railroad workers and their families.

III. The Remedial Purpose Of The Railroad Retirement Act Requires A Liberal Construction Of “Final Decision” In Favor Of Beneficiaries.

This Court has consistently instructed that social-insurance statutes enacted for the benefit of workers must be liberally construed to effectuate that purpose. In Social Security Board v. Nierotko, 327 U.S. 358 (1946), the Court broadly construed the term “wages” under the Social Security Act—on which the Railroad Retirement Act is modeled—to advance the statute’s remedial aims, rejecting a narrow construction that would deprive a worker of benefits. Id. at 364. Similarly, in Fishgold v. Sullivan Drydock & Repair Corp., 328 U.S. 275 (1946), the Court held that remedial legislation protecting individuals “should be liberally construed for the benefit of those” it was designed to protect. Id. at 285. The same interpretive charity is due here: “final decision” in § 355(f) should be read to include reopening denials so that claimants like Mr. Salinas, who may have meritorious but procedurally delayed claims, are not left entirely without recourse.

Finally, this Court has cautioned against administrative finality doctrines that insulate agency error from any judicial check. See Estep v. United States, 327 U.S. 114, 121 (1946) (administrative determinations are not immune from review where doing so would leave affected individuals without any remedy for agency overreach). The Fifth Circuit’s categorical bar does precisely that, permitting the Board’s reopening decisions to escape review no matter how arbitrary. This Court should reverse.
