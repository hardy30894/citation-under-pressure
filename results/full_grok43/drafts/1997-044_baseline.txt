**ARGUMENT**

**I. Section 523(a)(2)(A) excepts from discharge only those debts representing money or property actually obtained by fraud; statutory treble damages that exceed the amount so obtained are dischargeable.**

Section 523(a)(2)(A) provides that a discharge “does not discharge an individual debtor from any debt … for money, property, services, or an extension, renewal, or refinancing of credit, to the extent obtained by … actual fraud.” 11 U.S.C. § 523(a)(2)(A). The phrase “to the extent obtained by” limits the exception to the sum the debtor actually received through the fraudulent conduct. *Grogan v. Garner*, 498 U.S. 279, 287 (1991) (noting that the provision “is intended to protect victims of fraud” by preventing discharge of “the fruits of the fraud”).

The rent overcharges totaling $31,382.50 constitute the only sums Cohen obtained from the tenants. The additional $62,765 awarded as treble damages under the New Jersey Consumer Fraud Act, N.J.S.A. 56:8-19, was not received by Cohen; it was imposed by statute after the fact as a penalty. Because those sums were never “obtained by” fraud, they fall outside the statutory exception. *See In re Levy*, 951 F.2d 196, 198 (9th Cir. 1991) (holding that punitive damages “are not a debt ‘obtained by’ fraud” and therefore remain dischargeable).

The contrary view—that any judgment debt traceable to fraudulent conduct is nondischargeable—reads the limiting phrase “to the extent obtained by” out of the statute. That construction would permit creditors to enlarge the nondischargeable amount through state-law multipliers or punitive awards, contrary to the text Congress enacted. *See Molzof v. United States*, 502 U.S. 301, 305 (1992) (meaning of terms in a federal statute is a federal question).

**II. Allowing discharge of the treble-damages portion preserves the Code’s fresh-start policy for the honest but unfortunate debtor while denying it only to the extent of actual fraudulent gains.**

This Court has repeatedly recognized that the Bankruptcy Code embodies “a well-known purpose … to aid the honest but unfortunate debtor.” *Grogan*, 498 U.S. at 286-87 (quoting *Local Loan Co. v. Hunt*, 292 U.S. 234, 244 (1934)). The exception in § 523(a)(2)(A) is narrow and should be construed accordingly. *See Gleason v. Thaw*, 236 U.S. 558, 562 (1915) (exceptions to discharge “are to be narrowly construed”). Treble damages that function as punishment rather than compensation exceed the scope of that narrow exception.

The tenants’ reliance on *In re St. Laurent*, 991 F.2d 672 (11th Cir. 1993), is misplaced. That decision treated the entire judgment debt as nondischargeable without regard to whether the punitive component was “obtained by” the debtor. The Ninth Circuit’s contrary reading in *Levy* better accords with the statutory language and with the principle that “exceptions to discharge are narrowly construed.” *Grogan*, 498 U.S. at 287.

The judgment below should be reversed insofar as it declares the treble damages nondischargeable.
