**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Due Process Clause Does Not Guarantee a Freestanding Right to Introduce Mental-Disease Evidence to Negate Mens Rea Outside the Insanity Defense.**

The Fourteenth Amendment does not enshrine any particular allocation of proof, or any particular use of evidence, for mental-illness claims. This Court has repeatedly held that “[t]he Due Process Clause does not put a State to the choice of abandoning those altered burdens or of ‘otherwise erecting insuperable barriers to the doctrine of diminished responsibility.’” *Patterson v. New York*, 432 U.S. 197, 202, 210 (1977). In *Patterson*, this Court upheld New York’s rule placing the burden of proving an affirmative defense on the defendant, explaining that the Constitution requires only that the prosecution prove beyond a reasonable doubt “every fact necessary to constitute the crime,” not that a State structure its evidentiary rules in any particular way regarding mitigating mental conditions. *Id.* at 210.

This principle governs squarely here. In *Montana v. Egelhoff*, 518 U.S. 37 (1996), this Court sustained a state statute that barred a defendant from introducing evidence of voluntary intoxication to negate the mental-state element of a crime, even though such evidence was logically relevant to whether the defendant possessed the requisite intent. The plurality recognized that the exclusion reflected a legitimate judgment that such evidence, however probative in the abstract, need not be given controlling effect on the mens rea question. *Id.* at 56 (plurality opinion). If a State may exclude relevant evidence of intoxication-induced incapacity from the mens rea inquiry, it follows that Arizona may likewise channel evidence of mental disease into the insanity defense without offending due process.

The controlling due process standard is whether the challenged rule “offends some principle of justice so rooted in the traditions and conscience of our people as to be ranked as fundamental.” *Patterson*, 432 U.S. at 202 (quoting *Speiser v. Randall*, 357 U.S. 513, 523 (1958)); *see also Medina v. California*, 505 U.S. 437, 445 (1992) (state procedural and evidentiary rules bearing on criminal proceedings “are entitled to a presumption of validity”). No such tradition compels the rule Clark seeks. To the contrary, this Court in *Leland v. Oregon*, 343 U.S. 790 (1952), upheld Oregon’s decision to abolish the “irresistible impulse” test and to require a defendant to prove insanity beyond a reasonable doubt, holding that “the science of psychiatry has not reached the point where its practitioners are able to prove legal insanity”and that due process leaves the States wide latitude to formulate insanity standards and channel mental-state evidence accordingly. *Id.* at 800–01. The same latitude that permits a State to define the insanity defense permits it to define which evidence bears on that defense as opposed to the mens rea element.

**II. Arizona’s Channeling Rule Serves Legitimate State Interests and Does Not Deprive Clark of a Meaningful Opportunity to Defend Himself.**

Arizona’s rule, announced in *State v. Mott*, 187 Ariz. 536, 931 P.2d 1046 (1997), reflects a considered judgment that expert testimony about mental disease—often speculative, susceptible to manipulation, and difficult for juries to evaluate—should be confined to the insanity inquiry rather than allowed to blur the mens rea determination reserved for lay assessment of observable conduct and intent. This Court has recognized that States possess substantial authority to structure the presentation of psychiatric evidence to avoid juror confusion and preserve the mens rea element as a matter for common experience rather than expert redefinition. *See Egelhoff*, 518 U.S. at 50–51 (plurality opinion) (recognizing state interest in avoiding "clever" exploitation of psychiatric or intoxication evidence to escape culpability, and in preserving simplicity and reliability of the factfinding process). Arizona’s rule accomplishes exactly this legitimate objective: it does not exclude the evidence altogether, but assigns it a defined procedural role—precisely the kind of accommodation *Patterson* found consistent with due process. 432 U.S. at 210.

Nor was Clark denied a meaningful opportunity to present a defense. Unlike *Chambers v. Mississippi*, 410 U.S. 284 (1973), where the exclusion of critical, reliable hearsay testimony left the defendant with no means to rebut the prosecution's case, Clark was permitted to introduce all of his mental-health evidence—including expert testimony about his schizophrenia—for its full probative value on the insanity defense. The trial court simply declined to allow that same evidence a second, independent use to negate intent directly, a limitation far less restrictive than the categorical exclusions this Court sustained in *Egelhoff*. Because Clark's insanity defense afforded a full opportunity to present his mental-state evidence to the factfinder, no due process concern like that in *Chambers* is implicated.

**III. The State’s Allocation of the Burden of Proof on Insanity Does Not Transform the Evidentiary Channeling Rule into a Due Process Violation.**

Clark’s argument effectively asks this Court to hold that any evidentiary limitation touching on mental capacity becomes constitutionally suspect whenever the defendant also bears the burden of proving insanity. But *Leland* and *Patterson* foreclose that argument: both cases upheld burden allocations for insanity defenses notwithstanding their impact on how mental-state evidence functions at trial. 343 U.S. at 799; 432 U.S. at 205–06. Arizona’s channeling rule is simply the evidentiary corollary of the burden-allocation choices this Court has already approved.

For these reasons, the judgment of the Arizona Court of Appeals should be affirmed.
