# ARGUMENT

## I. THE TEXT AND STRUCTURE OF OCSLA COMPEL A GAP-FILLING APPROACH

Section 1333(a)(2)(A) declares that adjacent state law becomes "the law of the United States" only "[t]o the extent that they are applicable and not inconsistent with this subchapter or with other Federal laws." 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(2)(A). This Court has recognized that OCSLA's purpose was "to define a body of law applicable" to the Shelf, treating fixed structures "as federal enclaves." *Rodrigue v. Aetna Casualty & Surety Co.*, 395 U.S. 352, 355, 366 (1969). Congress did not intend the wholesale, freestanding application of state law to a zone it declared subject to exclusive federal jurisdiction. Rather, state law serves a narrow, subordinate function: filling gaps that federal law leaves open.

This structure mirrors the classic federal-enclave model. In *Rodrigue*, this Court held that Louisiana wrongful-death law could be borrowed as surrogate federal law only because no federal remedy existed—the Death on the High Seas Act did not reach fixed platform accidents since they did not involve "collision with a vessel" or occur during "traditional maritime activity." 395 U.S. at 360, 366. The Court's holding depended on the absence of federal coverage: incorporation of state law removed "any obstacle" only because the federal scheme left a void. *Id.* at 366. Where, as here, Congress has already legislated comprehensively on the precise subject—wage and hour standards for employees—there is no gap for state law to fill.

The word "applicable" in § 1333(a)(2)(A) must be given independent meaning. If state law applied automatically whenever not expressly preempted, "applicable" would be surplusage; the statute would simply say "not inconsistent." This Court has repeatedly cautioned against constructions that render statutory language superfluous. *TRW Inc. v. Andrews*, 534 U.S. 19, 31 (2001) ("It is a cardinal principle of statutory construction that a statute ought, upon the whole, to be so construed that, if it can be prevented, no clause, sentence, or word shall be superfluous, void, or insignificant."). The gap-filling test gives "applicable" real work to do: state law is "applicable" only when federal law does not already speak to the subject.

## II. OCSLA'S HISTORY CONFIRMS THAT STATE LAW OPERATES AS A NARROW GAP-FILLER, NOT A CONCURRENT REGULATORY REGIME

Congress crafted OCSLA against the backdrop of federal-enclave jurisprudence, under which state law is assimilated only where federal law "was never designed to be a complete body of law in and of itself." 99 Cong. Rec. 6963 (1953). This is the same logic underlying the Assimilative Crimes Act, 18 U.S.C. § 13(a), which borrows state criminal law only for conduct "not made punishable by any enactment of Congress." Congress modeled OCSLA's choice-of-law provision on this gap-filling tradition precisely because it feared that federal law "standing alone" would prove inadequate for the unprecedented scale of offshore development. 99 Cong. Rec. 6963-64 (1953).

The Fifth Circuit's decision in *Continental Oil Co. v. London Steam-Ship Owners' Mutual Insurance Ass'n*, 417 F.2d 1030 (5th Cir. 1969), correctly captured this congressional design, holding that state law applies under OCSLA only where "necessity to fill a significant void or gap" exists. *Id.* at 1036. That test respects Congress's decision to make federal law—not a patchwork of state regulatory schemes—the primary source of law governing this zone of exclusive federal jurisdiction.

## III. THE FLSA IS A COMPREHENSIVE SCHEME THAT LEAVES NO GAP FOR CALIFORNIA WAGE-AND-HOUR LAW

The Fair Labor Standards Act, 29 U.S.C. § 201 *et seq.*, comprehensively regulates minimum wages, overtime, and related employment standards. It contains its own savings clause permitting more protective state law "in a State" to operate concurrently, 29 U.S.C. § 218(a), but that clause reflects Congress's judgment about domestic state-federal relations under the FLSA itself—not a signal that Congress intended additional state regulatory schemes to govern conduct occurring within an area OCSLA places under exclusive federal jurisdiction.

Because the FLSA directly and comprehensively addresses minimum wage, overtime, and recordkeeping requirements for employees such as respondent, there is no "significant void or gap," *Continental Oil*, 417 F.2d at 1036, that California's wage-and-hour laws could fill. The district court correctly recognized that OCSLA does not permit courts to import state labor codes wholesale merely because they are not directly contradicted by federal text.

## IV. THE NINTH CIRCUIT'S "NOT INCONSISTENT" TEST WOULD PRODUCE AN UNWORKABLE PATCHWORK CONTRARY TO CONGRESSIONAL INTENT

The Ninth Circuit's rule—permitting state law whenever it is not affirmatively inconsistent with federal law—would transform the Shelf into a checkerboard of fifty different wage-and-hour regimes, contrary to Congress's expressed desire for a uniform body of law under exclusive federal jurisdiction. *See Rodrigue*, 395 U.S. at 355. Such a rule would also destabilize reliance interests of employers operating across state-adjacent platforms, requiring them to track and comply with the specific labor code of whichever state lies nearest their installation—precisely the fragmentation Congress sought to avoid when it rejected incorporating the Shelf into state boundaries. *See* S. Rep. No. 83-411, at 6 (1953).

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should hold that OCSLA permits the application of state law only when there is a genuine gap in the coverage of federal law, and should reverse the judgment of the Ninth Circuit.
