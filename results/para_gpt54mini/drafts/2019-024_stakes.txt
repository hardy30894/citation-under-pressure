**Argument**

Section 1113(2) bars suit three years after “the earliest date on which the plaintiff had actual knowledge of the breach or violation.” 29 U.S.C. § 1113(2). That text requires real awareness, not mere availability of information. The Ninth Circuit’s rule below—treating disclosure to the plaintiff as enough even if he never read or remembered the disclosures—cannot be squared with the statute’s ordinary meaning or this Court’s ERISA precedents.

This Court has repeatedly recognized that “actual knowledge” requires more than constructive notice. In **Mohawk Industries, Inc. v. Carpenter**, 558 U.S. 100, 107 (2009), the Court observed that statutory text controls, and “actual” ordinarily means what is “present and existing in fact.” ERISA’s use of “actual knowledge” therefore points to subjective awareness, not what a participant could have learned from documents he did not read. The same understanding appears in the Court’s ERISA decisions construing limitation provisions according to their text. See **Tibble v. Edison Int’l**, 575 U.S. 523, 530-31 (2015); **Lockheed Corp. v. Spink**, 517 U.S. 882, 887 (1996).

The courts of appeals have likewise distinguished between possession of information and actual knowledge. The Ninth Circuit itself has said that actual knowledge is a “factual” inquiry requiring examination of the record. **Ziegler v. Connecticut General Life Insurance Co.**, 916 F.2d 548, 552 (9th Cir. 1990). In **Blanton v. Anzalone**, 760 F.2d 989, 991-92 (9th Cir. 1985), the court held that limitations ran from knowledge of the transaction, not knowledge of the law, but the trustees there were “parties to the transaction” and “actually made the decision to undertake” it. Likewise, in **Meagher v. International Association of Machinists & Aerospace Workers Pension Plan**, 856 F.2d 1418, 1423 (9th Cir. 1988), the plaintiff knew he was receiving reduced checks; in **Ziegler**, the plan fiduciaries were expressly told that the challenged election would cause loss of funds, 916 F.2d at 552. None of those cases holds that an ERISA plaintiff has actual knowledge merely because a defendant mailed or posted documents that the plaintiff did not read.

That distinction matters here. Intel relies on plan documents and website postings that disclosed the investment mix and associated risks. But Sulyma testified that he was “unaware” that his accounts were invested in hedge funds or private equity and did “not recall seeing” the relevant materials. On Intel’s theory, actual knowledge would exist whenever a fiduciary disclosed information somewhere the plaintiff could have found it. That collapses actual knowledge into constructive knowledge—the very concept Congress rejected when it amended § 1113 in 1987. See Omnibus Budget Reconciliation Act of 1987, Pub. L. No. 100-203, § 9342(b), 101 Stat. 1330; **Phillips v. Alaska Hotel & Restaurant Employees Pension Fund**, 944 F.2d 509, 520-21 (9th Cir. 1991).

The Second Circuit has taken the same approach. In **Caputo v. Pfizer, Inc.**, 267 F.3d 181, 193 (2d Cir. 2001), the court held that “actual knowledge” means “knowledge of all material facts necessary to understand that some claim exists,” and that mere access to information is not enough. The Third Circuit similarly held that actual knowledge requires awareness of the underlying facts, not simply receipt of documents. **Gluck v. Unisys Corp.**, 960 F.2d 1168, 1177 (3d Cir. 1992). And the Sixth Circuit has explained that “actual knowledge” does not arise from “mere receipt of information” when the plaintiff is unaware of its significance. **Brown v. Owens Corning Investment Review Committee**, 622 F.3d 564, 571-72 (6th Cir. 2010). Intel’s position is contrary to that consensus.

The government’s contrary reading would also distort ERISA’s remedial scheme. Congress enacted ERISA to protect plan participants and beneficiaries, see **Firestone Tire & Rubber Co. v. Bruch**, 489 U.S. 101, 113 (1989), and limitations defenses should not be expanded beyond the statute’s text. Where Congress wanted constructive notice, it knew how to say so. Compare 29 U.S.C. § 1113(1) with § 1113(2). Intel asks the Court to rewrite “actual knowledge” into “availability of information.” The Court should decline that invitation.

Because the undisputed record shows, at most, that Intel disclosed information that Sulyma did not read or remember, the three-year period in § 1113(2) did not begin to run until he actually learned of the alleged breach. The judgment below should be reversed.
