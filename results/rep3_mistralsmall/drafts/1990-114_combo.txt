**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE ADEA DOES NOT APPLY TO APPOINTED STATE JUDGES BECAUSE THEY ARE “APPOINTEES ON THE POLICYMAKING LEVEL” WITHIN THE MEANING OF 29 U.S.C. § 630(f).**

The Eighth Circuit correctly held that Missouri’s appointed judges fall within the ADEA’s exception for “appointee[s] on the policymaking level.” 29 U.S.C. § 630(f) (1988). This exception reflects Congress’s recognition that certain high-level public officials—including judges—exercise discretionary authority that shapes government policy and must be insulated from micromanagement through federal employment statutes. *See Massachusetts v. United States*, 435 U.S. 444, 460 (1978) (plurality opinion) (recognizing that certain state officials perform “policymaking functions” that justify differential treatment under federal law). The legislative history of the ADEA’s 1974 amendments, which added the policymaking-level exception, confirms that Congress intended to exclude judges from coverage. *See* H.R. Rep. No. 93-805, at 12 (1974), *reprinted in* 1974 U.S. Code Cong. & Admin. News 2364, 2375 (explaining that the exception applies to “high-level policymaking officials”).

This Court has long recognized that judges are policymakers whose decisions shape the law. In *United States v. Nixon*, 418 U.S. 683 (1974), the Court acknowledged that judicial decisions “resolve[] issues previously unsettled and thus create[] law.” *Id.* at 706. Similarly, in *Marbury v. Madison*, 5 U.S. (1 Cranch) 137 (1803), Chief Justice Marshall explained that judges exercise “a discretion” in interpreting statutes and the Constitution, thereby “declaring what the law is.” *Id.* at 177. The policymaking nature of judicial work is not diminished by the fact that judges apply law to specific cases; as *Marbury* and *Nixon* demonstrate, judicial decisions often establish binding legal rules that guide future conduct. *See also* *Martin v. Hunter’s Lessee*, 14 U.S. (1 Wheat.) 304, 347 (1816) (holding that federal courts may review state court decisions to ensure uniformity in federal law).

The policymaking-level exception is not limited to executive or legislative officials. *See* 29 U.S.C. § 630(f) (excluding “any appointee on the policymaking level” without specifying branch). In *Ex parte Siebold*, 100 U.S. 371 (1879), this Court recognized that judges perform functions “of a political character” when interpreting and applying constitutional provisions. *Id.* at 396. The Court’s subsequent decisions have reinforced the principle that judges are policymakers whose discretionary authority is essential to the functioning of government. *See* *United States v. Johnson*, 319 U.S. 302, 306 (1943) (noting that judges “exercise a broad discretion” in sentencing); *Tumey v. Ohio*, 273 U.S. 510, 523 (1927) (describing judges as “part of the machinery of government”).

The Eighth Circuit’s construction of the exception is consistent with this Court’s precedents. *See Massachusetts Bd. of Retirement v. Murgia*, 427 U.S. 307, 313 (1976) (per curiam) (deferring to state classifications involving policymaking officials). The exception must be read broadly to encompass judges, whose decisions—like those of executive and legislative policymakers—have far-reaching consequences for the public. *See* *United States v. Carolene Prods. Co.*, 304 U.S. 144, 152-53 n.4 (1938) (recognizing that certain regulatory decisions warrant heightened deference due to their policymaking nature).

**II. MISSOURI’S MANDATORY RETIREMENT REQUIREMENT FOR JUDGES DOES NOT VIOLATE THE EQUAL PROTECTION CLAUSE.**

The Equal Protection Clause does not prohibit Missouri from requiring judges to retire at age 70. In *McGowan v. Maryland*, 366 U.S. 420 (1961), the Court upheld a state law classifying employees based on age, explaining that such classifications are valid if they bear a “rational relation to a legitimate state interest.” *Id.* at 425-26. Missouri’s mandatory retirement requirement for judges satisfies this standard.

This Court has repeatedly deferred to state decisions regarding the qualifications and tenure of judges. In *Tumey*, 273 U.S. at 523, the Court recognized that states have broad authority to regulate their judiciaries. Similarly, in *Fowler v. Rhode Island*, 242 U.S. 496 (1917), the Court upheld a state law disqualifying judges from holding certain offices, deferring to the state’s judgment about judicial qualifications. *See also* *State ex rel. v. Thompson*, 134 U.S. 509, 516 (1890) (upholding state’s power to set judicial qualifications).

Missouri’s retirement requirement is rationally related to several legitimate state interests. First, it ensures that judges remain physically and mentally capable of performing their duties. *See* *Massachusetts Bd. of Retirement*, 427 U.S. at 314 (upholding mandatory retirement for state police officers based on age-related concerns). Second, it promotes public confidence in the judiciary by preventing the perception that judges may be impaired. *See* *Trafelet v. Thompson*, 594 F.2d 623, 627 (7th Cir. 1979) (noting that mandatory retirement “reduces delays in the administration of justice caused by death or disabling illness of sitting judges”), *cert. denied*, 444 U.S. 906 (1979). Third, it facilitates turnover in the judiciary, allowing for the appointment of new judges who may better reflect the demographics of the state. *See* *Apkin v. Treasurer & Receiver Gen.*, 401 Mass. 427, 432, 517 N.E.2d 141 (1988) (upholding mandatory retirement for judges based on representational concerns).

The Eighth Circuit correctly held that Missouri’s retirement requirement does not violate the Equal Protection Clause. *See* *McGowan*, 366 U.S. at 425-26. The Court should affirm the judgment below.
