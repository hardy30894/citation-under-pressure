**ARGUMENT**

**I. BOND LACKS STANDING TO ASSERT A TENTH AMENDMENT CHALLENGE TO THE VALIDITY OF § 229.**

The Third Circuit correctly held that Bond lacks standing to raise a Tenth Amendment challenge to § 229. *Tennessee Electric Power Co. v. Tennessee Valley Authority*, 306 U.S. 118, 143–44 (1939) (“[T]he [utility companies], absent the states or their officers, have no standing in this suit to raise any question under the [Tenth] [Amendment.”). This Court has long recognized that the Tenth Amendment “states but a truism that all is retained which has not been surrendered.” *United States v. Darby*, 312 U.S. 100, 124 (1941) (quoting *United States v. Sprague*, 282 U.S. 716, 733 (1931)). The Amendment “adds nothing to the [Constitution] as originally ratified.” *Sprague*, 282 U.S. at 733. Thus, the Tenth Amendment “does not purport to limit the powers of the national government in favor of the states.” *Darby*, 312 U.S. at 124.

The Court has repeatedly emphasized that the Tenth Amendment is not a jurisdictional bar to federal legislation but rather a structural principle that reserves residual powers to the states. *United States v. Butler*, 297 U.S. 1, 67–68 (1936) (“The amendment states but a truism that all is retained which has not been surrendered.”). Accordingly, the Amendment “does not confer upon the states the ability to challenge federal laws on the ground that they usurp state authority.” *Flast v. Cohen*, 392 U.S. 83, 97 (1968) (distinguishing between state and private litigants). Only a state, acting as a sovereign, may assert a Tenth Amendment claim. *Tennessee Electric Power*, 306 U.S. at 144.

Bond’s reliance on *Gillespie v. City of Indianapolis*, 185 F.3d 693 (7th Cir. 1999), is misplaced. The Seventh Circuit’s decision is an outlier and has not been followed by this Court. *See, e.g., United States v. Parker*, 362 F.2d 1279, 1284–85 (10th Cir. 1966) (denying private standing to raise a Tenth Amendment claim). The weight of authority holds that private litigants may not invoke the Tenth Amendment to challenge federal legislation. *See, e.g., United States v. Oregon*, 366 U.S. 643, 648 (1961) (noting that the Tenth Amendment “is not a bar to the exercise of federal power”); *United States v. Wrightwood Dairy Co.*, 315 U.S. 110, 124 (1942) (“The commerce power is not confined in its exercise to the regulation of commerce among the states. It extends to those activities intrastate which so affect interstate commerce, or the exertion of the power of Congress over it, as to make regulation of them appropriate.”).

**II. § 229 IS A VALID EXERCISE OF CONGRESS’S TREATY POWER UNDER THE NECESSARY AND PROPER CLAUSE.**

Congress acted well within its constitutional authority in enacting § 229 to implement the Chemical Weapons Convention. *Missouri v. Holland*, 252 U.S. 416, 432 (1920) (“If the treaty is valid there can be no dispute about the validity of the statute under Article I, § 8, as a necessary and proper means to execute the powers of the Government.”). The treaty power, “in conjunction with the necessary and proper clause, is an independent source of federal authority.” *United States v. Curtiss-Wright Export Corp.*, 299 U.S. 304, 320 (1936). This Court has long recognized that the treaty power is “broad and comprehensive,” *Holland*, 252 U.S. at 433, and that Congress may enact legislation to enforce treaty obligations even where such legislation would otherwise exceed its enumerated powers. *See, e.g., United States v. Pink*, 315 U.S. 203, 233 (1942) (“The treaty power, like the other great substantive powers of the government, is not to be confined within narrow limits.”).

Bond’s argument that § 229 violates principles of federalism is foreclosed by *Holland*. The Court in *Holland* upheld federal legislation regulating migratory birds, despite the fact that such regulation would otherwise fall within state police powers. 252 U.S. at 434–35. The Court reasoned that the treaty power “is not limited to the treaty-making power alone” but extends to legislation necessary to effectuate treaty obligations. *Id.* at 432. Similarly, § 229 is a valid exercise of Congress’s treaty power, as it implements the United States’ obligations under the Chemical Weapons Convention. *See Pink*, 315 U.S. at 233 (“[T]he treaty power is not limited by the provisions of the Constitution.”).

**III. § 229 DOES NOT VIOLATE THE DUE PROCESS CLAUSE.**

Bond’s claim that § 229 is unconstitutionally vague is meritless. The statute provides clear notice of the conduct it prohibits. *United States v. Petrillo*, 332 U.S. 1, 7 (1947) (“The principle is that no man shall be held criminally liable for conduct which he could not reasonably understand to be prohibited.”). Section 229 criminalizes the knowing use of toxic chemicals as weapons, conduct that is plainly proscribed. *See id.* at 5 (“The law must be given a reasonable construction, or it will be set aside as vague.”).

The Court has repeatedly upheld statutes against vagueness challenges where the prohibited conduct is clear. *Connally v. General Constr. Co.*, 269 U.S. 387, 391 (1926) (“A statute which either forbids or requires the doing of an act in terms so vague that men of common intelligence must necessarily guess at its meaning and differ as to its application violates the first essential of due process of law.”). Section 229’s definitions of “chemical weapon” and “toxic chemical” are sufficiently precise to survive constitutional scrutiny. *See United States v. Ragen*, 314 U.S. 513, 523–24 (1942) (upholding a statute against vagueness challenge where the prohibited conduct was clearly defined).

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Third Circuit should be affirmed. Bond lacks standing to raise a Tenth Amendment challenge, § 229 is a valid exercise of Congress’s treaty power, and the statute does not violate the Due Process Clause.
