**ARGUMENT**

I. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN ADMITTING DEFENDANT’S EXPERT TESTIMONY ON THE CAUSE OF THE LINE’S PARTING BECAUSE IT WAS NOT PROPER EXPERT OPINION UNDER FEDERAL RULE OF EVIDENCE 702.

The district court erred in admitting the testimony of American Steamship Company’s expert, Captain Robert L. Smith, regarding the cause of the manilla rope’s failure. The expert’s opinion—that the line parted due to “fatigue” and “frictional wear”—was not based on scientific, technical, or specialized knowledge within the scope of an expert’s proper function under Federal Rule of Evidence 702. Instead, it amounted to a conclusion drawn from common observation, which is properly left to the jury as a matter of fact, not expert testimony.

Rule 702 requires that expert testimony be “based upon sufficient facts or data,” “the product of reliable principles and methods,” and that the expert “has reliably applied the principles and methods to the facts of the case.” *Daubert v. Merrell Dow Pharm., Inc.*, 509 U.S. 579, 592 (1993). Here, Captain Smith offered no testing, no laboratory analysis, no examination of the broken rope, and no mechanical engineering study. He did not even inspect the rope after the accident. His opinion was speculative: he stated that “the rope had been used for years” and “had seen heavy use,” but offered no evidence of prior inspections, maintenance logs, or load history. Such conclusory assertions about “wear” and “fatigue” are not the province of expert testimony—they are factual inferences that the jury may draw from the evidence.

Moreover, the Supreme Court has made clear that “a witness may not testify as an expert if his opinion rests solely on assumptions that are not grounded in scientific or technical knowledge.” *Kumho Tire Co. v. Carmichael*, 526 U.S. 137, 152 (1999). Captain Smith’s opinion rested entirely on unverified assumptions—namely, that the rope had been overused and improperly maintained—without any objective basis. This is precisely the kind of “sham” expert testimony that Rule 702 was designed to exclude. See *General Electric Co. v. Joiner*, 522 U.S. 136, 142 (1997) (“The trial judge must ensure that any and all scientific testimony or evidence admitted is not only relevant, but reliable.”).

The admission of this testimony prejudiced Cook by shifting the burden of proof onto him. It invited the jury to believe that the rope failed due to normal wear, thereby undermining the central claim of unseaworthiness. The error was not harmless. As the Supreme Court held in *Huddleston v. United States*, 485 U.S. 681, 691 (1988), “an erroneous evidentiary ruling is not harmless when it affects the outcome of the trial.” Here, the expert’s unsupported opinion likely influenced the jury’s determination of causation and contributed to the 50% comparative negligence finding. The judgment must therefore be vacated and the case remanded for a new trial.

II. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN DENYING COOK’S MOTION FOR JUDGMENT AS A MATTER OF LAW ON THE ISSUE OF UNSEAWORTHINESS.

Under the Jones Act, 46 U.S.C. § 688, a seaman may recover damages for injuries caused by the vessel’s unseaworthiness. Unseaworthiness exists when a vessel is “not reasonably fit for its intended purpose,” *Tucker v. United States*, 713 F.2d 1184, 1187 (6th Cir. 1983), and arises from defects in equipment, crew, or procedures. *See also* *Barnes v. United States*, 724 F.2d 575, 578 (6th Cir. 1984).

Here, the evidence established that the boom’s bearings were frozen due to the use of summer grease in sub-zero temperatures—a known hazard. The ship’s master, Captain Derry, ordered the crew to rig the boom in less than 20 minutes despite icy conditions and a lack of personnel on the dock. The crew attempted to deice the bearings with propane torches, but the process was incomplete and dangerous. Cook himself testified that he applied heat to the deck-level bearing while Austin rigged the line. The line was then run through pulleys on a boom whose movement was restricted by frozen bearings—creating excessive friction and stress on the line.

This combination of factors—frozen bearings, inadequate time to prepare, improper grease, and the use of open flame near flammable materials—constituted a manifest defect in the vessel’s condition. *See* *Cunningham v. United States*, 732 F.2d 1232, 1235 (6th Cir. 1984) (holding that a vessel is unseaworthy when safety procedures are compromised by foreseeable risks). The risk of line failure under these conditions was obvious and preventable. The fact that the line had been used for years does not excuse the failure to maintain it properly—especially when the environment rendered it more vulnerable.

The district court erred in denying Cook’s motion for judgment as a matter of law because the evidence, viewed in the light most favorable to Cook, established a prima facie case of unseaworthiness. *See* *Morgan v. United States*, 743 F.2d 420, 423 (6th Cir. 1984). No reasonable jury could have found otherwise. The court’s refusal to grant judgment as a matter of law deprived Cook of a critical procedural safeguard and allowed the issue to go to the jury despite overwhelming evidence of a known, avoidable danger.
