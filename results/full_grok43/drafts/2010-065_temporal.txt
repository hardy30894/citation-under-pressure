The Tenth Amendment reserves to the States powers not delegated to the United States. U.S. Const. amend. X. A private litigant may not invoke that reservation to challenge an Act of Congress. Tennessee Electric Power Co. v. Tennessee Valley Authority, 306 U.S. 118, 144 (1939). There the Court held that utility companies “absent the states or their officers, have no standing in this suit to raise any question under the [Tenth] Amendment.” Id.

That rule follows from the nature of the federal system. “Under our federal system the administration of criminal justice rests with the States except as Congress, acting within the scope of its delegated powers, has created offenses against the United States.” Screws v. United States, 325 U.S. 91, 109 (1945). Only a State, or its officers, may assert that Congress has exceeded those powers and thereby invaded the reserved domain. Tennessee Electric, 306 U.S. at 144.

Petitioner, a private defendant, therefore lacks standing to contend that 18 U.S.C. § 229 intrudes upon powers reserved to the States. The statute was enacted to implement a treaty. Missouri v. Holland, 252 U.S. 416, 432 (1920). When a treaty is valid, “there can be no dispute about the validity of the statute under Article I, § 8, as a necessary and proper means to execute the powers of the Government.” Id. Petitioner may not second-guess that constitutional judgment by invoking the Tenth Amendment.

Because standing is absent, the judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
