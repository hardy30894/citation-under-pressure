The District’s blanket prohibition on religious speech in its facilities, even after hours, abridges the freedom of speech. See Minersville School District v. Gobitis, 310 U.S. 586, 593 (1940) (“The First Amendment, and the Fourteenth through its absorption of the First, sought to guard against repetition of those bitter religious struggles by prohibiting the establishment of a”). The policy cannot be justified as necessary to avoid an establishment of religion. See Zorach v. Clauson, 343 U.S. 306, 315 (1952) (“When the state encourages religious instruction or cooperates with religious authorities by adjusting the schedule of public events to sectarian needs, it follows the best of our traditions.”); Illinois ex rel. McCollum v. Board of Education, 333 U.S. 203, 212 (1948) (program violated Constitution where “religious teachers, employed by private religious groups, were permitted to come weekly into the school buildings during the regular hours set apart for secular teaching”); Engel v. Vitale, 370 U.S. 421, 435 (1962) (“We think that by using its public school system to encourage recitation of the Regents’ prayer, the State of New York has adopted a practice wholly inconsistent with the Establishment Clause.”).

The same principle permits neutral access. See Board of Education v. Allen, 392 U.S. 236, 248 (1968) (“No tax money is expended for the purchase of any books; the money is expended for the purchase of secular textbooks.”); Cochran v. Louisiana State Board of Education, 281 U.S. 370, 375 (1930) (statute supplying textbooks “does not appropriate any money to the private schools” and therefore raises no establishment concern). Historical practice confirms that incidental religious use of public property does not offend the First Amendment. See Santos v. Holy Roman Catholic and Apostolic Church, 212 U.S. 463, 470 (1909) (chapel “always was devoted to the ceremonies and worship of the Roman Catholic Church” yet remained public property).

Equal access for religious and nonreligious groups alike does not discriminate. See Cumming v. Richmond County Board of Education, 175 U.S. 528, 544 (1899) (“It being in their discretion, they could, without a violation of the law or of any constitution, devote a portion of the taxes collected for school purposes to the support of this high school”). Nor does it impair free exercise. See University v. People, 99 U.S. 309, 321 (1879) (constitutional exemption for school property turns on whether the property serves “school purposes,” not on the religious character of the user). The District’s refusal therefore violates the speech guarantee without advancing any legitimate establishment interest.
