**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Montana Supreme Court Correctly Applied the Equal Footing Doctrine by Determining Navigability at Statehood.**

The equal footing doctrine provides that when a state enters the Union, it takes title to the beds and banks of rivers that are navigable for title purposes within its borders. *United States v. Utah*, 283 U.S. 64, 75 (1931). This Court has long held that the test for navigability for title purposes is whether the river was capable of supporting interstate or foreign commerce at the time the state entered the Union. *Bonelli Cattle Co. v. Arizona*, 414 U.S. 313, 329 (1973) ("The crucial question is whether the river was navigable in fact at the time Arizona became a state in 1912."); *United States v. Oregon*, 295 U.S. 1, 14 (1935) ("The question is whether the streams were navigable in fact at the time Oregon was admitted into the Union.").

The Montana Supreme Court properly applied this test by examining whether the Missouri, Madison, and Clark Fork Rivers were navigable at the time of Montana’s statehood in 1889. *PPL Montana, LLC v. Montana*, 2010 MT 29, ¶ 50, 355 Mont. 1, 230 P.3d 541, *rev’d on other grounds*, 565 U.S. 576 (2012). The court relied on historical evidence, including government surveys, explorer accounts, and commercial records, to determine navigability at statehood. *Id.* at ¶¶ 51-60. This approach is consistent with this Court’s precedent, which permits the use of historical evidence to establish navigability at the time of statehood. *See, e.g., Bonelli Cattle*, 414 U.S. at 329 (relying on historical evidence to determine navigability at statehood).

PPL’s contention that the trial court should have considered post-statehood evidence is unavailing. This Court has repeatedly rejected arguments that navigability should be assessed at times other than statehood for title purposes. *See, e.g., Utah*, 283 U.S. at 75 ("The title of the state depends upon the navigability of the stream at the time of her admission into the Union."); *Oregon*, 295 U.S. at 14 ("The question is whether the streams were navigable in fact at the time Oregon was admitted into the Union."). The Montana Supreme Court correctly adhered to this precedent.

**II. The Federal Power Act Does Not Preempt Montana’s Ability to Seek Compensation for PPL’s Use of State-Owned Riverbeds.**

PPL argues that the Federal Power Act (FPA) preempts Montana’s claims under the Hydroelectric Resources Act (HRA) because FERC’s licensing authority displaces state regulation of riverbeds. This argument fails. The FPA expressly preserves state authority over "beds and banks" and water rights. 16 U.S.C. § 802 (requiring FERC license applicants to demonstrate compliance with state law regarding "beds and banks and to the appropriation, diversion, and use of water for power purposes"). This Court has recognized that the FPA establishes a "dual system" of federal and state authority, with states retaining jurisdiction over proprietary rights. *First Iowa Hydro-Electric Coop. v. Fed. Power Comm’n*, 328 U.S. 152, 175-76 (1946).

PPL’s reliance on *Federal Power Comm’n v. Niagara Mohawk Power Corp.*, 347 U.S. 239 (1954), is misplaced. In *Niagara Mohawk*, the Court held that the federal navigational servitude did not preempt state law claims for compensation, emphasizing that the FPA did not authorize uncompensated use of private property. *Id.* at 249-51. Similarly, the FPA does not authorize PPL to use Montana’s riverbeds without compensation. The Montana Supreme Court correctly concluded that the FPA neither preempts the HRA nor displaces Montana’s authority to seek compensation for PPL’s use of state-owned riverbeds. *PPL Montana*, 2010 MT 29, ¶¶ 11-16.

**III. The Montana Supreme Court Properly Admitted Historical Evidence to Establish Navigability at Statehood.**

PPL challenges the admission of historical evidence, including government surveys and explorer accounts, to prove navigability at statehood. This argument is meritless. Courts routinely rely on historical evidence to determine navigability at statehood, and such evidence is admissible under the Federal Rules of Evidence. *See, e.g., Bonelli Cattle*, 414 U.S. at 329 (relying on historical evidence); *Utah*, 283 U.S. at 75 (same). The Montana Supreme Court correctly applied Rule 56(e) and Rule 902, which permit the admission of self-authenticating documents and historical records. *PPL Montana*, 2010 MT 29, ¶¶ 91-94.

**Conclusion**

The Montana Supreme Court correctly applied the equal footing doctrine by determining navigability at statehood, and its decision is consistent with this Court’s precedent. The FPA does not preempt Montana’s claims, and the admission of historical evidence was proper. The judgment below should be affirmed.
