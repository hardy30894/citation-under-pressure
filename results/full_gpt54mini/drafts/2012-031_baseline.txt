**Argument**

Rule 23 does not permit class certification on speculation. A plaintiff seeking certification under Rule 23(b)(3) must prove—by a preponderance of the evidence—that common issues predominate. *Wal-Mart Stores, Inc. v. Dukes*, 564 U.S. 338, 350-51 (2011); *In re Hydrogen Peroxide Antitrust Litig.*, 552 F.3d 305, 320-21 (3d Cir. 2008). That burden includes showing that liability and damages can be established through evidence common to the class. *Hydrogen Peroxide*, 552 F.3d at 311, 320. A district court must conduct a “rigorous analysis,” id. at 316-17, and may not certify a class based on “mere allegations” or an untested expert assertion that a classwide method exists. *Wal-Mart*, 564 U.S. at 350; *Hydrogen Peroxide*, 552 F.3d at 320-21.

That rule controls here. The court of appeals affirmed certification even though the district court narrowed the case to one theory of antitrust impact—reduced overbuilder competition—but nevertheless accepted a damages model that did not measure damages attributable solely to that theory. The result conflicts with Rule 23 because the proposed model must “measure only those damages attributable to” the theory of antitrust impact that survives certification. *Comcast Corp. v. Behrend*, 569 U.S. 27, 35 (2013). A model that “does not isolate damages resulting from any one theory of antitrust impact” cannot establish predominance. Id. at 36.

The district court found that classwide impact existed only on the theory that Comcast’s clustering conduct deterred overbuilders in the Philadelphia DMA. But Plaintiffs’ expert model bundled together several alleged harms, including supposed price effects from other theories the court rejected. That is fatal. In antitrust class actions, damages must flow from the same injury common to the class; a model that encompasses rejected theories cannot show that damages are capable of classwide proof. *Comcast*, 569 U.S. at 35-36; *Hydrogen Peroxide*, 552 F.3d at 307, 321-22. As this Court explained in *Hydrogen Peroxide*, the district court must resolve factual disputes relevant to certification and determine whether the evidence proves the Rule 23 requirements. 552 F.3d at 320-21. It may not accept a damages methodology that assumes liability theories the court has already excluded. See *Wal-Mart*, 564 U.S. at 350-51; *Comcast*, 569 U.S. at 35-36.

Nor can Plaintiffs avoid that defect by invoking a generalized “common methodology.” Rule 23 requires proof, not just a methodology in the abstract. *Hydrogen Peroxide*, 552 F.3d at 320-21. The Third Circuit’s decision effectively allowed certification so long as Plaintiffs could identify some econometric model, even if it did not correspond to the sole theory of impact left in the case. That approach contradicts *Comcast*, which held that “no model supporting a plaintiff’s damages case may be certified under Rule 23(b)(3) if it contains damages not traceable to the theory of liability.” 569 U.S. at 38. The district court therefore abused its discretion by certifying a class without adequate admissible evidence that damages could be measured on a classwide basis for the particular theory that purportedly united the class. *Hydrogen Peroxide*, 552 F.3d at 320-21; *Comcast*, 569 U.S. at 35-38.

The decision below also conflicts with this Court’s and the Supreme Court’s insistence that certification cannot turn on disputed merits assumptions. *Eisen v. Carlisle & Jacquelin*, 417 U.S. 156, 177-78 (1974), teaches that a court may not conduct a merits trial in the guise of certification. Yet the premise of certification here was that Plaintiffs would later show damages through a model not yet established as tied to the only viable antitrust theory. Rule 23 does not permit such forward-looking optimism. *Wal-Mart*, 564 U.S. at 350-51; *Hydrogen Peroxide*, 552 F.3d at 320-21.

This case is important because it preserves the line between certification and merits adjudication. A class may be certified only when the record contains admissible evidence showing that damages can be measured in a way common to the class and consistent with the surviving theory of liability. *Comcast*, 569 U.S. at 35-36; *Hydrogen Peroxide*, 552 F.3d at 320-21. Because Plaintiffs failed to make that showing, the class should not have been certified.
