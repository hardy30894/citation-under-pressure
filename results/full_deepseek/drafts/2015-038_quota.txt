**ARGUMENT**

The Ninth Circuit correctly held that ANILCA § 103(c) does not strip the National Park Service of authority to enforce generally applicable regulations on non-federally owned lands within Alaska’s conservation system units. The text, structure, and purpose of ANILCA, together with this Court’s precedents, confirm that NPS retains regulatory control over state-owned lands and waters within park boundaries.

**I. Section 103(c) Limits Only Regulations Promulgated “Solely for the Management of Public Lands”**

ANILCA provides that non-federally owned lands within conservation system units “shall not be subject to the regulations promulgated pursuant to this Act *solely* for the management of public lands.” 16 U.S.C. § 3103(c) (emphasis added). The modifier “solely” is dispositive. A regulation like the hovercraft ban, which applies uniformly to all lands and waters within a park unit to protect visitor safety and park resources, is not a regulation directed exclusively at public lands. It is a regulation of general applicability. Where the statutory text is plain, the inquiry ends. *Chevron, U.S.A., Inc. v. Natural Res. Def. Council, Inc.*, 467 U.S. 837, 842–43 (1984). The Ninth Circuit’s reading—that § 103(c) exempts non-federal lands only from regulations that apply solely to public lands—is the only interpretation that gives effect to the word “solely.”

**II. ANILCA’s Structure and Purpose Confirm NPS Authority Over Entire Park Units**

Congress created conservation system units to protect entire ecosystems, not merely federal fee lands. The Act defines a “conservation system unit” to include the full area within its boundaries, and NPS’s organic statute authorizes it to regulate all lands within park boundaries to preserve park resources. *See* 54 U.S.C. § 100101(a). This Court has long recognized Congress’s power to protect public lands and resources. *Kleppe v. New Mexico*, 426 U.S. 529, 540 (1976). That authority extends to navigable waters, even where title to submerged lands rests with the State. *United States v. Alaska*, 521 U.S. 1, 5 (1997); *Alaska v. United States*, 545 U.S. 194, 204 (2005). Thus, Alaska’s ownership of the riverbed does not defeat NPS’s regulatory jurisdiction over activities on the Nation River.

**III. The Katie John Decisions Foreclose Sturgeon’s Argument**

The Ninth Circuit has already rejected the argument that state ownership of submerged lands removes navigable waters from NPS’s ANILCA jurisdiction. In *State of Alaska v. Babbitt (Katie John I)*, 72 F.3d 698, 704 (9th Cir. 1995), the court held that ANILCA’s subsistence provisions apply to navigable waters within park units, notwithstanding Alaska’s title to the submerged lands. In *John v. United States (Katie John III)*, 720 F.3d 1214, 1224 (9th Cir. 2013), the court reaffirmed that ANILCA does not limit NPS authority over navigable waters. These decisions are consistent with the text and demonstrate that NPS regulatory authority extends to state-owned waters within park boundaries.

**IV. NPS’s Interpretation Is Entitled to Deference**

Even if § 103(c) were ambiguous, NPS’s interpretation—that it may enforce generally applicable regulations on non-federal lands—is reasonable and entitled to deference. *Chevron*, 467 U.S. at 844. The agency has consistently applied its regulations to all lands within park units, and its interpretation is supported by ANILCA’s purpose of preserving park resources. *See Skidmore v. Swift & Co.*, 323 U.S. 134, 140 (1944) (agency interpretations are entitled to respect). This Court has likewise instructed that federal regulations governing navigable waters should be read broadly. *United States v. Locke*, 529 U.S. 89, 99 (2000). And in *United States v. Riverside Bayview Homes, Inc.*, 474 U.S. 121, 133 (1985), the Court upheld an agency’s broad construction of its regulatory jurisdiction where the statute’s purpose supported it. The same principle applies here.

**Conclusion**

The judgment of the Ninth Circuit should be affirmed.
