**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether federal courts have exclusive jurisdiction in any case involving patent law, even when the patent issue is not the primary issue—requires this Court to resolve a fundamental tension between federal patent policy and the constitutional allocation of judicial power. The Supreme Court has long recognized that federal courts possess exclusive jurisdiction over patent-related claims when the resolution of a substantial federal issue is necessary to the outcome of the case. Here, the state-law legal malpractice claim necessarily depends on the resolution of a disputed, substantial federal patent issue—the experimental use exception to the on-sale bar—making federal jurisdiction not only proper but exclusive under *Christianson v. Colt Indus. Operating Corp.*, 486 U.S. 800, 808–09, 108 S.Ct. 2166, 100 L.Ed.2d 811 (1988), and *Grable & Sons Metal Prods., Inc. v. Darue Eng’g & Mfg.*, 545 U.S. 308, 314, 125 S.Ct. 2363, 162 L.Ed.2d 257 (2005). The Texas Supreme Court erred in concluding otherwise.

### I. THE LEGAL MALPRACTICE CLAIM RELIES ON A SUBSTANTIAL, DISPUTED FEDERAL PATENT ISSUE THAT IS NECESSARY TO RESOLUTION OF THE CLAIM

Under *Christianson*, federal jurisdiction exists when a plaintiff’s right to relief “necessarily depends on resolution of a substantial question of federal patent law.” 486 U.S. at 809, 108 S.Ct. 2166. This standard is not satisfied merely because a federal issue is “involved” or “implicated.” Rather, the federal issue must be “necessary” to the claim. Id. Here, Minton’s malpractice claim cannot succeed without proving that the experimental use exception would have allowed the '643 patent to survive the on-sale bar. Without that defense, the patent was invalid, and Minton’s underlying infringement suit failed. Thus, the viability of the experimental use exception is not merely a background matter—it is the linchpin of Minton’s entire claim.

The Federal Circuit has repeatedly held that the experimental use exception is a “substantial” and “disputed” issue when raised in the context of patent litigation. See *Electromotive Div. of Gen. Motors Corp. v. Tramp. Sys. Div. of Gen. Elec. Co.*, 417 F.3d 1203, 1210 (Fed.Cir.2005) (holding that the exception is a “recognized defense” to the on-sale bar). In *Air Measurement Technologies, Inc. v. Akin Gump Strauss Hauer & Feld, L.L.P.*, 504 F.3d 1262, 1273 (Fed.Cir.2007), the court affirmed that when a state malpractice claim “requires the application of federal patent law to determine whether the underlying patent was valid,” federal jurisdiction is exclusive. The court emphasized that “establishing patent infringement is a necessary element of a malpractice claim stemming from mishandling of earlier patent litigation,” and that such claims “arise under” federal law. Id. at 1273. This precedent is binding on this Court in the context of federal question jurisdiction, particularly where the issue is one of statutory interpretation under § 1338(a).

The Texas Supreme Court’s attempt to distinguish *Air Measurement* and *Immunocept* on the grounds that they “fail to apply the federalism analysis required by *Grable*” is misplaced. *Grable* does not require courts to ignore binding precedent from the Federal Circuit when that precedent is consistent with Supreme Court doctrine. Rather, *Grable* requires courts to assess whether federal jurisdiction would “disturb the balance of federal and state judicial responsibilities.” 545 U.S. at 314, 125 S.Ct. 2363. The Federal Circuit has consistently held that uniformity in patent law—ensured by exclusive federal jurisdiction—is a compelling federal interest. See *Air Measurement*, 504 F.3d at 1271 (“Congress intended federal courts to have exclusive jurisdiction over patent-related claims to ensure uniformity in the interpretation and enforcement of patent law.”). This interest is not diminished simply because the claim arises in state court. Indeed, allowing state courts to resolve patent validity issues in malpractice cases would create the very risk of inconsistent rulings that *Grable* seeks to prevent.

### II. THE FEDERAL ISSUE IS BOTH SUBSTANTIAL AND ACTUALLY DISPUTED

The experimental use exception is not a mere technicality. It is a well-established doctrine that turns on the *purpose* of a sale—whether commercial or experimental. See *Electromotive*, 417 F.3d at 1210. The facts here demonstrate that the issue was genuinely contested: Minton’s original counsel did not raise it, and the Federal Circuit affirmed the district court’s denial of reconsideration precisely because the defense was not timely asserted. *Minton v. Nat’l Ass’n of Sec. Dealers, Inc.*, 336 F.3d 1373, 1379–81 (Fed.Cir.2003). This shows that the issue was not hypothetical—it was actively litigated and rejected on the merits. The fact that the Federal Circuit upheld the denial of the exception does not render it “insubstantial”; rather, it confirms that the issue was both real and significant.

Moreover, the Texas Supreme Court’s reliance on *Singh v. Duane Morris LLP*, 538 F.3d 384 (5th Cir.2008), is misplaced. *Singh* did not involve a malpractice claim arising from patent litigation. It concerned a claim under the federal securities laws, and the court found that federal jurisdiction was not triggered because the federal issue was not “necessary” to the outcome. 538 F.3d at 340. The facts here are materially different: the malpractice claim cannot be resolved without determining whether the patent was valid under federal law. The *Singh* court itself acknowledged that federal jurisdiction may exist where “the federal issue is actually disputed and substantial, and federal jurisdiction will not disturb the balance of federal and state judicial responsibilities.” Id. at 338.
