# ARGUMENT

## I. MILFORD'S DENIAL WAS REASONABLE, VIEWPOINT-NEUTRAL SUBJECT-MATTER REGULATION OF A LIMITED PUBLIC FORUM

Milford created a limited public forum, opening its facilities for "social, civic and recreational meetings" concerning "the welfare of the community," while expressly excluding "religious purposes." This Court has long recognized that when a government entity opens property as a limited public forum, it "may be justified by reference to the purposes served by the forum," and the government "does not have to allow persons to engage in every type of speech." *Cornelius v. NAACP Legal Defense & Educational Fund, Inc.*, 473 U.S. 788, 806 (1985). A limited forum need not "satisfy a public forum's requirement of favorable treatment of all viewpoints"; a State may reserve the forum "for its intended purposes." *Rosenberger v. Rector and Visitors of Univ. of Va.*, 515 U.S. 819, 829 (1995).

The distinction between permissible subject-matter regulation and impermissible viewpoint discrimination is dispositive here. In *Rosenberger*, this Court struck down funding denial because the university excluded *all* religious perspectives on subjects it otherwise funded—the university did not exclude the topic of religion itself, but discriminated against a viewpoint on permitted topics. By contrast, Milford excluded the *category* of religious worship and instruction entirely, regardless of viewpoint. This is subject-matter exclusion, not viewpoint discrimination, and it survives First Amendment scrutiny so long as it is "reasonable in light of the purpose served by the forum" and viewpoint neutral. *Cornelius*, 473 U.S. at 806, 809.

The Second Circuit correctly found that the Good News Club's activities—proselytizing prayer, scripted salvation invitations, and religious worship—cross this line. The Club's own submitted lesson plan, "Israel Demands a King," instructs children that they "can be saved today" and provides a scripted prayer: "Dear Lord Jesus, I do believe You died for me... Please take away my sin and help me obey You." This is not a moral lesson informed by religious perspective; it is religious conversion. As the court below found, the Club's activities "fall outside the bounds of 'pure' moral and character development" and instead constitute "religious instruction and prayer" indistinguishable from "material to be presented at Sunday school." 202 F.3d at 510.

Milford permitted the Boy Scouts, Girl Scouts, and 4-H Club precisely because those organizations, whatever their values-based content, do not conduct religious worship or invite children to accept a particular faith's savior. This is content-based line-drawing tied to the forum's secular purpose—not discrimination against a viewpoint on a permitted topic.

## II. EVEN IF THE EXCLUSION IMPLICATED PROTECTED SPEECH, MILFORD'S ESTABLISHMENT CLAUSE CONCERNS JUSTIFIED THE RESTRICTION

Milford's denial was independently justified by its reasonable, good-faith concern that hosting weekly religious worship and evangelization of elementary schoolchildren, immediately after the school day, in the school building, would violate the Establishment Clause.

This Court has recognized that avoiding Establishment Clause violations constitutes a compelling governmental interest sufficient to justify content-based distinctions that would otherwise trigger strict scrutiny. In *Widmar v. Vincent*, 454 U.S. 263 (1981), the Court acknowledged that "the interest of the State in avoiding an Establishment Clause violation may be sufficiently compelling to justify content-based discrimination." *Id.* at 271. Although the Court found no Establishment Clause problem on the facts of *Widmar*—a university setting with mature students—it expressly preserved the principle that such concerns can justify exclusion where the forum, audience, and circumstances present genuine endorsement risk.

The circumstances here differ critically from *Widmar*. Milford's forum serves elementary schoolchildren, ages six to twelve—a population this Court has repeatedly recognized as psychologically vulnerable to perceived school endorsement of religion. In *School District of Abington Township v. Schempp*, 374 U.S. 203 (1963), and *Lee v. Weisman*, 505 U.S. 577 (1992), this Court emphasized that public schools occupy a unique constitutional position because of the susceptibility of young students to subtle pressures regarding religious conformity. *Lee* recognized that "there are heightened concerns with protecting freedom of conscience from subtle coercive pressure in the elementary and secondary public schools." 505 U.S. at 592.

The Club's meetings would occur on school property, immediately following the instructional day, advertised to children through school channels, and directed at classmates of the very children who attend Milford's classrooms. A reasonable observer—particularly a child observer—could conclude that Milford itself was endorsing the Club's evangelical message, sponsoring religious conversion activities as a continuation of the school day. This is precisely the type of endorsement concern that animates the Establishment Clause analysis this Court applied in *Lee*, where subtle pressures on impressionable schoolchildren rendered otherwise permissible religious activity constitutionally suspect.

Milford's Superintendent reasonably concluded, after careful review of the Club's own submitted materials—including the "Daily Bread" pamphlets referencing salvation and the Resurrection—that the Club's proposed use was "the equivalent of religious worship" rather than "the expression of religious views or values on a secular subject matter." This was not pretext; it was a considered judgment based on the specific content the Club itself provided, including scripted altar-call prayers directed at young children.

## CONCLUSION

Milford's Community Use Policy reasonably distinguished between permissible discussion of morals from a religious perspective and impermissible religious worship and instruction. That distinction reflects legitimate subject-matter limits on a limited public forum, consistent with *Cornelius* and *Rosenberger*, and is independently justified by Milford's reasonable Establishment Clause concerns regarding elementary schoolchildren, consistent with *Widmar* and *Lee*. The judgment of the Second Circuit should be affirmed.
