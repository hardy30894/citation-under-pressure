**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Due Process Clause Does Not Compel Arizona to Admit Expert Mental-Disease Evidence to Negate Mens Rea When the State Provides a Separate Insanity Defense.**

Arizona’s evidentiary rule—limiting expert testimony on mental disease to the insanity defense—does not violate the Fourteenth Amendment. This Court has long recognized that “the States possess broad authority under their police powers to regulate the procedures under which criminal laws are carried out.” *Patterson v. New York*, 432 U.S. 197, 201–02 (1977). That authority encompasses the power to define the elements of crimes and to allocate the burden of proof on mental-state issues. *See Martin v. Ohio*, 480 U.S. 228, 233–34 (1987) (upholding state’s requirement that defendant prove self-defense by preponderance even though it negated murder element).

Arizona’s scheme is a rational accommodation of competing concerns. The State may constitutionally channel evidence of mental disease into the insanity defense, which places the burden on the defendant, while preserving the prosecution’s obligation to prove mens rea beyond a reasonable doubt using ordinary evidence. *See Leland v. Oregon*, 343 U.S. 790, 798–99 (1952) (upholding state’s requirement that defendant prove insanity beyond a reasonable doubt). Nothing in the Due Process Clause requires a State to “undermine the usual presumption of sanity” by allowing a defendant to use the same evidence of mental disease to negate mens rea. *Rivera v. Delaware*, 429 U.S. 877, 877 (1976) (per curiam) (dismissing appeal for want of a substantial federal question).

**II. The State’s Rule Is a Permissible Exercise of Its Authority to Define the Relationship Between Mens Rea and Insanity.**

This Court in *Montana v. Egelhoff*, 518 U.S. 37, 56 (1996), held that a State may “exclude evidence of voluntary intoxication when offered to negate the existence of a mental state” because the State’s “sovereign” power to define the elements of crime includes the power to define the “mind” that is relevant to guilt. The same principle applies here. Arizona has chosen to define mens rea by reference to a “normal” capacity to reason, not by incorporating the effects of a recognized mental disease. *See* Ariz. Rev. Stat. § 13-502(A) (insanity defined as inability to know nature of act or wrongfulness). The State’s rule is a “substantive” decision about what mental states are “relevant to the criminal law.” *Egelhoff*, 518 U.S. at 56 (plurality opinion).

Moreover, the State’s approach avoids the “serious risk of confusion and prejudice” that can arise when expert testimony on mental disease is used to blur the line between issues of criminal responsibility and issues of guilt. *See Clark v. Arizona*, 548 U.S. 735, 771–72 (2006) (upholding Arizona’s rule). Expert testimony on mental disease “may mislead the jury” by suggesting that a defendant who cannot form the required intent is not criminally liable, even if the defendant is not legally insane. *Id.* at 775. Arizona’s rule is a “reasonable” way to “channel” such evidence to the insanity defense, where it is most relevant and where the burden of proof is properly allocated to the defendant. *Id.* at 776.

**III. The Defendant’s Right to Present a Defense Does Not Require Admission of Unreliable or Misleading Evidence.**

The right to present a defense is not absolute. *Montana v. Egelhoff*, 518 U.S. at 42. States may exclude evidence that is “repetitive, only marginally relevant, or poses an undue risk of prejudice.” *Crane v. Kentucky*, 476 U.S. 683, 689–90 (1986). Expert testimony on mental disease, when offered to negate mens rea, carries a “danger of misleading the jury into thinking that the defendant’s mental condition, not his conduct, is the real issue.” *Clark*, 548 U.S. at 775. Arizona’s rule is a “traditional” and “longstanding” restriction that serves the “legitimate interest” of focusing the trial on the defendant’s conduct and intent at the time of the crime. *See Patterson*, 432 U.S. at 206.

Arizona does not bar the defendant from presenting any evidence of his mental state; it merely limits the form of that evidence. The defendant may still testify about his subjective perceptions, and he may call fact witnesses to describe his behavior. The rule excludes only the *expert’s opinion* that the defendant’s mental disease prevented him from forming the requisite intent. That is a “time-honored” limitation that does not offend due process. *See Rivera*, 429 U.S. at 877.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Arizona Supreme Court should be affirmed.
