**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE INDICTMENT FAILED TO STATE AN OFFENSE BECAUSE MRS. PARKER LACKED AUTHORITY TO PERFORM “OFFICIAL ACTS” UNDER 18 U.S.C. § 201(b)(2)(C).**

Mrs. Parker’s conviction rests on the erroneous premise that her clerical assistance—typing, mailing, and forging ALJ Aronson’s signature—constituted “official acts” under § 201(b)(2)(C). This misreads the statute and controlling precedent.

Section 201(b)(2)(C) criminalizes bribery of a public official for acts “in violation of the official duty” of that official. 18 U.S.C. § 201(b)(2)(C). An “official act” is defined broadly in § 201(a)(3) as “any decision or action on any question, matter, cause, suit, proceeding or controversy” pending before or within the official’s purview. However, this definition does not extend to acts outside an official’s authority. *United States v. Birdsall*, 233 U.S. 223, 231 (1914) (limiting § 201 to acts within the official’s “duty or authority”). The Supreme Court has cautioned that § 201 “does not reach every act of a public official, but only those acts performed in an official capacity.” *United States v. Cincotta*, 689 F.2d 238, 241 (1st Cir. 1982) (citing *Birdsall*, 233 U.S. at 231).

Mrs. Parker’s duties were ministerial—assisting ALJ Aronson by typing and mailing decisions, not adjudicating claims or approving benefits. She lacked authority to grant or deny SSI benefits, and her forgery of ALJ Aronson’s signature was a private act, not an “official act.” *See United States v. Gjieli*, 717 F.2d 968, 972 (6th Cir. 1983) (holding that an official’s unauthorized acts do not qualify as “official acts” under § 201). The government’s theory—that any act taken in the office’s name constitutes an “official act”—would criminalize routine clerical misconduct, a result Congress did not intend. *See S. Rep. No. 87-2213*, reprinted in 1962 U.S.C.C.A.N. 3852, 3856 (explaining that § 201 targets bribery of official decision-making, not clerical errors).

**II. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN ADMITTING MRS. PARKER’S CONFESSION BECAUSE IT WAS OBTAINED IN VIOLATION OF FED. R. CRIM. P. 5 AND THE FIFTH AMENDMENT.**

Mrs. Parker’s confession was procured during an un-Mirandized interrogation after she had invoked her right to counsel. The district court erred in admitting this evidence.

The Fifth Amendment requires that once a suspect invokes the right to counsel, all interrogation must cease unless initiated by the suspect. *Miranda v. Arizona*, 384 U.S. 436, 474 (1966). Mrs. Parker’s confession followed an FBI interview where she was not advised of her rights until *after* she implicated herself. This violated *Edwards v. Arizona*, 451 U.S. 477, 484–85 (1981), which bars police-initiated interrogation after a suspect invokes the right to counsel. The government’s reliance on *Oregon v. Bradshaw*, 462 U.S. 1039 (1983), is misplaced, as Mrs. Parker did not initiate further discussion.

Moreover, the confession was obtained in violation of Fed. R. Crim. P. 5, which requires presentment before a magistrate “without unnecessary delay.” Mrs. Parker was interrogated for hours before being taken before a magistrate, a delay the Fifth Circuit has deemed unreasonable. *United States v. Bengivenga*, 845 F.2d 593, 597 (5th Cir. 1988) (en banc).

**III. THE GOVERNMENT FAILED TO PROVE CONSPIRACY BECAUSE THERE WAS NO EVIDENCE OF AN AGREEMENT TO COMMIT AN OFFENSE.**

To sustain a conspiracy conviction under § 371, the government must prove an agreement to commit an offense. *United States v. Shabani*, 513 U.S. 10, 14 (1994). Here, the evidence showed only that Mrs. Parker acted unilaterally to extort money in exchange for false benefits. There was no proof that Mr. Parker agreed to participate in a bribery scheme. *See United States v. Jiminez*, 864 F.2d 152, 154 (5th Cir. 1989) (requiring evidence of a “meeting of the minds”).

The government’s theory—that Mr. Parker’s presence during the $500 exchange evidenced tacit agreement—is insufficient. *See United States v. Duhon*, 104 F.3d 75, 78 (5th Cir. 1997) (holding that mere association or knowledge of criminal activity does not establish conspiracy).

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the convictions of Joann A. Parker and Ralph Parker should be reversed. The indictment failed to state an offense, Mrs. Parker’s confession was obtained in violation of the Fifth Amendment and Rule 5, and the government failed to prove conspiracy.
