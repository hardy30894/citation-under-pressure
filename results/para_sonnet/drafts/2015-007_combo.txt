# ARGUMENT

## I. FLORIDA'S CAPITAL SENTENCING SCHEME DOES NOT VIOLATE THE SIXTH AMENDMENT BECAUSE THE JURY, NOT THE JUDGE, RETAINS THE ESSENTIAL FACT-FINDING FUNCTION HISTORICALLY COMMITTED TO IT

The Sixth Amendment guarantees a defendant the right to have a jury determine his guilt of the offense charged. *Callan v. Wilson*, 127 U.S. 540, 549 (1888) (the right to trial by jury is "a fundamental right, essential to political liberty"). Florida's scheme fully respects this guarantee: the jury alone determines whether the defendant committed first-degree murder, the substantive offense for which the maximum sentence—death—has always been authorized under Florida law once that verdict is returned. Florida's capital sentencing procedure asks the jury to render an advisory recommendation on the appropriate sentence, a role fully consistent with the historic understanding of the jury's function as recognized in *Patton v. United States*, 281 U.S. 276, 288 (1930), where this Court explained that the "essential elements" of the jury trial right are the number of jurors, the presence of a judge, and unanimity in verdict—elements Florida preserves as to the guilt determination.

This Court has never held that findings bearing solely on the *selection* of punishment, once guilt of a capital offense has been established, must be made by a jury applying the reasonable-doubt standard. In *Williams v. New York*, 337 U.S. 241, 246 (1949), this Court upheld a New York procedure permitting a judge to impose a death sentence based on information beyond that presented at trial, explaining that "sentencing judges are not confined to the narrow issue of guilt" and that "[a] sentencing judge... is not restricted to information received in open court." The Court there emphasized that "[m]odern concepts individualizing punishment have made it all the more necessary that a sentencing judge not be denied an opportunity to obtain pertinent information." *Id.* at 247. Williams demonstrates that the historic Sixth Amendment guarantee has never mandated jury control over sentencing factors, even in the capital context.

This principle finds further support in *Spencer v. Texas*, 385 U.S. 554, 568 (1967), where the Court reaffirmed that the Constitution does not impose federal constraints on state procedures for developing sentencing-relevant facts absent fundamental unfairness. Florida's advisory jury recommendation, followed by judicial imposition of sentence based on statutorily enumerated aggravating and mitigating circumstances, does not offend this principle. The jury's central role in determining guilt—the true predicate for eligibility for the death penalty under Florida's first-degree murder statute—remains undisturbed.

Moreover, this Court's decision in *Andres v. United States*, 333 U.S. 740, 748 (1948), while requiring unanimity for a jury verdict "in Federal courts under the Sixth Amendment," addressed only the requirement of unanimity for the jury's ultimate verdict on guilt, not sentencing recommendations. Florida's separate advisory sentencing vote is not the "verdict" contemplated by *Andres*; it is a recommendation to the court, functionally analogous to the presentence findings this Court sanctioned in *Williams*.

## II. THE EIGHTH AMENDMENT DOES NOT REQUIRE JURY UNANIMITY OR JURY DETERMINATION OF MENTAL RETARDATION IN CAPITAL SENTENCING

The Eighth Amendment's prohibition on cruel and unusual punishment, as incorporated through the Fourteenth Amendment, see *Robinson v. California*, 370 U.S. 660, 666 (1962), does not mandate the particular procedural mechanisms Hurst urges. This Court has consistently held that the Eighth Amendment is concerned with the nature of the punishment imposed, not with the precise decisional architecture a State employs to reach that punishment. In *Trop v. Dulles*, 356 U.S. 86, 99 (1958), the Court explained that the touchstone of Eighth Amendment analysis is whether a punishment comports with "the evolving standards of decency that mark the progress of a maturing society"—a substantive inquiry, not a procedural one dictating jury composition or voting rules.

Florida's procedure, which permits the trial judge to make the ultimate factual determination regarding mental retardation, is consistent with the framework this Court sanctioned in *Williams*, 337 U.S. at 251, where the Court recognized the propriety of judicial fact-finding in the sentencing context, stating that "[t]he due-process clause should not be treated as a device for freezing the evidential procedure of sentencing in the mold of trial procedure." The determination whether a defendant suffers from mental retardation is precisely the sort of individualized, clinical judgment historically entrusted to the sentencing judge, who is best positioned to weigh expert testimony and other evidence in a holistic sentencing determination.

Nor does the absence of a unanimity requirement for the jury's advisory sentencing recommendation render the punishment cruel and unusual. In *In re Kemmler*, 136 U.S. 436, 447 (1890), this Court articulated a narrow Eighth Amendment inquiry limited to "punishments which inflict torture," a formulation reaffirmed in *Weems v. United States*, 217 U.S. 349, 368 (1910), where the Court examined whether a punishment was "cruel in its excess" relative to the offense. Neither *Kemmler* nor *Weems* suggests that the voting mechanism by which a jury reaches an advisory recommendation implicates the substantive protections of the Eighth Amendment. The ultimate sentence here—death for a robbery-murder involving sixty incised wounds inflicted upon a bound and gagged victim—reflects a punishment proportionate to the offense under any standard this Court has articulated, including that of *Weems*.

Finally, this Court's admonition in *Louisiana ex rel. Francis v. Resweber*, 329 U.S. 459, 463 (1947), that the Eighth Amendment does not concern itself with "the ordinary pains that accompany imprisonment or death" but with "something inhuman and barbarous," counsels against extending the Amendment to encompass novel procedural entitlements—such as mandatory jury unanimity on sentencing recommendations—that find no grounding in the constitutional text or this Court's historic Eighth Amendment jurisprudence.

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Supreme Court of Florida should be affirmed.
