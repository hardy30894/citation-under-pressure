ARGUMENT

I. The Government Failed to Prove the Requisite Mens Rea for the Machine Gun Conviction

The government failed to meet its burden of proving beyond a reasonable doubt that Belen Nieves-Castaño knew the AK-47 rifle she possessed was a machine gun, as required by Staples v. United States, 511 U.S. 600 (1994). In Staples, the Supreme Court held that to convict a defendant under 26 U.S.C. § 5861(d) for possessing an unregistered machine gun, the government must prove that the defendant knew the firearm was a machine gun. Id. at 602. The Court reasoned that the "potentially harsh penalty" attached to the violation and the "dangerous nature of the weapon" required a mens rea element to avoid "criminalizing a broad range of entirely innocent conduct." Id. at 610-11.

The same reasoning applies to 18 U.S.C. § 922(o), which prohibits the possession of a machine gun. See United States v. Rogers, 119 F.3d 1377, 1384 (9th Cir. 1997) (applying Staples to § 922(o) and holding that the government must prove the defendant knew the firearm was a machine gun). The government presented no evidence that Nieves-Castaño knew the AK-47 was a machine gun. In fact, the evidence suggests that she did not know the rifle's capabilities, as she simply stated that she knew it was an AK-47. (R. at 123). This is insufficient to establish the requisite mens rea.

The government's failure to prove that Nieves-Castaño knew the AK-47 was a machine gun renders her conviction under 18 U.S.C. § 922(o) invalid. See United States v. Edwards, 90 F.3d 199, 204-05 (7th Cir. 1996) (reversing a conviction under § 922(o) for insufficient evidence where the government failed to prove the defendant knew the firearm was a machine gun). Therefore, this court should reverse Nieves-Castaño's conviction for unlawful possession of a machine gun.

II. The School-Zone Statute is Void for Vagueness

Nieves-Castaño's conviction under 18 U.S.C. § 922(q)(2)(A) for possessing a firearm in a school zone should also be reversed because the statute is unconstitutionally vague. The statute prohibits possessing a firearm within 1000 feet of a school, but it fails to specify how to measure this distance. See 18 U.S.C. § 922(q)(2)(A). This lack of clarity renders the statute void for vagueness under the Fifth Amendment's Due Process Clause.

A statute is void for vagueness if it "fails to give a person of ordinary intelligence fair notice that his contemplated conduct is forbidden by the statute." United States v. Harriss, 347 U.S. 612, 617 (1954). The Supreme Court has held that a statute must provide "sufficiently definite" standards to avoid arbitrary and discriminatory enforcement. Kolender v. Lawson, 461 U.S. 352, 358 (1983). The school-zone statute's failure to specify how to measure the 1000-foot distance creates uncertainty and invites arbitrary enforcement.

Other circuits have not directly addressed this issue, but the lack of clarity in the statute raises concerns about its constitutionality. See, e.g., United States v. Lopez, 514 U.S. 549, 561-62 (1995) (noting that the school-zone statute "contains no jurisdictional element" and expressing concerns about its scope). This court should follow the reasoning of the Supreme Court in Harriss and Kolender and hold that § 922(q)(2)(A) is void for vagueness.

III. Sufficiency of the Evidence

In addition to the above arguments, Nieves-Castaño also challenges the sufficiency of the evidence on both counts. The government's evidence was insufficient to establish the necessary elements of the crimes charged. On the machine-gun count, as discussed above, the government failed to prove that Nieves-Castaño knew the AK-47 was a machine gun. On the school-zone count, the government failed to provide clear evidence that Nieves-Castaño possessed the firearm within 1000 feet of a school, given the ambiguity in measuring the distance.

For the foregoing reasons, this court should reverse Nieves-Castaño's convictions under 18 U.S.C. § 922(o) and § 922(q)(2)(A).
