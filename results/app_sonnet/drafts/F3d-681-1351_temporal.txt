# ARGUMENT

## I. The Board Erred by Extending Harari I's Narrow "Reasonable Examiner" Standard to the Separate Question of Statutory Continuity Under § 120.

This Court's decision in Harari I addressed only whether an examiner reviewing the '880 application's *initial filing papers*—including the transmittal sheet and preliminary amendment submitted contemporaneously with that application—could determine the identity of the incorporated reference. Harari I, 602 F.3d at 1352-53. Nothing in that holding purported to relax the settled rule that an intervening application in a chain of continuations must, standing on its own four corners as of its own filing date, clearly identify the document it purports to incorporate. That rule predates this dispute by decades. As the Court of Customs and Patent Appeals held in *In re Heritage*, 182 F.2d 639 (C.C.P.A. 1950), a reference to another application does not incorporate its contents unless the reference itself, at the time it is made, identifies the incorporated document with sufficient particularity that a reader is not left to guess. The Board's remand order collapsed this settled distinction, treating the correction later made in the '880 application's preliminary amendment as if it cured the defective incorporation language in the '838 and '768 applications years earlier. It does not. Each application in a priority chain must independently satisfy the requirements of 35 U.S.C. § 120, and a later-filed application's clarifying amendment cannot retroactively repair a defective disclosure in an earlier-filed, intervening application.

## II. The '838 and '768 Applications Do Not Satisfy the Longstanding Requirement That Incorporation by Reference Be Clear and Unambiguous on Its Face.

The incorporation language at issue states that the referenced "Multi-State EEprom Read and Write Circuits and Techniques" application was "filed on the same day as the present application." J.A. 1204. That statement was true when it appeared in the original '566 application, filed the same day as the '579 application. But when the identical language was copied, unaltered, into the '838 and '768 applications—filed years after the '579 application—the statement became facially false. No transmittal sheet, no preliminary amendment, and no contemporaneous filing accompanied the '838 or '768 applications correcting this error, unlike the '880 application in Harari I. See 602 F.3d at 1353-54.

This defect is fatal under long-established doctrine. The Court of Customs and Patent Appeals has repeatedly held that an incorporation by reference must permit a skilled reader, from the document itself, to identify the incorporated material without resort to hindsight or extrinsic correction. *In re Fried*, 312 F.2d 930 (C.C.P.A. 1963). A reference containing an internal falsehood as to the very fact—contemporaneous filing—that supposedly identifies the incorporated document cannot satisfy that standard. A reasonable examiner reviewing the '838 or '768 applications alone, without the benefit of hindsight knowledge of the true prosecution history, would have no basis to conclude that the "same day" application referenced was, in fact, the '579 application filed nearly four years earlier.

This is not a mere technical quibble; it implicates the core purpose of the written description requirement. As this Court's predecessor explained in *In re Ruschig*, 379 F.2d 990 (C.C.P.A. 1967), an application's disclosure must convey the invention to one skilled in the art without requiring speculation beyond what the document itself discloses. The '838 and '768 applications, containing an incorporation statement that is demonstrably inaccurate on its face, cannot be said to convey the '579 application's disclosure "as filed." Permitting such applications to serve as the intervening links in a priority chain reaching back to 1989 would sanction exactly the kind of after-the-fact importation of subject matter that the written description requirement, and the prohibition on new matter under 35 U.S.C. § 132, are designed to prevent. *See* Schriber-Schroth Co. v. Cleveland Trust Co., 305 U.S. 47 (1938) (a patentee may not, after the fact, expand or supply disclosure beyond what was contained in the application as filed).

## III. Because the Chain of Applications Is Broken, the '880 Application Is Entitled Only to the Filing Date of the '708 Application.

Section 120's benefit of an earlier filing date is not automatic; it is available only where the chain of applications is continuous and each application "discloses the invention" in the manner required by § 112. This principle traces to the earliest continuation-practice jurisprudence of the Supreme Court, which recognized that an applicant may claim the benefit of an earlier filing only where the substance of the invention was genuinely disclosed at each link in the chain, not merely asserted after the fact. Godfrey v. Eames, 68 U.S. (1 Wall.) 317 (1863). Because the '838 and '768 applications fail to adequately incorporate the '579 application, the chain of copending disclosure required to reach back to the '566 application's April 13, 1989 filing date is broken at the '838 application. The earliest date to which the '880 application is entitled is therefore December 20, 1996—the filing date of the '708 application, which undisputedly and correctly discloses the '579 application without the fatal "same day" defect.

Because Harari's '880 application is entitled only to the '708 application's filing date, it does not antedate Hollmer's conception, and the Board's judgment against Hollmer cannot stand.
