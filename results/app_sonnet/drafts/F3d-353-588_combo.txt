**ARGUMENT**

**I. The District Court Erred in Granting Summary Judgment on Epps's ADA and MHRA Claims Because Genuine Factual Disputes Exist as to Whether Pine Lawn Regarded Him as Disabled**

Summary judgment is a blunt instrument, and courts have long cautioned against its use where a defendant's motive or state of mind is the central issue. In *Poller v. Columbia Broadcasting System, Inc.*, 368 U.S. 464 (1962), the Supreme Court reversed a grant of summary judgment, explaining that "summary procedures should be used sparingly" in cases "where motive and intent play leading roles." *Id.* at 473. That principle applies with full force here. Chief Hardy's own memoranda establish that he reviewed Dr. Mirkin's note stating Epps would "likely need to seek a different occupation," coupled with Epps's history of workers' compensation filings and sick leave, before twice recommending termination. A reasonable jury could infer from this record that Hardy regarded Epps as substantially limited in his ability to work generally, not merely as unable to perform one narrow task. Whether Hardy's characterization of Epps's condition reflected a genuine, individualized assessment or a generalized, erroneous perception of incapacity is precisely the sort of factual dispute that must be resolved by a jury, not by the court on a paper record. See *Sartor v. Arkansas Natural Gas Corp.*, 321 U.S. 620, 628 (1944) (holding that trial by affidavit is "no substitute for trial by jury" where credibility and inference are at stake).

Moreover, the timing and content of Hardy's memoranda supply the kind of circumstantial proof from which discriminatory motive may properly be inferred. In the analogous context of discharge disputes, the Supreme Court has long recognized that a decisionmaker's stated justification need not be taken at face value where the surrounding circumstances suggest the true motivation lies elsewhere. See *NLRB v. Link-Belt Co.*, 311 U.S. 584, 602 (1941) (motive for discharge may be inferred from the totality of the circumstances rather than accepted at face value from the employer's own explanation). Likewise, in *Phelps Dodge Corp. v. NLRB*, 313 U.S. 177 (1941), the Court held that an employer's asserted business justification does not foreclose a finding of unlawful motive where the record permits an inference that the employee's protected characteristic or conduct was a substantial factor in the decision. *Id.* at 187. Here, Hardy's repeated invocation of Epps's medical condition and his compensation history, in memoranda recommending termination, is sufficient circumstantial evidence to send the "regarded as" question to a jury.

**II. The District Court Erred in Holding That Missouri's Sovereign Immunity Statute Bars Epps's Workers' Compensation Retaliation and Intentional Infliction of Emotional Distress Claims**

Sovereign immunity is a doctrine that exists in derogation of an injured party's right to redress, and statutes providing exceptions to that immunity — or defining its scope — should not be read so broadly as to swallow the individual's remedy. Justice Holmes's foundational articulation of the doctrine in *Kawananakoa v. Polyblank*, 205 U.S. 349, 353 (1907), rested the immunity on the proposition that "there can be no legal right against the authority that makes the law on which the right depends" — a rationale grounded in the sovereign's own lawmaking authority, not a general license to insulate municipalities from liability for retaliatory or outrageous conduct that the legislature itself has separately condemned through the workers' compensation retaliation statute. Where the legislature has already spoken by creating a statutory right against retaliatory discharge, immunity provisions should not be construed to nullify that very right by implication.

This approach mirrors the treatment federal courts have given comparable immunity questions under the Federal Tort Claims Act. In *Indian Towing Co. v. United States*, 350 U.S. 61 (1955), the Court rejected a "niggardly" construction of a waiver of immunity and instead read the statute to accord with its remedial purpose. *Id.* at 68–69. Similarly, in *Rayonier, Inc. v. United States*, 352 U.S. 315 (1957), the Court construed the scope of governmental liability generously to align with the statute's evident purpose of permitting recovery for negligent conduct that would be actionable against a private party. *Id.* at 319. Pine Lawn's immunity defense should likewise be read consistently with the Missouri Legislature's evident purpose in enacting Section 287.780 — to protect employees from retaliation for exercising a statutory right — rather than construed to eliminate the very remedy the statute created.

**III. Genuine Issues of Material Fact Preclude Summary Judgment on Epps's Intentional Infliction of Emotional Distress Claim**

Finally, even if sovereign immunity were properly considered, Epps's evidence of Hardy's conduct — targeting an officer for termination based explicitly on his exercise of statutory workers' compensation rights and his medical condition — raises a jury question as to outrageousness. The seminal formulation of the tort requires conduct that is "extreme and outrageous," exceeding all bounds of decency. *State Rubbish Collectors Ass'n v. Siliznoff*, 38 Cal. 2d 330, 240 P.2d 282, 285 (1952). Whether Hardy's repeated, calculated targeting of an injured officer's medical history meets that standard is a question for the trier of fact, not one resolvable on summary judgment. See *Sartor*, 321 U.S. at 628.

For these reasons, the judgment below should be reversed and the case remanded for trial.
