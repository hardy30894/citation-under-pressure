**ARGUMENT**

I. **An inmate has no protected life or liberty interest in clemency proceedings themselves.**

The Due Process Clause protects only interests that are independently cognizable under the Constitution or otherwise secured by law. Here, Ohio’s clemency process creates no such protected entitlement. In *Connecticut Board of Pardons v. Dumschat*, 452 U.S. 458 (1981), the Court held that a prisoner had no liberty interest in commutation because “[p]ardon and commutation decisions” have “not traditionally been the business of courts.” *Id.* at 464. The Court further explained that clemency is a matter of grace, not of right, and that the “unguarded discretion” of the clemency authority does not create a protected expectation. *Id.* at 465.

That reasoning controls here. Ohio law gives the Governor discretion to grant or deny clemency, and the Adult Parole Authority’s role is advisory only. See *Ohio Adult Parole Auth. v. Woodard*, 523 U.S. 272, 280-83 (1998) (plurality opinion); *id.* at 289-90 (O’Connor, J., concurring in part and concurring in the judgment). Because the decision remains wholly discretionary, an inmate cannot claim a protected life or liberty interest in the possibility of mercy. *Dumschat*, 452 U.S. at 464-67; *Greenholtz v. Inmates of Nebraska Penal and Correctional Complex*, 442 U.S. 1, 7-11 (1979) (state procedure creates liberty interest only when it contains mandatory substantive predicates and limits official discretion).

Nor does the existence of a state-created procedure itself transform clemency into a constitutionally protected adjudication. In *Town of Castle Rock v. Gonzales*, 545 U.S. 748, 756-61 (2005), the Court reiterated that procedural mechanisms do not create a substantive entitlement absent a real limitation on discretion. Likewise, *Olim v. Wakinekona*, 461 U.S. 238, 249-50 (1983), teaches that an expectation of a particular procedure is not a protected liberty interest. Ohio’s rules therefore do not give Woodard a federally protected interest in clemency review itself.

II. **The voluntary interview does not violate the Fifth Amendment.**

The Fifth Amendment prohibits compelled self-incrimination. It does not forbid the government from offering a prisoner the option to speak, nor does it make every adverse consequence of silence unconstitutional. *Minnesota v. Murphy*, 465 U.S. 420, 426-34 (1984), holds that the privilege is not self-executing in ordinary settings and is violated only by compulsion. Likewise, *Baxter v. Palmigiano*, 425 U.S. 308, 316-20 (1976), sustained adverse inferences from silence where the State did not force testimony. And in *McKune v. Lile*, 536 U.S. 24, 37-48 (2002), a plurality and concurrence recognized that the Constitution is not offended where a prisoner is given a choice that may carry collateral consequences but does not amount to compulsion.

Ohio’s clemency interview is voluntary. As the regulation provides, if the prisoner requests a personal interview, he may appear and speak; if he does not, he may decline. *Ohio Adult Parole Auth.*, 523 U.S. at 276-77 (describing the procedure). Nothing in the process requires Woodard to answer questions, waive the privilege, or forfeit clemency eligibility by remaining silent. The Constitution forbids coercion, not a voluntary opportunity to be heard. See *Murphy*, 465 U.S. at 434; *Baxter*, 425 U.S. at 318-20.

Woodard’s theory also depends on speculation about future use of statements in some later proceeding. The Fifth Amendment claim is not ripe absent a concrete compulsion or actual threat of incrimination. *Texas v. United States*, 523 U.S. 296, 300-01 (1998); *Murphy*, 465 U.S. at 430. Because Ohio’s procedure does not compel testimony and does not on its face require waiver, there is no constitutional violation. See *Ohio Adult Parole Auth.*, 523 U.S. at 286-89 (plurality opinion); *id.* at 289-90 (O’Connor, J., concurring in part and concurring in the judgment).

III. **At most, due process is minimal in clemency, and Ohio satisfies it.**

Even where the Court has recognized a narrow role for judicial review in clemency, it has emphasized only the most basic protection against arbitrary action. See *Ohio Adult Parole Auth.*, 523 U.S. at 289 (O’Connor, J., concurring in part and concurring in the judgment) (a prisoner would be entitled only to relief against “a scheme whereby a state official flipped a coin to determine whether to grant clemency”); *Dumschat*, 452 U.S. at 464-67. Ohio’s procedure plainly exceeds that minimal baseline. Woodard receives notice, may submit materials, and may request an interview. *Ohio Adult Parole Auth.*, 523 U.S. at 276-77. Nothing in the Constitution requires more. *Id.* at 285-89; *Dumschat*, 452 U.S. at 464-67.

For these reasons, the judgment below should be reversed insofar as it recognized a protected clemency interest or a Fifth Amendment violation.
