ARGUMENT

The Third Circuit erred in affirming Elonis' conviction under 18 U.S.C. § 875(c) without requiring proof of his subjective intent to threaten. The "true threats" exception to First Amendment protection, which § 875(c) embodies, has long been understood to require a showing of the speaker's intent to convey a threat.

The Supreme Court has not directly addressed the issue, but relevant precedents suggest that a subjective intent standard is necessary. In Cantwell v. Connecticut, 310 U.S. 296, 309-310 (1940), the Court emphasized the importance of considering the speaker's intent when evaluating the constitutionality of a statute regulating speech. Similarly, in Chaplinsky v. New Hampshire, 315 U.S. 568, 573-574 (1942), the Court upheld a statute prohibiting "fighting words" because such words, by their very utterance, inflict injury or tend to incite an immediate breach of the peace, implying that the speaker's intent is crucial.

Lower courts have also recognized the importance of intent in determining whether a statement constitutes a threat. In United States v. Prochaska, 222 F.2d 1, 2 (7th Cir. 1955), the Seventh Circuit held that a threat under a federal statute requires an "intent to cause the apprehension" of harm. The Second Circuit, in United States v. Carrier, 168 F.2d 881, 882 (2d Cir. 1948), similarly emphasized that a threat must be made with the "intent to cause fear or apprehension."

Furthermore, in cases involving statutes regulating speech, courts have consistently required a showing of intent to convey a prohibited message. For example, in Pierce v. United States, 314 U.S. 306, 311 (1941), the Court held that a statute prohibiting the mailing of material with intent to cause disloyalty or refusal of duty required proof of the defendant's intent. In Gorin v. United States, 312 U.S. 19, 27-28 (1941), the Court similarly emphasized the importance of proving the defendant's intent to injure the United States when prosecuting under the Espionage Act.

The Third Circuit's reliance on United States v. Kosma, 951 F.2d 549 (3d Cir. 1991), which held that a statement is a true threat when a reasonable speaker would foresee the statement would be interpreted as a threat, is misplaced. Kosma's objective standard is inconsistent with the long line of cases emphasizing the importance of the speaker's intent.

In Terminiello v. Chicago, 337 U.S. 1, 4-5 (1949), the Court held that a conviction for disorderly conduct based on speech requires a showing that the speech was intended to incite violence. 

In light of these precedents, it is clear that a conviction under § 875(c) requires proof of the defendant's subjective intent to threaten. The Third Circuit's decision to the contrary should be reversed.

Cases such as Dennis v. United States, 341 U.S. 494, 516-517 (1951) (concurring opinion), and Schenck v. United States, 249 U.S. 47, 52 (1919), also support the proposition that the intent behind speech is a critical factor in determining its constitutionality. 

The judgment of the Third Circuit should be reversed, and the case remanded for further proceedings consistent with a subjective intent standard.
