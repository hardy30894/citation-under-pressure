**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Controlled Substances Act’s Delegation to the Attorney General Is Constitutional Because It Provides an “Intelligible Principle” Sufficient to Guide Executive Action.**

The Constitution vests “[a]ll legislative Powers” in Congress, U.S. Const. art. I, § 1, and thus prohibits Congress from delegating its legislative authority to another branch. *Field v. Clark*, 143 U.S. 649, 692 (1892). However, the Court has long recognized that Congress may delegate authority to the executive when it supplies an “intelligible principle” to guide the delegate’s discretion. *J.W. Hampton, Jr., & Co. v. United States*, 276 U.S. 394, 409 (1928). The Controlled Substances Act (CSA) satisfies this standard.

The CSA directs the Attorney General to temporarily schedule a substance if he finds that it poses “an imminent hazard to the public safety” and meets three enumerated criteria: (1) the history and current pattern of abuse, (2) the scope, duration, and significance of abuse, and (3) the risk to public health. 21 U.S.C. § 811(h)(1), (3) (1988). These criteria are sufficiently definite to channel the Attorney General’s discretion. *See United States v. Grimaud*, 220 U.S. 506, 517 (1911) (upholding delegation where Congress provided “definite standards” for executive action). The Court has repeatedly upheld delegations where Congress specified factors for the executive to consider, even where those factors required judgment. *See, e.g., FTC v. American Tobacco Co.*, 264 U.S. 298, 305–06 (1924) (delegation to FTC upheld where statute required it to prevent “unfair methods of competition”); *Hampton*, 276 U.S. at 409 (delegation to President to adjust tariffs to equalize production costs upheld where statute provided standards).

The CSA’s delegation is no broader than those upheld in *Hampton* and *American Tobacco*. The temporary scheduling provision does not grant the Attorney General unfettered discretion; it requires him to act only when specific conditions are met. *See Lichter v. United States*, 334 U.S. 742, 785 (1948) (upholding delegation where Congress required executive to act only upon finding a specified condition). The Court has emphasized that delegations are permissible where Congress “lay[s] down an intelligible principle” to guide the executive. *Mistretta v. United States*, 488 U.S. 361, 372 (1989) (quoting *J.W. Hampton*, 276 U.S. at 409). The CSA does precisely that.

**II. The Attorney General’s Subdelegation of Temporary Scheduling Authority to the DEA Is Authorized by the CSA and Consistent with Established Principles of Administrative Law.**

The CSA does not expressly prohibit the Attorney General from subdelegating his authority, and the Court has long recognized that executive officers may subdelegate authority where Congress has not expressly prohibited it. *See United States v. Chemical Foundation, Inc.*, 272 U.S. 1, 11–12 (1926) (upholding subdelegation of patent management authority to a private corporation where statute was silent on subdelegation). The Court has also upheld subdelegations where the delegatee is an executive officer acting under the supervision of the principal delegate. *See United States ex rel. Attorney General v. Delaware & Hudson Co.*, 213 U.S. 366, 416 (1909) (upholding subdelegation of rate-setting authority to a subordinate official).

Here, the Attorney General subdelegated temporary scheduling authority to the DEA Administrator, an executive officer acting under his supervision. The CSA’s structure—vesting scheduling authority in the Attorney General but permitting him to delegate administrative functions—mirrors the delegation scheme upheld in *Chemical Foundation* and *Delaware & Hudson*. The subdelegation is therefore valid.

**III. The Temporary Scheduling Provision Does Not Violate the Constitution’s Separation of Powers.**

The non-delegation doctrine is not a bar to the CSA’s temporary scheduling provision. The Court has repeatedly cautioned against reviving the doctrine in a manner that would unduly restrict Congress’s ability to address complex regulatory problems. *See Yakus v. United States*, 321 U.S. 414, 424–25 (1944) (upholding delegation of price-control authority where Congress provided standards and limited duration). The CSA’s temporary scheduling provision is temporary by design, limiting the Attorney General’s authority to one year (with a possible six-month extension). *See 21 U.S.C. § 811(h)(2)*. This temporal limitation ensures that the delegation does not permanently transfer legislative power. *See Opp Cotton Mills v. Administrator*, 312 U.S. 126, 144–45 (1941) (upholding delegation where Congress provided for periodic reconsideration and expiration).

Moreover, the Court has distinguished between delegations that create crimes and those that do not, but it has not held that delegations creating crimes are per se unconstitutional. *See United States v. Reese*, 92 U.S. 214, 221 (1875) (upholding statute criminalizing interference with voting rights even though it did not specify all elements of the offense). The CSA’s temporary scheduling provision does not criminalize conduct outright; it merely enables the executive to respond to emerging threats. *See United States v. Wurzbach*, 280 U.S. 396, 403 (1930) (upholding delegation where Congress provided standards for executive action).

**Conclusion**

The CSA’s delegation to the Attorney General is constitutional because it provides an intelligible principle to guide executive action. The Attorney General’s subdelegation to the DEA is authorized by the CSA and consistent with established principles of administrative law. The temporary scheduling provision does not violate the separation of powers. The judgment below should be affirmed.
