**ARGUMENT**

The district court’s failure to advise Manuel Peguero of his right to appeal did not entitle him to habeas relief because he undisputedly knew of that right and voluntarily chose not to appeal. A violation of Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 32(a)(2) is subject to harmless-error review, and where the defendant’s actual knowledge renders the omission harmless, no relief is warranted.

**I. A Rule 32 Violation Is Subject to Harmless-Error Review**

The Federal Rules of Criminal Procedure expressly provide that “[a]ny error, defect, irregularity, or variance that does not affect substantial rights must be disregarded.” Fed. R. Crim. P. 52(a). This Court has consistently applied harmless-error analysis to procedural errors at sentencing, including failures to comply with Rule 32. *United States v. Robinson*, 485 U.S. 25, 30 (1988) (applying harmless-error review to a district court’s failure to advise a defendant of his right to appeal under former Rule 32(a)(2)). In *Robinson*, the Court held that “the failure to give the advice required by Rule 32(a)(2) is not a jurisdictional error” and that “the defendant must show that the error caused him prejudice.” *Id.* at 30–31. The same principle governs here. Because Peguero suffered no prejudice, the error was harmless.

**II. Peguero’s Actual Knowledge of His Appeal Right Renders the Omission Harmless**

The district court found, after an evidentiary hearing, that Peguero “knew of his right to appeal when the sentencing hearing occurred.” That finding is amply supported by the record and is not challenged on appeal. When a defendant possesses actual knowledge of his right to appeal, the purpose of Rule 32(a)(2)—to ensure that defendants are informed of that right—is fully served. *Robinson*, 485 U.S. at 30–31 (holding that “the purpose of the rule is to ensure that a defendant is aware of his right to appeal” and that “if the defendant already knows of that right, the omission is harmless”). Because Peguero knew he could appeal, the district court’s failure to repeat that advice caused him no harm.

**III. Habeas Relief Requires a Showing of Prejudice**

A collateral attack under 28 U.S.C. § 2255 is not a vehicle for correcting every technical error. Relief is available only for errors that “resulted in a complete miscarriage of justice” or that “inherently result[ed] in a complete miscarriage of justice.” *Hill v. United States*, 368 U.S. 424, 428 (1962). In *Hill*, the Court denied habeas relief for a violation of Rule 32(a)’s requirement that the defendant be afforded an opportunity to allocute, because the error did not “affect the substantial rights of the defendant.” *Id.* at 428–29. Similarly, here, the omission did not affect Peguero’s substantial rights. He knew of his appeal right, made a conscious decision not to appeal, and cannot now claim that the district court’s oversight deprived him of any opportunity.

**IV. Peguero’s Contrary Arguments Lack Merit**

Peguero contends that any violation of Rule 32(a)(2) automatically entitles a defendant to habeas relief, regardless of knowledge or prejudice. That argument is foreclosed by *Robinson*, which explicitly rejected the notion that “any deviation from the rule’s command requires reversal.” 485 U.S. at 30. The rule is a procedural safeguard, not a substantive right. Where the safeguard is unnecessary because the defendant already possesses the information, the error is harmless. *See also United States v. Daniels*, 902 F.2d 1238, 1240 (5th Cir. 1990) (applying *Robinson* to hold that failure to advise is harmless when defendant knows of right). Peguero’s reliance on the rule’s mandatory language ignores this Court’s settled harmless-error jurisprudence.

**Conclusion**

Because Peguero knew of his right to appeal and chose not to exercise it, the district court’s failure to advise him of that right was harmless. The judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
