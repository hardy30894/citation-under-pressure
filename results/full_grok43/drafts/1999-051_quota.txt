**ARGUMENT**

A state prisoner may rely on a procedurally defaulted claim of ineffective assistance of appellate counsel to establish cause for the default of an underlying constitutional claim. This Court has long recognized that cause exists when “some objective factor external to the defense” impeded compliance with state procedural rules. *Murray v. Carrier*, 477 U.S. 478, 488 (1986). Ineffective assistance of counsel that violates the Sixth Amendment constitutes such an external factor. *Id.* at 488; *see also Coleman v. Thompson*, 501 U.S. 722, 753–54 (1991).

The requirement that a prisoner first exhaust state remedies for the ineffective-assistance claim itself does not foreclose its use as cause. *Rose v. Lundy*, 455 U.S. 509, 518–19 (1982), addressed only the total-exhaustion rule for habeas petitions, not the distinct question whether a defaulted claim may supply cause. When the ineffective-assistance claim is itself defaulted, the prisoner may still demonstrate cause and prejudice for that default under the same standards. *Wainwright v. Sykes*, 433 U.S. 72, 90–91 (1977); *Engle v. Isaac*, 456 U.S. 107, 129 (1982).

This Court has repeatedly treated ineffective assistance as cause without first demanding that the claim have been properly presented in state court. *See Smith v. Murray*, 477 U.S. 527, 535–36 (1986); *Kimmelman v. Morrison*, 477 U.S. 365, 383–84 (1986). Requiring a separate showing of cause for the cause claim would erect an additional layer of procedural hurdles unsupported by precedent. *Strickler v. Greene*, 527 U.S. 263, 282–83 (1999), confirms that cause analysis focuses on whether the default was attributable to external factors, not on whether those factors were themselves preserved.

The Sixth Circuit correctly held that Carpenter’s defaulted ineffective-assistance claim could excuse the default of his sufficiency claim. Because appellate counsel’s failure to raise the sufficiency issue on direct appeal constituted constitutionally deficient performance that prejudiced Carpenter, *see Strickland v. Washington*, 466 U.S. 668, 687 (1984), the default is excused. The district court’s grant of habeas relief therefore rests on settled principles governing procedural default.
