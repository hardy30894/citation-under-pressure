**ARGUMENT**

**I. Section 523(a)(2)(A) Excepts From Discharge Only the Debt "To the Extent Obtained By" Fraud, Not Additional Statutory Penalties Layered On Top of That Debt.**

The statute does not except every debt that happens to arise from fraudulent conduct; it excepts a debt only "to the extent obtained by" false pretenses, false representation, or actual fraud. That limiting phrase has independent meaning: it confines nondischargeability to the value the debtor actually received through deception, not to every dollar a court or legislature later chooses to add on top of that sum.

This Court's construction of the materially identical predecessor language in the Bankruptcy Act of 1898 confirms as much. In *Crawford v. Burke*, 195 U.S. 176 (1904), the Court examined whether a judgment "merged" the underlying fraud, and held that the creditor's claim remained excepted from discharge only because the judgment was, in substance, nothing more than "the liability upon which it is based," id. at 187 — that is, the liability for the property fraudulently obtained. Similarly, in *Friend v. Talcott*, 228 U.S. 27 (1913), the Court examined the scope of the exception for "liabilities for obtaining property by false pretenses or false representations" and confined it to the property the debtor actually procured through the deception. And in *Forsyth v. Vehmeyer*, 177 U.S. 177 (1900), the Court treated the excepted debt as coextensive with the money and property the debtor obtained by fraud — not with any enlarged recovery a creditor might later secure. The common thread in these decisions is that the exception tracks the debtor's ill‑gotten gain, not the outer limits of whatever remedy state law happens to attach to the underlying wrong.

**II. Exceptions to Discharge Must Be Construed Strictly Against the Creditor and Liberally in Favor of the Debtor's Fresh Start.**

This Court has repeatedly instructed that provisions carving debts out of discharge are exceptions to a broad and beneficent rule, and must be read narrowly. In *Gleason v. Thaw*, 236 U.S. 558 (1915), the Court held that such exceptions "should be confined to those plainly expressed," id. at 562, refusing to extend the exception for debts "for" services rendered in a fiduciary capacity beyond its plain terms. *Williams v. United States Fidelity & Guaranty Co.*, 236 U.S. 549 (1915), reinforced the same principle, construing the fraud-and-fiduciary exceptions narrowly against the party invoking them. And in *Neal v. Clark*, 95 U.S. 704 (1877), the Court held that the word "fraud" in the discharge-exception context means only "positive fraud, or fraud in fact, involving moral turpitude or intentional wrong," id. at 709, not fraud implied in law — a construction adopted precisely to prevent the exception from swallowing the debtor's general right to discharge.

That right to discharge is not a mere technicality; it is the animating purpose of the bankruptcy laws. As the Court explained in *Local Loan Co. v. Hunt*, 292 U.S. 234 (1934), the central purpose of bankruptcy is "to give to the honest but unfortunate debtor... a new opportunity in life and a clear field for future effort, unhampered by the pressure and discouragement of pre-existing debt." Id. at 244. Reading § 523(a)(2)(A) to reach not only the sums a debtor fraudulently obtained but also treble damages, penalties, and fees layered on by state consumer-protection statutes would convert a narrow, fraud-tailored exception into a vehicle for importing whatever punitive multiplier a State chooses to attach to ordinary civil liability — precisely the expansive reading *Gleason* and *Williams* forbid.

**III. The Treble Damages, Attorney's Fees, and Costs Awarded Under the New Jersey Consumer Fraud Act Were Not "Obtained By" Cohen's Conduct and Therefore Remain Dischargeable.**

Applying these settled principles, only the $31,382.50 in excess rent — the sum Cohen actually obtained from respondents through the rent overcharges — falls within the fraud exception. That is the "debt... obtained by" the alleged misrepresentation within the meaning of the statute, and within the historical understanding of that phrase confirmed in *Crawford*, *Friend*, and *Forsyth*. The additional $62,765 in treble damages, together with the attorney's fees and costs awarded under the New Jersey Consumer Fraud Act, were not obtained by Cohen at all. They are sums fixed by state statute as a remedial-and-deterrent multiplier and fee-shifting mechanism, imposed on top of the actual overcharge; Cohen never received them, and respondents never lost them to him. To except such derivative statutory damages from discharge would be to stretch § 523(a)(2)(A) beyond the "plainly expressed" terms of the statute, *Gleason*, 236 U.S. at 562, and beyond the narrow scope this Court has always given exceptions of this kind, see *Williams*, 236 U.S. 549; *Neal*, 95 U.S. 704.

Denying Cohen the fresh start as to these punitive and fee-based add-ons — sums he never obtained through any misrepresentation — would frustrate rather than serve the bankruptcy laws' central purpose "to relieve the honest debtor from the weight of oppressive indebtedness." *Local Loan Co.*, 292 U.S. at 244. The judgment below should accordingly be reversed insofar as it holds the treble-damages, fee, and cost components of the award nondischargeable.
