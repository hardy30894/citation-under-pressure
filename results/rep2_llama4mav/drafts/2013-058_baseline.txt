The Seventh Circuit correctly held that an inherited IRA does not qualify as exempt "retirement funds" under Section 522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12) of the U.S. Bankruptcy Code. The plain meaning of "retirement funds" requires that the funds be held for the debtor's retirement. As the Seventh Circuit observed, an inherited IRA is not a "retirement fund" because it is not held for the beneficiary's retirement; instead, it is a "time-limited tax-deferral vehicle" that must begin distributing its assets within a year of the original owner's death (Clark v. Rameker, 673 F.3d 705, 706 (7th Cir. 2013)).

The statutory text supports this interpretation. Section 522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12) exempt "retirement funds to the extent that those funds are in a fund or account that is exempt from taxation under section... 408... of the Internal Revenue Code of 1986." Although an inherited IRA is exempt from taxation under Section 408, it does not contain "retirement funds" because the beneficiary cannot hold the assets until their own retirement (26 U.S.C. §402(c)(11)(A), incorporating 26 U.S.C. §401(a)(9)(B)). The Seventh Circuit correctly noted that the money in an inherited IRA "does not represent anyone's retirement funds" because it is not reserved for the beneficiary's retirement (Clark, 673 F.3d at 706).

The Fifth Circuit's decision in In re Chilton, 674 F.3d 486 (5th Cir. 2012), is distinguishable. The Chilton court focused on the fact that Section 522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12) do not explicitly require that the "retirement funds" be the debtor's. However, this omission does not imply that the funds need not be held for the debtor's retirement. As the Seventh Circuit observed, the statute's language "does not throw creditors' claims to the wolves in order to enhance the savings and bequest motives" (Clark, 673 F.3d at 711, quoting Rodriguez v. United States, 480 U.S. 522, 525-26 (1987)).

Furthermore, exempting inherited IRAs from creditor claims would create an anomaly. If Ruth Heffron had withdrawn the entire $300,000 from her IRA and given it to Heidi, the money would not be exempt from creditors' claims. The Seventh Circuit correctly observed that it should not make a difference whether the money passed to Heidi on Ruth's death or a little earlier (Clark, 673 F.3d at 707). In both cases, the money is not being used for the beneficiary's retirement.

The Seventh Circuit's decision is consistent with the purpose of the Bankruptcy Code's exemption provisions. The homestead exemption, for example, requires that the debtor have lived in the property for a certain period (Section 522(b)(3)(A)). Similarly, "retirement funds" should be interpreted to require that the funds be held for the debtor's retirement. The Seventh Circuit's decision should be affirmed because it correctly interprets the statutory text and is consistent with the purpose of the Bankruptcy Code's exemption provisions.
