The issue before this Court is whether the rule announced in Johnson regarding what constitutes a violent offense applies retroactively to Gregory Welch's case. Although Johnson was decided after Welch's conviction became final, the principle that the Supreme Court's decisions construing federal statutes apply retroactively supports the retroactive application of Johnson. See United States v. Schooner Peggy, 5 U.S. 103 (1801). The Court in Schooner Peggy stated that "if subsequent to the judgment and before the decision of the appellate court, a law intervenes and positively changes the rule which governs, the law must be obeyed." Id. at 110.

The Armed Career Criminal Act (ACCA) imposes a minimum sentence of 15 years for defendants with three prior convictions for violent felonies or serious drug offenses. The ACCA defines a "violent felony" as a crime that involves certain elements or conduct. 18 U.S.C. § 924(e)(2)(B). The Supreme Court has held that a statute is void for vagueness if it fails to provide adequate notice of the conduct it prohibits. See United States v. L. Cohen Grocery Co., 255 U.S. 81, 89 (1921) (holding that a statute must be "so indefinite as really to be no rule or standard at all"). In Connally v. General Construction Co., the Court emphasized the importance of clarity in penal statutes, stating that they must be "sufficiently explicit to inform those who are subject to it what conduct on their part will render them liable to its penalties." 269 U.S. 385, 391 (1926).

In Johnson, the Court held that the residual clause of the ACCA was unconstitutionally vague because it failed to provide clear guidance on what constitutes a violent felony. The Court's decision in Johnson was based on the principle that a statute must provide fair notice of the conduct it prohibits. The residual clause was held to be unconstitutionally vague because it allowed for arbitrary and discriminatory enforcement, a concern also raised in Cline v. Frink Dairy Co., 274 U.S. 445 (1927).

The decision in Johnson applies retroactively to Welch's case because it is a substantive rule that changes the definition of a violent felony under the ACCA. See Davis v. United States, 417 U.S. 333, 346-47 (1974). The rule announced in Johnson is similar to the rule in United States v. United States Coin & Currency, 401 U.S. 715 (1971), which was applied retroactively. The rule announced in Johnson is similarly substantive and applies retroactively to Welch's case.

In light of the above authorities, the rule announced in Johnson applies retroactively to Gregory Welch's case. The residual clause of the ACCA is unconstitutionally vague, and Welch's prior conviction for strong arm robbery does not qualify as a predicate offense under the ACCA. Therefore, Welch's sentence should be vacated, and his case should be remanded for resentencing.

The relevant case authorities that support this argument are: United States v. Schooner Peggy, 5 U.S. 103 (1801); United States v. L. Cohen Grocery Co., 255 U.S. 81 (1921); Connally v. General Construction Co., 269 U.S. 385 (1926); Cline v. Frink Dairy Co., 274 U.S. 445 (1927); United States v. United States Coin & Currency, 401 U.S. 715 (1971); Davis v. United States, 417 U.S. 333 (1974); Screws v. United States, 325 U.S. 91 (1945).
