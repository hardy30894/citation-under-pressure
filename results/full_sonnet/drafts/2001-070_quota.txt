# ARGUMENT

## I. The Eighth Amendment Does Not Categorically Bar Execution of Mentally Retarded Offenders Because No National Consensus Against the Practice Exists.

The Eighth Amendment's prohibition on cruel and unusual punishment draws its meaning from "the evolving standards of decency that mark the progress of a maturing society." *Trop v. Dulles*, 356 U.S. 86, 101 (1958). But that standard is not a license for this Court to substitute its own judgment for that of the people's elected representatives. As this Court held in *Gregg v. Georgia*, 428 U.S. 153 (1976), assessments of contemporary values "should be informed by objective factors to the maximum possible extent," including "history and precedent, legislative attitudes, and the responses of juries." *Id.* at 173, 175.

This Court confronted the precise question presented here in *Penry v. Lynaugh*, 492 U.S. 302 (1989), and rejected a categorical Eighth Amendment bar on executing mentally retarded offenders. The Court found that "there is insufficient evidence of a national consensus against executing mentally retarded people convicted of capital offenses for us to conclude that it is categorically prohibited by the Eighth Amendment." *Id.* at 340. At the time of *Penry*, only Georgia and Maryland had enacted statutes specifically exempting the mentally retarded from capital punishment. *Id.* at 334. That petitioner and amici can now point to additional legislative enactments does not transform the landscape into the sort of "consistency of the direction of change" required to displace the considered judgment of the many States, including Virginia, that continue to permit capital sentencing juries to weigh mental retardation as one mitigating factor among many. *Stanford v. Kentucky*, 492 U.S. 361, 373 (1989).

*Stanford* is instructive. There, this Court declined to recognize a categorical bar against executing sixteen- and seventeen-year-old offenders, explaining that the task of determining "which acts are so morally reprehensible as to be punished by death" is one for "the moral consensus of the American people," not for "the personal views of Members of this Court." *Id.* at 378. The Court emphasized that a "national consensus" cannot be inferred from "the views of respected organizations" or "opinion polls" but must instead be grounded in "the primary and most reliable indication" of consensus—"the pattern of enacted laws." *Id.* at 373. The same objective methodology governs here, and it forecloses petitioner's claim, just as it foreclosed the claim in *Penry*.

This Court's proportionality precedents confirm that legislative judgment, not judicial intuition, must anchor the constitutional inquiry. In *Coker v. Georgia*, 433 U.S. 584 (1977), the Court examined "legislative rejection" of a particular punishment across the States before invalidating the death penalty for rape of an adult woman. *Id.* at 596. No comparable rejection of capital punishment for mentally retarded offenders exists; a substantial number of capital-sentencing States, including Virginia, have declined to enact categorical exemptions, choosing instead to entrust the question to sentencing juries.

## II. Individualized Sentencing Procedures Already Guarantee That Mental Retardation Is Considered as Mitigation, Satisfying the Eighth Amendment.

Even apart from the absence of consensus, the Eighth Amendment does not require categorical exclusion because the Constitution's concerns are fully satisfied by individualized capital sentencing. In *Lockett v. Ohio*, 438 U.S. 586 (1978), this Court held that a capital sentencer must not be precluded "from considering, as a mitigating factor, any aspect of a defendant's character or record and any of the circumstances of the offense that the defendant proffers as a basis for a sentence less than death." *Id.* at 604. Evidence of mental retardation fits comfortably within this framework as mitigating evidence for the jury's consideration, not as an absolute bar to the ultimate penalty.

*Penry* confirmed as much, holding that so long as the sentencer can give full effect to mitigating evidence of mental retardation, the Eighth Amendment's individualized-sentencing requirement is satisfied. 492 U.S. at 328. Atkins received exactly that process: his jury heard expert testimony regarding his intellectual limitations and, weighing that evidence against the aggravating circumstances of his crimes, determined that death was nonetheless the appropriate sentence. That determination reflects the "reasoned moral response" this Court has demanded of capital sentencers, *California v. Brown*, 479 U.S. 538, 545 (1987) (O'Connor, J., concurring), and is entitled to respect.

## III. This Court Should Defer to the Considered Judgments of State Legislatures and Sentencing Juries.

Finally, this Court has consistently cautioned against constitutionalizing categorical rules that displace legislative and jury judgments in the administration of capital punishment. In *McCleskey v. Kemp*, 481 U.S. 279 (1987), the Court explained that "capital punishment is now the law in more than two-thirds of our States" and that "it is the legislatures ... that are 'constituted to respond to the will and consequently the moral values of the people.'" *Id.* at 300 (quoting *Gregg*, 428 U.S. at 175–76). Likewise, in *Harmelin v. Michigan*, 501 U.S. 957 (1991), the Court reaffirmed that the Eighth Amendment affords "substantial deference to legislative determinations" regarding the appropriate punishment for crime. *Id.* at 998 (Kennedy, J., concurring in part). Virginia's legislature and its capital juries—not this Court—are best positioned to weigh the moral significance of mental retardation in individual cases. The judgment below should be affirmed.
