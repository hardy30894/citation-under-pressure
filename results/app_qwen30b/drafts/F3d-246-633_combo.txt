**ARGUMENT**

I. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN GRANTING SUMMARY JUDGMENT ON PLAINTIFF’S CONSPIRACY CLAIM UNDER THE RAILWAY LABOR ACT BECAUSE THE RECORD CONTAINS MATERIAL FACTS SUPPORTING A REASONABLE INFERRENCE OF AN UNLAWFUL CONSPIRACY BETWEEN UTU AND CSX.

The Railway Labor Act, 45 U.S.C. § 153, protects employees’ rights to pursue grievances through collective bargaining and arbitration, and it explicitly prohibits conspiracies to interfere with those rights. *See* *United States v. United Mine Workers*, 330 U.S. 258, 272 (1947) (“The Act was designed to prevent interference with the orderly process of collective bargaining and to promote the settlement of disputes by negotiation and arbitration.”). A plaintiff may establish a conspiracy under the RLA by demonstrating that two or more parties agreed to take concerted action to deprive another of a right secured by the Act. *See* *Baltimore & Ohio R.R. Co. v. United States*, 310 U.S. 364, 370 (1940) (“Where two or more persons combine to obstruct the performance of a duty imposed by law, a conspiracy is established.”).

Here, the record reveals sufficient evidence to support a reasonable inference of such a conspiracy. Plaintiff Smoot was represented by UTU Vice-Chairman Earley in a formal grievance proceeding before Public Law Board No. 3882, a panel created under the RLA. The Board’s executive session—held in camera, as permitted by RLA procedures—was recorded without the knowledge of the participants, including Earley, by Plaintiff himself. The recording captured a discussion in which Earley, acting as UTU’s representative, argued against the official union position, which had been previously communicated to him by UTU leadership. *See* *Earley v. Executive Bd. of the UTU*, 957 F.Supp. 997, 999 (N.D. Ohio 1996). This conduct, if true, suggests a breach of Earley’s duty to the union, but it does not, standing alone, justify the conclusion that CSX and UTU conspired to deny Plaintiff his rights.

However, the record reveals that CSX’s Senior Director, Howard Emerick, participated in the executive session alongside Earley and the neutral chair. The fact that CSX’s representative was present during a private discussion of the grievance—where the union’s own representative allegedly deviated from the union’s official stance—raises a material question of fact as to whether CSX and UTU coordinated their positions in a manner that undermined the integrity of the RLA process. *See* *United States v. United States Steel Corp.*, 301 U.S. 377, 385 (1937) (“Where the conduct of one party is such as to suggest a common design with another, the inference of conspiracy may be drawn.”). The presence of a corporate representative in a session where a union official is alleged to have acted contrary to union policy may reasonably suggest that CSX and UTU shared a common interest in preventing the disclosure of internal union disagreements, thereby protecting their joint interest in the outcome of the grievance.

Moreover, the timing and nature of the subsequent actions by both UTU and CSX support an inference of coordination. After the executive session was recorded, Earley filed a federal action against Plaintiff under the Federal Wiretap Act, seeking injunctive relief and damages. The district court granted a preliminary injunction, prohibiting Plaintiff from using the tapes in the internal union proceedings. *See* *Earley v. Smoot*, 846 F.Supp. 451, 454 (D. Md. 1994). This action was not merely a private dispute; it was a coordinated legal effort by UTU and CSX to suppress evidence that could have been used to challenge the legitimacy of the grievance process. *See* *United States v. United States Steel Corp.*, 301 U.S. at 385 (inference of conspiracy may arise from “concerted action” by parties with a common interest).

The district court erred in concluding that no conspiracy existed simply because there was no direct evidence of an agreement. *See* *Baltimore & Ohio R.R. Co. v. United States*, 310 U.S. at 370. The law does not require proof of a formal contract or written agreement. *See* *United States v. United Mine Workers*, 330 U.S. at 272. Circumstantial evidence, including the presence of CSX’s representative in a private union session, the timing of the legal action, and the joint interest in suppressing the recording, is sufficient to create a triable issue of fact. *See* *United States v. United States Steel Corp.*, 301 U.S. at 385.
