The District of Arizona's decision to invoke 28 USC section 1404(a) to assign the transferred case to itself for trial directly contravenes the explicit language of 28 USC section 1407(a), which mandates that the Judicial Panel on Multidistrict Litigation "shall" remand any action transferred for pretrial proceedings to the original district "at or before the conclusion of such pretrial proceedings." The use of the word "shall" in section 1407(a) is unequivocal, leaving no discretion for the transferee court to retain the case for trial.

The plain meaning of section 1407(a) is controlling, as established by a long line of Supreme Court decisions. See Caminetti v. United States, 242 U.S. 470, 485 (1917) ("It is elementary that the meaning of a statute must, in the first instance, be sought in the language in which the act is framed, and if that is plain...the sole function of the courts is to enforce it according to its terms."); United States v. Wiltberger, 18 U.S. 76, 95-96 (1820) (Marshall, C.J.) ("The intention of the legislature is to be collected from the words they employ."). The language of section 1407(a) is clear, and its meaning should be enforced according to its terms.

Allowing transferee courts to retain cases for trial under section 1404(a) would undermine the policy choice reflected in section 1407(a), which is to limit the transfer of cases to pretrial proceedings and preserve the plaintiff's traditional right to select the forum for trial. See Baltimore & Ohio R. Co. v. Kepner, 314 U.S. 44, 52-53 (1941) (emphasizing the importance of the plaintiff's choice of forum); Ex parte Collett, 337 U.S. 55, 58 (1949) (noting that section 1404(a) is a general transfer provision that must be read in conjunction with other statutory provisions governing the transfer of cases). The statutory scheme established by section 1407(a) is designed to balance the need for efficient pretrial proceedings with the need to respect the plaintiff's choice of forum. Permitting transferee courts to retain cases for trial would upset this balance and contravene the statutory purpose.

The general transfer provision of section 1404(a) is subordinate to the specific mandate of section 1407(a). See Ginsberg & Sons v. Popkin, 285 U.S. 204, 208 (1932) ("General language of a statutory provision, although broad enough to include it, will not be held to apply to a matter specifically dealt with in another part of the same enactment."). The specific directive of section 1407(a) takes precedence over the general transfer authority granted by section 1404(a), and the District of Arizona's decision to invoke section 1404(a) to retain the case for trial was therefore in error.

The importance of adhering to the statutory text and respecting the policy choices reflected in the statute is well-established. See, e.g., United States v. Dickerson, 310 U.S. 554, 562 (1940) ("The meaning to be ascribed...is to be drawn from the statute as a whole."); FTC v. Bunte Bros., 312 U.S. 349, 352-53 (1941) (noting that "the plain words of the statute" should be given effect). In light of these authorities, it is clear that the District of Arizona erred in invoking section 1404(a) to assign the transferred case to itself for trial, and the Ninth Circuit's affirmance of that decision was incorrect.

The case should be remanded to the original district for trial in accordance with the plain meaning of section 1407(a). By enforcing the statutory text, this Court can ensure that the policy choices reflected in section 1407(a) are respected and that the plaintiff's traditional right to select the forum for trial is preserved.
