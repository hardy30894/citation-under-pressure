The Massachusetts statute creating a 35-foot buffer zone around abortion clinics is a constitutional time, place, and manner regulation that does not violate the First Amendment. The First Circuit correctly upheld the law, and its decision should be affirmed.

The buffer zone is content-neutral, as it applies equally to all individuals, regardless of their message or viewpoint. The statute's exemptions for certain individuals, such as clinic employees and law enforcement officers, are narrowly tailored to serve the state's interest in ensuring public safety and protecting patients' access to healthcare facilities. See Hill v. Colorado, 530 U.S. 703, 725-26 (2000) (upholding a similar Colorado statute that prohibited certain activities within 100 feet of healthcare facility entrances).

The buffer zone also leaves open adequate alternative channels of communication for individuals like the petitioners, who engage in "sidewalk counseling" outside abortion clinics. As the First Circuit noted, the petitioners can still communicate with patients through oral speech, distribution of literature, displays of signage and symbols, and prayer alone or in groups. See McCullen v. Coakley, 708 F.3d 1, 13 (1st Cir. 2013). The fact that the buffer zone may make it more difficult for the petitioners to engage in "close personal contact" with patients does not render the statute unconstitutional. See Hill, 530 U.S. at 729-30 (rejecting a similar argument).

The petitioners' argument that the statute is unconstitutional as applied to them is unpersuasive. The First Circuit correctly applied the law of the case doctrine, which precludes relitigation of issues that were previously decided in the same case. See United States v. Matthews, 643 F.3d 9, 13 (1st Cir. 2011). The petitioners' reliance on recent Supreme Court decisions, such as Citizens United v. FEC, 558 U.S. 310 (2010), is misplaced, as those cases involved different factual scenarios and did not overrule or undermine the holding in Hill.

The Attorney General's guidance letter, which was issued to clarify the statute's exemptions, does not render the statute unconstitutional. The letter is not a binding interpretation of the statute, and the petitioners had ample opportunity to challenge it in the district court. The district court did not abuse its discretion in denying the petitioners' motion to amend their complaint to include a direct challenge to the letter. See Hatch v. Dep't for Children, Youth & Their Families, 274 F.3d 12, 19 (1st Cir. 2001).

In conclusion, the Massachusetts statute creating a 35-foot buffer zone around abortion clinics is a constitutional time, place, and manner regulation that does not violate the First Amendment. The First Circuit's decision upholding the law should be affirmed.
