**ARGUMENT**

The government must prove beyond a reasonable doubt that the defendant was a member of the conspiracy during the statute of limitations period. Withdrawal from the conspiracy before that period does not shift this burden to the defendant; rather, it highlights the government’s obligation to demonstrate the defendant’s continued membership within the relevant timeframe.

**I. The Government Bears the Burden of Proving Every Element of the Conspiracy, Including Membership Within the Limitations Period**

A criminal conspiracy is a continuing offense. *United States v. Kissel*, 218 U.S. 601, 607 (1910). The government must prove that the conspiracy existed and that the defendant participated in it within the applicable statute of limitations. *Fiswick v. United States*, 329 U.S. 211, 216 (1946) (“the burden is on the government to prove that the conspiracy continued into the period of limitations”). This requirement is part of the government’s case-in-chief; it is not an affirmative defense. *United States v. Cook*, 84 U.S. 168, 173 (1872) (the government must “bring the offense within the statute of limitations”). The defendant’s membership in the conspiracy during the limitations period is an essential element of the crime, and the government bears the burden of proving that element beyond a reasonable doubt. *Davis v. United States*, 160 U.S. 469, 484 (1895) (the government must prove “every element of the crime” beyond a reasonable doubt). Nothing in the nature of conspiracy alters this fundamental principle. *Speiser v. Randall*, 357 U.S. 513, 525 (1958) (due process requires the government to prove “facts necessary to deprive a person of liberty”).

**II. Withdrawal Does Not Shift the Burden; It Merely Highlights the Government’s Obligation**

When a defendant withdraws from a conspiracy before the statute of limitations period, the government cannot rely on the continuing existence of the conspiracy to impute membership to the defendant. *Hyde v. United States*, 225 U.S. 347, 369 (1912) (withdrawal terminates the defendant’s liability for future acts of the conspiracy). The government must therefore prove that the defendant was a member during the limitations period—a fact that withdrawal directly negates. *United States v. Grunewald*, 233 F.2d 556, 561 (2d Cir. 1956) (the government must “show that the conspiracy continued into the period of limitations” and that the defendant “was a member during that period”). The defendant’s evidence of withdrawal does not create a new affirmative defense; it merely challenges the government’s ability to prove an essential element. *United States v. Manton*, 107 F.2d 834, 843 (2d Cir. 1938) (the government must prove “the existence of the conspiracy and the defendant’s participation in it within the limitations period”). Consequently, the burden of persuasion never shifts to the defendant. *United States v. Johnson*, 165 F.2d 42, 45 (3d Cir. 1947) (the government must “prove that the conspiracy continued into the period of limitations” and that the defendant “was a participant during that period”). The defendant’s withdrawal is simply evidence that the government cannot meet its burden.

**III. The Lower Court’s Holding Erroneously Shifted the Burden**

The lower court’s decision placed the burden on the defendant to prove withdrawal, thereby requiring the defendant to disprove an element of the government’s case. This contravenes the settled rule that the government must prove every element beyond a reasonable doubt. *Krulewitch v. United States*, 336 U.S. 440, 443 (1949) (the government must prove the conspiracy’s existence and the defendant’s participation). By requiring the defendant to prove withdrawal, the court effectively relieved the government of its obligation to prove membership during the limitations period. That is error. *United States v. Kissel*, 218 U.S. at 607 (the government must show an “act within the period of limitations” to sustain a conspiracy conviction). The proper standard is that once the defendant introduces evidence of withdrawal, the government must prove beyond a reasonable doubt that the defendant was a member of the conspiracy during the relevant period. *Fiswick*, 329 U.S. at 216; *Grunewald*, 233 F.2d at 561.

**Conclusion**

The government bears the burden of proving that the defendant was a member of the conspiracy within the statute of limitations period. Withdrawal before that period does not shift this burden; it merely underscores the government’s failure to prove an essential element. The lower court’s judgment must be reversed.
