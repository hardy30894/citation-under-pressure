**ARGUMENT**

Milford Central School’s exclusion of the Good News Club’s proposed use of school premises for religious instruction and prayer does not violate the First Amendment. The Community Use Policy opens school facilities only for social, civic, and recreational meetings and other uses pertaining to the welfare of the community. It expressly prohibits religious purposes. The Club’s activities—singing songs about Christ, memorizing scripture, hearing Bible lessons, and praying—constitute religious instruction and worship rather than secular discussion of morals.

In *Illinois ex rel. McCollum v. Board of Education*, 333 U.S. 203 (1948), this Court held unconstitutional a program in which “religious teachers, employed by private religious groups, were permitted to come weekly into the school buildings during the regular hours set apart for secular teaching, and then and there for a period of thirty minutes substitute their religious teaching for the secular education provided under the compulsory education law.” *Id.* at 205. The same principle bars religious instruction inside school buildings here.

*Abington School District v. Schempp*, 374 U.S. 203 (1963), confirms that “the practices at issue and the laws requiring them are unconstitutional under the Establishment Clause, ‘as applied to the States through the Fourteenth Amendment.’” *Id.* at 205. Bible reading and prayer exercises, even when voluntary, advance religion. The Club’s meetings present the identical constitutional defect.

By contrast, *Zorach v. Clauson*, 343 U.S. 306 (1952), upheld released-time religious instruction conducted off school premises because “[t]his ‘released time’ program involves neither religious instruction in public school classrooms nor the expenditure of public funds.” *Id.* at 308. Milford’s policy respects that boundary by denying on-premises religious instruction.

*Board of Education v. Allen*, 392 U.S. 236 (1968), and *Cochran v. Louisiana State Board of Education*, 281 U.S. 370 (1930), permitted loans of secular textbooks to parochial-school students precisely because the books were not “appropriated for the purchase of school books for the use of any church, private, sectarian or even public school.” *Cochran*, 281 U.S. at 374. The Club seeks not neutral materials but active religious teaching.

The policy treats all religious groups alike. *Fowler v. Rhode Island*, 345 U.S. 67 (1953), condemned preference among sects; here Milford excludes religious worship uniformly. *Poulos v. New Hampshire*, 345 U.S. 395 (1953), recognized that reasonable, nondiscriminatory permit requirements for religious meetings in public places do not abridge speech or worship. Milford’s content-based restriction on religious instruction in a limited forum is therefore permissible.

Finally, *Kramer v. Union Free School District No. 15*, 395 U.S. 621 (1969), underscores that school facilities and elections remain subject to constitutional limits that protect the Establishment Clause. Because Milford has not opened its buildings for religious instruction, denying the Club access neither discriminates among viewpoints nor compels the school to violate the First Amendment.
