**ARGUMENT**

**I. Under *Penry v. Lynaugh*, the Eighth Amendment does not impose a categorical bar on the execution of mentally retarded offenders.**

This Court has already confronted the precise question presented here and rejected the categorical rule Atkins now seeks. In *Penry v. Lynaugh*, 492 U.S. 302 (1989), the Court held that "in light of the diverse capacities and life experiences of mentally retarded persons," a categorical exemption from capital punishment was unwarranted, and that mental retardation is instead "a factor that may well lessen a defendant's culpability for a capital offense" to be weighed by the sentencer along with the other evidence of aggravation and mitigation. *Id.* at 322, 328. Atkins offers no principled basis to abandon that holding, and stare decisis strongly counsels against doing so. This Court has long recognized that adherence to precedent "promotes the evenhanded, predictable, and consistent development of legal principles," a value with special force in the capital sentencing context, where this Court has repeatedly insisted upon individualized, reasoned decision-making rather than categorical rules that remove relevant evidence from the jury's consideration. *See* *Gregg v. Georgia*, 428 U.S. 153, 189 (1976) (plurality opinion) (the Eighth Amendment requires that "the punishment of death not be imposed in an arbitrary or capricious manner").

**II. Application of the Eighth Amendment turns on "evolving standards of decency," measured principally by objective legislative evidence, and that evidence does not establish the consensus necessary to overturn *Penry*.**

The touchstone of Eighth Amendment analysis is whether a punishment offends "the evolving standards of decency that mark the progress of a maturing society." *Trop v. Dulles*, 356 U.S. 86, 101 (1958) (plurality opinion). This inquiry is not a license for the Court to substitute its own judgment about the wisdom of executing mentally retarded offenders. As this Court explained in *Penry* itself, and reaffirmed in *Stanford v. Kentucky*, 492 U.S. 361 (1989), the "clearest and most reliable objective evidence of contemporary values is the legislation enacted by the country's legislatures." *Penry*, 492 U.S. at 331 (quoting *Gregg*, 428 U.S. at 175). At the time *Penry* was decided, only two death-penalty jurisdictions barred the execution of the mentally retarded, and the Court found that showing "insufficient" to establish a national consensus. *Id.* Although additional legislatures have since acted, a substantial number of death-penalty States have not, and continued legislative activity on the subject—rather than settled uniformity—is itself evidence that the matter remains one of vigorous ongoing democratic debate, not the kind of virtually unanimous rejection that has previously justified categorical Eighth Amendment rules. *Cf.* *Coker v. Georgia*, 433 U.S. 584, 593–96 (1977) (relying on the fact that only one jurisdiction retained the challenged punishment for the offense in question).

This case is unlike *Ford v. Wainwright*, 477 U.S. 399 (1986), in which the Court barred the execution of the insane based on a rule with "impressive historical credentials," *id.* at 406, tracing back through the common law and reflected in the unbroken practice of every American jurisdiction. No comparable historical or unanimous contemporary practice exists with respect to mentally retarded offenders. Where, as here, the objective indicia are mixed and evolving rather than uniform and settled, this Court should not constitutionalize one side of an unresolved policy debate that properly belongs to the state legislatures. *See* *Stanford*, 492 U.S. at 377–78 (plurality opinion) (declining to find a national consensus against executing 16- and 17-year-old offenders despite some legislative movement in that direction).

**III. Individualized capital sentencing procedures, including those employed in this case, already ensure that a defendant's mental limitations are given full mitigating weight.**

This Court's capital jurisprudence has consistently preferred individualized assessment of culpability over categorical exclusion. In *Lockett v. Ohio*, 438 U.S. 586 (1978), the Court held that a capital sentencer must not be precluded from considering, "as a mitigating factor, any aspect of a defendant's character or record and any of the circumstances of the offense." *Id.* at 604 (plurality opinion). That principle was fully honored below. Atkins was permitted to—and did—present expert testimony regarding his intellectual functioning at both sentencing proceedings, and the jury was free to weigh that evidence against the aggravating circumstances of the abduction, armed robbery, and capital murder he committed. The jury's rejection of Atkins' mitigation case, particularly after the Commonwealth rebutted the defense expert's assessment at resentencing, reflects precisely the individualized moral judgment this Court's precedents demand. *See* *McCleskey v. Kemp*, 481 U.S. 279, 311 (1987) ("[D]iscretion is essential to the criminal justice process."). A categorical rule removing this class of evidence from case-by-case evaluation—rather than requiring the sentencer to weigh it, as *Penry* directs—would displace the individualized sentencing scheme that this Court's precedents require and that Virginia's procedure faithfully implements.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Supreme Court of Virginia should be affirmed.
