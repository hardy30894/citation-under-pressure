The federal courts have exclusive jurisdiction over cases involving patent law, even when the patent issue is not the primary issue in the case. The Supreme Court has established that federal jurisdiction extends to cases where federal patent law is a necessary element of one of the well-pleaded claims. As stated in Pratt v. Paris Gas Light & Coke Co., 168 U.S. 255 (1897), the federal courts have exclusive jurisdiction over cases involving the validity and infringement of patents. Id. at 259.

The Court has long recognized the importance of federal law in determining the outcome of a case. In Shulthis v. McDougal, 225 U.S. 561 (1912), the Court stated that a suit arises under federal law when it "really and substantially involves a dispute or controversy respecting the validity, construction or effect of [federal law]." Id. at 569. Here, Minton's state-based legal malpractice claim necessarily depends on the resolution of a substantial question of federal patent law, specifically the experimental use exception to the on-sale bar.

The Court has also held that a case arises under federal law when it involves a dispute or controversy respecting federal law. Shulthis, 225 U.S. at 569. The validity of Minton's patent is at issue, and the experimental use exception is a critical component of that determination.

In Gully v. First Nat'l Bank in Meridian, 299 U.S. 109 (1936), the Court cautioned against an overly broad interpretation of "arising under" jurisdiction. The Court noted that not every case involving a federal question arises under federal law. Id. at 117-18. Applying this principle, it is clear that Minton's claim involves a substantial question of federal patent law that is necessary to the resolution of his state-based malpractice claim.

The Court has considered the importance of uniformity in the application of federal patent law. In Kendall v. Winsor, 62 U.S. 322 (1858), the Court emphasized the need for consistency in the application of patent law. Id. at 328. Allowing state courts to adjudicate patent issues in the context of malpractice claims could undermine this uniformity.

Furthermore, the Court has recognized that federal jurisdiction is exclusive in cases involving patent law. Pratt, 168 U.S. at 259. This principle is consistent with the idea that federal law is supreme in matters involving patent law.

In addition, the Court has stated that when a case involves a federal question, it is not necessary for the federal issue to be the primary issue in the case for federal jurisdiction to apply. In Osborn v. Bank of the United States, 22 U.S. 738 (1824), the Court held that federal jurisdiction is proper when a case involves a federal question, even if it is not the sole issue in the case. Id. at 822.

Other cases also support the proposition that federal courts have exclusive jurisdiction over patent law cases. See, e.g., Bloomer v. McQuewan, 55 U.S. 539 (1852); Wilson v. Sandford, 51 U.S. 99 (1850). These cases demonstrate that the federal courts have long been recognized as the proper forum for resolving disputes involving patent law.

In conclusion, based on the principles established in Shulthis, Gully, Kendall, Pratt, Osborn, Bloomer, and Wilson, it is clear that the federal courts have exclusive jurisdiction over Minton's state-based legal malpractice claim, which necessarily involves a substantial question of federal patent law.
