**ARGUMENT**

The Civil Service Reform Act channels “mixed cases”—those in which a federal employee challenges an adverse action appealable to the Merit Systems Protection Board and alleges that the action was motivated by prohibited discrimination—to district court. 5 U.S.C. § 7702(a)(1); Kloeckner v. Solis, 568 U.S. 41, 50 (2012). The Board’s dismissal of such a case on jurisdictional grounds does not alter that command.

**I.**

Section 7703(b)(2) directs that “cases of discrimination” described in § 7702(a)(1) “shall be filed” in district court. Kloeckner held that this rule applies whenever the Board dismisses a mixed-case appeal without reaching the merits, whether the ground is procedural or otherwise. 568 U.S. at 56. The Court’s reasoning turned on the employee’s allegation that the action was appealable to the Board and rested on discrimination, not on the Board’s ultimate disposition. Id. at 50, 56.

Powell v. Department of Defense, 158 F.3d 597 (D.C. Cir. 1998), held that a jurisdictional dismissal removes the case from § 7702(a)(1). That holding cannot survive Kloeckner. The statutory text requires only that the employee “has been affected by an action which [she] may appeal to the Board” and “alleges” discrimination. 5 U.S.C. § 7702(a)(1). A claim that retirement or suspension was involuntary because of discrimination satisfies both elements; the Board’s later conclusion that the action was voluntary does not erase the allegation. See Garcia v. Department of Homeland Security, 437 F.3d 1322, 1328 (Fed. Cir. 2006) (en banc).

**II.**

The distinction between procedural and jurisdictional dismissals is untenable. Kloeckner repeatedly described the question before it as the proper forum “when the MSPB has dismissed her mixed case on procedural grounds.” 568 U.S. at 603. Yet the Court cited with approval decisions that had treated jurisdictional dismissals identically. Harms v. IRS, 321 F.3d 1001, 1007-08 (10th Cir. 2003); Downey v. Runyon, 160 F.3d 139, 145-46 (2d Cir. 1998). The Court’s uniform rule for all pre-merits dismissals therefore governs here.

Conforto v. Merit Systems Protection Board, 713 F.3d 1111 (Fed. Cir. 2013), and the decision below rest on the premise that only procedural dismissals involve an “action which the employee may appeal to the Board.” That premise is inconsistent with Elgin v. Department of the Treasury, 567 U.S. 1, 13-14 (2012), which emphasized that the CSRA’s judicial-review provisions turn on the nature of the claim presented, not the Board’s jurisdictional ruling. See also United States v. Fausto, 484 U.S. 439, 449 (1988) (CSRA creates “comprehensive” and “integrated” scheme).

**III.**

Voluntariness determinations are inextricably intertwined with the merits of a discrimination claim. An employee who alleges that discrimination rendered a settlement involuntary presents precisely the type of mixed case Congress directed to district court. See Williams v. Department of the Army, 715 F.2d 1485, 1489-90 (Fed. Cir. 1983) (en banc). Requiring such claims to proceed in the Federal Circuit would produce the very “procedural ping-pong” Kloeckner condemned. 568 U.S. at 49.

The petition for review should therefore be transferred to the district court.
