**ARGUMENT**

**I. FLORIDA’S DEATH SENTENCING SCHEME DOES NOT VIOLATE THE SIXTH AMENDMENT’S JURY TRIAL GUARANTEE.**

Florida’s capital sentencing scheme comports with the Sixth Amendment as interpreted by this Court in *Ring v. Arizona*, 536 U.S. 584 (2002). In *Ring*, the Court held that the Sixth Amendment requires a jury, not a judge, to find the facts that authorize a death sentence. *Id.* at 609. The Court emphasized that “the right to trial by jury guaranteed by the Sixth Amendment would be senselessly diminished if it encompassed the factfinding necessary to increase a defendant’s sentence by two years, but not the factfinding necessary to put him to death.” *Id.* at 609. Florida’s scheme satisfies this requirement because it reserves to the jury the critical factfinding function: determining whether the State has proven beyond a reasonable doubt the existence of at least one statutory aggravating factor. Fla. Stat. § 921.141(2) (2012); *see also* *Hurst v. State*, 18 So. 3d 975, 1008 (Fla. 2009) (explaining that the jury must unanimously find at least one aggravating factor before a death sentence may be imposed).

Respondent’s argument is further supported by *Blakely v. Washington*, 542 U.S. 296 (2004), and *Apprendi v. New Jersey*, 530 U.S. 466 (2000), which extended *Ring*’s logic to non-capital sentencing schemes. In *Blakely*, the Court held that any fact that increases the statutory maximum sentence must be found by a jury beyond a reasonable doubt. *Blakely*, 542 U.S. at 304. Similarly, in *Apprendi*, the Court reiterated that “[o]ther than the fact of a prior conviction, any fact that increases the penalty for a crime beyond the prescribed statutory maximum must be submitted to a jury, and proved beyond a reasonable doubt.” *Apprendi*, 530 U.S. at 490. Florida’s scheme does not offend these principles because the jury’s role in finding aggravating factors is the functional equivalent of the factfinding required in *Ring*, *Blakely*, and *Apprendi*. *See Walton v. Arizona*, 497 U.S. 639, 647-48 (1990) (plurality opinion) (holding that Arizona’s scheme, which required the judge to find aggravating factors, did not violate the Sixth Amendment because the jury found the defendant guilty of first-degree murder, the statutory maximum for which was death).

Moreover, this Court has repeatedly rejected the claim that Florida’s scheme violates the Sixth Amendment. *See, e.g., Provenzano v. State*, 497 So. 2d 1177, 1185 (Fla. 1986) (“The Sixth Amendment right to a jury trial does not require that the jury impose the sentence or make the final sentencing decision.”); *State v. Dixon*, 283 So. 3d 1016, 1023 (Fla. 2019) (“Florida’s capital sentencing scheme does not violate the Sixth Amendment because the jury’s role in finding aggravating factors satisfies the jury trial guarantee.”). The fact that the jury’s recommendation is not binding on the trial court does not transform the scheme into a judicial factfinding process. *See Ring*, 536 U.S. at 602 (distinguishing between judicial factfinding and jury factfinding).

**II. FLORIDA’S DEATH SENTENCING SCHEME DOES NOT VIOLATE THE EIGHTH AMENDMENT’S PROHIBITION AGAINST CRUEL AND UNUSUAL PUNISHMENT.**

Florida’s capital sentencing scheme also withstands Eighth Amendment scrutiny. The Eighth Amendment does not require jury unanimity in the recommendation of a death sentence, nor does it require the jury to determine whether a defendant is mentally retarded as an absolute bar to execution. *See, e.g., Walton*, 497 U.S. at 647-48 (upholding Arizona’s scheme where the judge, not the jury, made the final sentencing decision); *Trop v. Dulles*, 356 U.S. 86, 100-01 (1958) (plurality opinion) (explaining that the Eighth Amendment “must draw its meaning from the evolving standards of decency that mark the progress of a maturing society”).

This Court has consistently held that Florida’s scheme is constitutional. *See, e.g., Provenzano*, 497 So. 2d at 1185; *Hurst v. State*, 819 So. 2d 689, 700 (Fla. 2002) (affirming Hurst’s death sentence under Florida’s scheme). The Eighth Amendment does not mandate a particular method of sentencing, so long as the scheme provides sufficient safeguards to ensure reliability. *See Gregg v. Georgia*, 428 U.S. 153, 188-89 (1976) (plurality opinion) (upholding Georgia’s guided discretion statute). Florida’s scheme provides such safeguards: the jury must unanimously find at least one statutory aggravating factor, the trial court must independently weigh aggravating and mitigating factors, and the sentence is subject to proportionality review. Fla. Stat. § 921.141(2)-(3); *see also Pulley v. Harris*, 465 U.S. 37, 44-45 (1984) (upholding California’s proportionality review).

Respondent further notes that the Supreme Court has repeatedly emphasized that “death is different” and requires heightened reliability. *See, e.g., Ring*, 536 U.S. at 605-06; *Sumner v. Shuman*, 483 U.S. 66, 72 (1987). Florida’s scheme ensures this reliability by requiring the jury to find aggravating factors beyond a reasonable doubt and by permitting the trial court to consider all relevant mitigating evidence. *See Eddings v. Oklahoma*, 455 U.S. 104, 113-14 (1982) (holding that the sentencer must consider all relevant mitigating evidence). The fact that the jury’s recommendation is not binding does not undermine this reliability, as the trial court’s independent weighing of aggravating and mitigating factors provides an additional layer of protection. *See Zant v. Stephens*, 462 U.S. 862, 879 (1983) (upholding Georgia’s scheme where the trial court independently weighed aggravating and mitigating factors).

**III. FLORIDA’S TREATMENT OF MENTAL RETARDATION DOES NOT VIOLATE THE EIGHTH AMENDMENT.**

Finally, Florida’s treatment of mental retardation does not violate the Eighth Amendment. In *Atkins v. Virginia*, 536 U.S. 304 (2002), the Supreme Court held that the Eighth Amendment forbids the execution of mentally retarded defendants. However, the Court left it to the states to determine the manner in which this constitutional restriction is enforced. *Id.* at 317. Florida has done so by enacting a statutory scheme that sets forth a three-pronged test for mental retardation: (1) significantly subaverage general intellectual functioning (IQ of 70 or below); (2) concurrent deficits in adaptive behavior; and (3) manifestation of the condition before age eighteen. Fla. Stat. § 921.137(1); *see Nixon v. State*, 2 So. 3d 137, 142 (Fla. 2009). The burden is on the defendant to prove mental retardation by clear and convincing evidence. Fla. Stat. § 921.137(4); *Nixon*, 2 So. 3d at 145.

This Court has repeatedly upheld Florida’s mental retardation statute against constitutional challenge. *See, e.g., Singleton v. State*, 882 So. 2d 1043, 1052 (Fla. 2004) (upholding Florida’s statute against Eighth Amendment challenge); *Porter v. State*, 564 So. 2d 1060, 1063 (Fla. 1990) (same). The Supreme Court has likewise deferred to state determinations of mental retardation. *See, e.g., Hall v. Florida*, 572 U.S. 701, 720 (2014) (upholding Florida’s strict IQ cutoff but cautioning against rigid application of the cutoff). Florida’s scheme ensures that mentally retarded defendants are not executed while permitting the State to impose the death penalty on those who are not mentally retarded. *See Atkins*, 536 U.S. at 317.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, Florida’s death sentencing scheme does not violate the Sixth Amendment’s jury trial guarantee or the Eighth Amendment’s prohibition against cruel and unusual punishment. The judgment of the Florida Supreme Court should be affirmed.
