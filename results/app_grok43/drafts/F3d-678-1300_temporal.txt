I. The Record Does Not Establish an Invalidating Public Use or Sale.

The on-sale and public-use bars require that the invention be “ready for patenting” through a commercial exploitation that is neither experimental nor secret. Pennock v. Dialogue, 27 U.S. (2 Pet.) 1, 19 (1829). An inventor may test an embodiment “in good faith for the purpose of testing the qualities of the invention” without triggering the bar. City of Elizabeth v. American Nicholson Pavement Co., 97 U.S. 126, 134 (1877). The evidence here shows only such testing.

Leader’s January 2002 white paper offered licenses while simultaneously stating that the platform remained “operational now with low user volumes.” J.A. 27207. Contemporaneous testimony established that the underlying Digital Leaderboard® engine was still undergoing refinement “in the 2002ish time frame.” J.A. 24829. These facts align with the experimental-use doctrine: the inventor “must be allowed to test it” before the statutory period begins. City of Elizabeth, 97 U.S. at 137.

No completed sale occurred. An offer to sell “an invention not yet perfected” does not constitute an invalidating transaction. Consolidated Fruit-Jar Co. v. Wright, 94 U.S. 92, 94 (1876). The white paper’s reference to “fully developed” software described only the engine, not the specific data-tagging and context-tracking features later claimed. Because the offered embodiment lacked the precise limitations of claims 1, 4, 7, 9, 11, 16, 21, 23, 25, 31, and 32, it could not anticipate them. Smith & Griggs Mfg. Co. v. Sprague, 123 U.S. 249, 257 (1887).

II. The District Court Erred in Denying JMOL.

Where the only evidence of pre-critical-date activity is experimental and the claimed combination is not shown to have been sold, judgment as a matter of law is required. Egbert v. Lippmann, 104 U.S. 333, 337 (1881); Hall v. Macneale, 107 U.S. 90, 96 (1883). The district court’s refusal to enter such judgment was error.
