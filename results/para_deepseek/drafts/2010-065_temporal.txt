**ARGUMENT**

**I. A Private Party Lacks Standing to Invoke the Tenth Amendment to Challenge a Federal Statute.**

The Tenth Amendment provides that “[t]he powers not delegated to the United States by the Constitution, nor prohibited by it to the States, are reserved to the States respectively, or to the people.” U.S. Const. amend. X. By its plain terms, this Amendment operates as a structural limitation on federal power, reserving certain sovereign authority to the States. It does not confer individual rights upon private persons. *See Tennessee Electric Power Co. v. Tennessee Valley Authority*, 306 U.S. 118, 143-144 (1939). In *Tennessee Electric*, private utility companies challenged the sale of electricity by the federally chartered Tennessee Valley Authority, arguing that such action violated the Tenth Amendment. The Supreme Court squarely held that the private plaintiffs lacked standing to raise that claim. The Court explained: “The sale of government property in competition with others is not a violation of the Tenth Amendment. As we have seen there is no objection to the Authority’s operations by the states, and, if this were not so, the [utility companies], absent the states or their officers, have no standing in this suit to raise any question under the [Tenth] Amendment.” *Id.* at 144.

This holding is not dictum. The Court reached the standing question as a necessary predicate to its merits analysis. *See Medeiros v. Vincent*, 431 F.3d 25, 34 (1st Cir. 2005) (citing *California v. United States*, 438 U.S. 645, 689 n.10 (1978)). Because standing implicates the constitutional jurisdiction of a federal court, *Steel Co. v. Citizens for a Better Env’t*, 523 U.S. 83, 94-95 (1998), the Court’s resolution of that issue controls here.

**II. Bond’s Claim Does Not Fall Within Any Recognized Exception to the General Rule.**

Bond argues that she may challenge the statute because her conviction directly injures her. But the Tenth Amendment protects States, not individuals. A private party suffers no cognizable injury from an alleged federal intrusion on state sovereignty. *See Frothingham v. Mellon*, 262 U.S. 447, 488-489 (1923) (holding that a private plaintiff lacked standing to challenge a federal statute as an infringement on state powers reserved by the Tenth Amendment). The Court in *Frothingham* emphasized that the plaintiff’s interest as a taxpayer was “comparatively minute and indeterminable” and that the “administration of the statute is a matter of public concern, not a matter of private right.” *Id.* at 487. Similarly, Bond’s interest in avoiding prosecution does not transform her into a surrogate for the Commonwealth of Pennsylvania.

**III. The Treaty Power Provides an Independent Source of Authority for the Statute.**

Even if Bond had standing, her challenge would fail on the merits. The Chemical Weapons Convention is a valid treaty, and Congress acted within its authority under the Necessary and Proper Clause to implement that treaty through 18 U.S.C. § 229. *See Missouri v. Holland*, 252 U.S. 416, 432 (1920). In *Holland*, the Court upheld a federal statute implementing a treaty protecting migratory birds, even though the statute would have been unconstitutional absent the treaty. The Court reasoned: “If the treaty is valid there can be no dispute about the validity of the statute under Article I, § 8, as a necessary and proper means to execute the powers of the Government.” *Id.* at 432. The Court further observed that “it is obvious that there may be matters of the sharpest exigency for the national well being that an act of Congress could not deal with but that a treaty followed by such an act could.” *Id.* at 433.

Bond’s conduct—using toxic chemicals to poison another person—falls squarely within the treaty’s prohibition on the use of chemical weapons. The statute requires no additional federal nexus because the treaty itself supplies the constitutional foundation. *See United States v. Lue*, 134 F.3d 79, 84 (2d Cir. 1998) (citing *Holland* for the proposition that “if a treaty is valid, there can be no dispute about the validity of the statute implementing it under the Necessary and Proper Clause”).

**IV. The Statute Is Not Vague or Overbroad as Applied to Bond.**

Bond’s vagueness challenge also fails. A statute is unconstitutionally vague if it fails to give fair notice of the conduct it prohibits. *See United States v. Harriss*, 347 U.S. 612, 617 (1954). Section 229 plainly prohibits the possession or use of a “chemical weapon,” defined as a “toxic chemical” that causes “death, temporary incapacitation or permanent harm to humans or animals.” 18 U.S.C. § 229F(8)(A). Bond, a trained microbiologist, stole potassium dichromate and 10-chloro-10H-phenoxarsine and repeatedly applied them to her victim’s doorknobs, car handles, and mailbox. She knew these substances were toxic. The statute gave her clear notice that her conduct was unlawful.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
