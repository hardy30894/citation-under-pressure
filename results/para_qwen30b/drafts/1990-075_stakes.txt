**ARGUMENT**

I. THE DELEGATION OF TEMPORARY SCHEDULING AUTHORITY TO THE ATTORNEY GENERAL IS CONSTITUTIONALLY PERMISSIBLE UNDER THE INTELLIGIBLE PRINCIPLE TEST ESTABLISHED IN J.W. HAMPTON, JR. & CO. V. UNITED STATES, 276 U.S. 394 (1928), AND REAFFIRMED IN MISTRETTA V. UNITED STATES, 488 U.S. 361 (1989).

The Constitution vests all legislative power in Congress, U.S. Const. art. I, § 1, but does not prohibit Congress from delegating authority to other branches when it provides an “intelligible principle” to guide the delegate. J.W. Hampton, Jr. & Co. v. United States, 276 U.S. 394, 409 (1928). This principle has been consistently upheld by the Supreme Court, including in *Mistretta v. United States*, 488 U.S. 361 (1989), where the Court upheld Congress’s delegation of authority to the Federal Sentencing Commission to promulgate sentencing guidelines. The Court emphasized that “Congress may not abdicate or transfer its essential legislative functions,” but may “obtain assistance from coordinate branches” so long as it “lays down by legislative act an intelligible principle to which the person or body authorized to act is directed to conform.” *Mistretta*, 488 U.S. at 654 (quoting *J.W. Hampton*, 276 U.S. at 409).

The Controlled Substances Act (CSA) satisfies this standard. Section 811(h) authorizes the Attorney General to temporarily schedule a substance if it poses an “imminent hazard to the public safety.” 21 U.S.C. § 811(h)(1). This standard is not vague or amorphous. It is anchored in specific, objective criteria: the substance’s potential for abuse, its history and pattern of abuse, the scope and significance of abuse, and the risk it poses to public health. See id. § 811(c)(4), (5), (6). These factors are not mere suggestions—they are mandatory considerations. The Attorney General must also provide notice and an opportunity for comment, and must consider the views of the Secretary of Health and Human Services. Id. § 811(h)(4). The temporary schedule is limited to one year, with a possible six-month extension, and is subject to a permanent rulemaking proceeding. Id. § 811(h)(2). These procedural and substantive constraints constitute an “intelligible principle” sufficient to pass constitutional muster.

The Toubys’ argument that the delegation is impermissible because it allows the creation of new crimes is foreclosed by *Mistretta*. There, the Court upheld the delegation of authority to define criminal penalties through the Sentencing Guidelines, rejecting the claim that such delegations are categorically invalid. *Mistretta*, 488 U.S. at 655–57. The Court held that “the Constitution does not prohibit Congress from delegating authority to a coordinate branch when it lays down an intelligible principle to guide the delegate.” Id. at 654. The same reasoning applies here: Congress has not abdicated its legislative function; it has merely empowered the Attorney General to act swiftly in response to emerging threats, within a framework of clear, objective standards.

II. THE ATTORNEY GENERAL’S SUBDELEGATION OF TEMPORARY SCHEDULING AUTHORITY TO THE DEA ADMINISTRATOR IS EXPRESSLY AUTHORIZED BY CONGRESS AND CONSISTENT WITH THE DELEGATION DOCTRINE.

Congress has long authorized the Attorney General to delegate functions under the CSA. In 1973, the Attorney General issued a regulation, 28 C.F.R. § 0.100(b), subdelegating to the DEA Administrator the authority to perform functions “vested in the Attorney General by the Comprehensive Drug Abuse Prevention and Control Act of 1970.” This regulation was reaffirmed and expanded in 1987, when the Attorney General amended the regulation to include the temporary scheduling authority conferred by the 1984 amendments. 28 C.F.R. § 0.100(b) (1989). The regulation explicitly states that the DEA Administrator “shall have the authority to perform all functions and duties delegated to the Attorney General under the Act.” This delegation is not only consistent with the CSA’s structure but is also a routine and well-established practice in federal administrative law.

The Supreme Court has repeatedly upheld such subdelegations when they are authorized by statute and accompanied by adequate standards. In *United States v. Fahey*, 332 U.S. 245 (1947), the Court upheld a delegation to a federal agency to administer a program under a statute that provided “well-known and generally acceptable standards.” Id. at 250. Here, the standards are not only well-known—they are codified in the CSA itself. The DEA Administrator is not acting in a vacuum; he is bound by the same statutory criteria that govern the Attorney General. The delegation is thus not an abdication of responsibility but a practical means of ensuring efficient and expert enforcement.

Moreover, the Toubys do not dispute that the DEA Administrator followed the statutory procedures: notice in the Federal Register, consideration of the Secretary of HHS’s views, and a determination that Euphoria posed an imminent hazard to public safety. 52 Fed. Reg. 30,174 (1987). The fact that the DEA Administrator exercised the delegated authority in a manner consistent with the law confirms the legitimacy of the subdelegation.

III. THE PROVISION IN § 811(h)(6) THAT “NO ORDER ISSUED UNDER THIS SUBSECTION SHALL BE SUBJECT TO JUDICIAL REVIEW” DOES NOT BAR CONSTITUTIONAL CHALLENGES.

The Toubys argue that the absence of judicial review under § 811(h)(6) renders the delegation unconstitutional. This argument is foreclosed by *Califano v. Sanders*, 430 U.S. 99 (1977), where the Court held that “the availability of judicial review is presumed” and that “Congress’ intent to foreclose review must be manifested by ‘clear and convincing’ evidence.” Id. at 109.
