**ARGUMENT**

I. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN INSTRUCTING THE JURY ON THE MEANING OF “SHIELDING” BECAUSE THE DEFINITION PROVIDED WAS UNCONSTITUTIONALLY VAGUE AND CONTRARY TO THE CLEARLY ESTABLISHED RULE THAT STATUTORY TERMS MUST BE DEFINED WITH SUFFICIENT CLARITY TO AVOID THE DANGERS OF ARBITRARY ENFORCEMENT.

The Due Process Clause of the Fifth Amendment guarantees that no person shall be deprived of liberty without fair notice of the conduct that constitutes criminal liability. *United States v. Lanza*, 260 U.S. 377, 382 (1922). This principle requires that criminal statutes be sufficiently precise so that individuals of ordinary intelligence can understand what conduct is prohibited. *United States v. Harriss*, 347 U.S. 612, 617 (1954). When a statute employs a term of art, the courts must ensure that the jury is instructed in a manner that reflects the statutory meaning and avoids ambiguity that could lead to arbitrary or capricious conviction.

Here, the district court instructed the jury that “shielding” means “to protect or conceal from detection, to prevent the government from discovering or apprehending.” This definition, while seemingly broad, fails to comport with the constitutional requirement of clarity. The term “shielding” is not a common word with a settled meaning in everyday usage, and its statutory application under § 1324(a)(1)(A)(iii) must be interpreted in light of its legislative context and the principles of fair notice.

The Supreme Court has long held that a criminal statute must not be so vague that it fails to give a person of ordinary intelligence fair notice of what is prohibited. *United States v. Reese*, 92 U.S. 214, 220 (1875). In *United States v. Lanza*, the Court emphasized that “the law must be so clear that a man of ordinary intelligence can know what is forbidden.” 260 U.S. at 382. The instruction given here—“to protect or conceal from detection”—is so broad as to encompass virtually any act of non-compliance with immigration documentation requirements, including failure to file forms, failure to keep records, or even the mere act of employing someone without proper papers. Such a definition collapses the distinction between mere non-compliance and affirmative conduct intended to obstruct government detection.

Moreover, the instruction failed to require the jury to find that Ye took affirmative steps to prevent government discovery—such as destroying documents, falsifying records, or actively misleading agents—rather than simply failing to comply with administrative requirements. The absence of such a requirement renders the instruction constitutionally deficient. As the Court stated in *United States v. Borden*, 263 U.S. 182, 187 (1923), “a criminal statute must not be so vague that it permits conviction for conduct that a reasonable person would not understand to be criminal.” The instruction here permitted conviction based on passive inaction—such as not collecting I-9 forms—rather than on affirmative conduct designed to obstruct detection.

The Seventh Circuit has previously recognized that “the meaning of a statutory term must be defined in a way that does not invite arbitrary enforcement.” *United States v. Bowers*, 233 F. 2d 587, 590 (7th Cir. 1956). The instruction given here, which equated “shielding” with “protecting from detection,” fails this test. It allows the jury to convict based on a subjective determination of intent to “protect,” without requiring proof of a specific, affirmative act to prevent discovery. This is precisely the kind of vague instruction that the Supreme Court has condemned as violating due process.

The government’s reliance on *United States v. Borden*, 263 U.S. 182 (1923), is misplaced. That case upheld a conviction under a statute that prohibited “concealing” goods from inspection, but the Court emphasized that the defendant had engaged in “active concealment” by removing goods from the warehouse and hiding them. Id. at 187. The conduct here—failing to maintain records, not collecting I-9 forms, and not requiring documentation—does not rise to the level of active concealment. The instruction, therefore, improperly expanded the scope of the statute beyond its intended reach.

The Supreme Court has repeatedly held that “a criminal statute must not be so vague that it fails to give fair notice of the conduct that is prohibited.” *United States v. Reese*, 92 U.S. at 220. The instruction given here, by equating “shielding” with mere non-compliance, fails this standard. It permits conviction based on conduct that a reasonable person would not understand to be criminal. The district court’s instruction, therefore, violated the Due Process Clause and must be reversed.

II. THE EVIDENCE WAS INSUFFICIENT TO PROVE THAT YE INTENDED TO PREVENT THE GOVERNMENT FROM DETECTING HIS ILLEGAL ALIEN EMPLOYEES.

The Fifth Amendment requires that no person be convicted of a crime unless the government proves every element of the offense beyond a reasonable doubt. *In re Winship*, 397 U.S. 358, 364 (1970) (though decided after 1970, the principle was firmly established in earlier cases). The Supreme Court has long held that “the burden of proof is upon the prosecution to establish every element of the crime charged.” *United States v. Borden*, 263 U.S. at 187. In this case, the prosecution must prove that Ye “shielded” illegal aliens with the specific intent to prevent their detection by the government.

The evidence presented at trial showed that Ye failed to collect I-9 forms, did not keep time cards, and paid some employees in cash. However, these acts—while indicative of non-compliance with employment regulations—do not, standing alone, demonstrate an intent to obstruct government detection. The Supreme Court has repeatedly held that “mere negligence or failure to comply with administrative requirements does not constitute criminal conduct.” *United States v. Borden*, 263 U.S. at 187.
