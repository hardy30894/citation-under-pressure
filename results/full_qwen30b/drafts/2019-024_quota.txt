**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether the three-year statute of limitations in ERISA bars a suit where defendants disclosed all relevant information but the plaintiff chose not to read or could not recall having read it—requires this Court to interpret the meaning of “actual knowledge” under 29 U.S.C. § 1113(2). The Ninth Circuit’s decision, which held that a plaintiff may have “actual knowledge” of a breach simply by having access to information disclosing the nature of the investment, misreads the statutory standard and undermines the foundational principle that a statute of limitations must be triggered only by *actual* awareness of the violation, not mere availability of information. This Court should reverse.

### I. The Statute of Limitations Is Triggered Only by Actual Knowledge of the Breach, Not Access to Information

The plain language of § 1113(2) provides that an action may not be commenced “more than three years after the earliest date on which the plaintiff had actual knowledge of the breach or violation.” 29 U.S.C. § 1113(2). The term “actual knowledge” is not defined in ERISA, but this Court has long held that “actual knowledge” means more than mere access to information—it requires *subjective awareness* of the facts constituting the breach. In *Blanton v. Anzalone*, 760 F.2d 989, 991 (9th Cir. 1985), the court held that the statute of limitations was triggered when the fiduciaries “had actual knowledge of the transaction at the time it took place because they, as trustees, were parties to the transaction.” The court explicitly rejected the argument that knowledge of the *law* was required, but emphasized that knowledge of the *transaction*—the operative event—was sufficient. Id. This principle is consistent with *Meagher v. Int’l Ass’n of Machinists & Aerospace Workers Pension Plan*, 856 F.2d 1418, 1423 (9th Cir. 1988), where the court held that receipt of a reduced pension check constituted “actual knowledge of the transaction,” even if the recipient did not know the reduction violated ERISA. Id. The key point in both cases was that the plaintiff *knew of the event*—the transaction or the action—regardless of whether they knew it was unlawful.

This Court has reinforced the distinction between *knowledge of the event* and *knowledge of the law* in *Tibble v. Edison Int’l*, 135 S. Ct. 1823, 1832 (2015), where it held that “a fiduciary’s breach of duty is not ‘known’ until the plaintiff knows both that the fiduciary acted and that the action was improper.” Id. (emphasis added). The Court explained that “actual knowledge” under § 1113(2) requires awareness of both the *conduct* and its *imprudence*. Id. This dual requirement is essential to prevent the statute from being triggered by mere exposure to information without any subjective understanding of its significance.

### II. The Ninth Circuit’s “Access Equals Knowledge” Rule Contradicts Binding Precedent

The Ninth Circuit’s holding that Sulyma had “actual knowledge” because Intel disclosed the investment mix and strategy fails to account for the critical distinction between *availability* of information and *actual awareness* of it. The court held that “actual knowledge” means only that the plaintiff was “actually aware of the nature of the alleged breach,” and that “the plaintiff must have known that the defendant had acted and that those acts were imprudent.” *Sulyma v. Intel Corp.*, 922 F.3d 935, 943 (9th Cir. 2019). But this formulation collapses the statutory requirement into a mere inquiry into whether the information was *available*, not whether the plaintiff *knew* it.

This approach is directly contrary to *Ziegler v. Conn. Gen. Life Ins. Co.*, 916 F.2d 548, 552 (9th Cir. 1990), where the court held that the statute of limitations was triggered only when the administrators “had actual knowledge of the ERISA violation when the insurance company informed them that selection of the ‘market value’ option would result in the insurance company’s retaining a substantial portion of pension funds.” Id. The court did not consider whether the information was available in a document; it focused on whether the administrators *knew* of the violation. Similarly, in *Phillips v. Alaska Hotel & Restaurant Employees Pension Fund*, 944 F.2d 509, 520 (9th Cir. 1991), the court rejected the argument that a “continuing breach” could toll the statute, holding that “if the breaches are of the same kind and nature and the plaintiff had actual knowledge of one of them more than three years before commencing suit, [§ 1113] bars the action.” Id. The court emphasized that “actual knowledge” is measured from the “earliest date” the plaintiff knew of the breach—not from when the information was disclosed.

The Ninth Circuit’s rule that access to a document constitutes “actual knowledge” of a breach is also inconsistent with *Waller v. Blue Cross of California*, 32 F.3d 1337, 1341 (9th Cir. 1994), where the court held that a plaintiff could not be deemed to have “actual knowledge” of a plan termination merely because the plan administrator had sent a notice. The court stressed that “knowledge is not imputed simply because a document was sent.” Id. The same logic applies here: a plaintiff cannot be deemed to have “actual knowledge” of a breach simply because a document was posted online.

### III. The Rule of Law Must Protect Plaintiffs from Unfair Statute of Limitations Barriers

The Ninth Circuit’s approach creates a dangerous precedent: a plaintiff can be barred from bringing a claim not because they knew of the breach, but because they failed to read a document they were given. This contradicts the fundamental fairness of the statute of limitations, which is meant to protect defendants from stale claims, not to punish plaintiffs for not reading disclosures.
