**Argument**

The judgment should be affirmed because the Constitution fixes the qualifications for Members of Congress and leaves no room for a State to add to them. Article I states the exclusive qualifications for the House and Senate: a Representative must be at least 25, seven years a citizen, and an inhabitant of the State at election; a Senator must be at least 30, nine years a citizen, and an inhabitant of the State at election. U.S. Const. art. I, §§ 2, cl. 2; 3, cl. 3. In *Powell v. McCormack*, 395 U.S. 486, 522-48 (1969), the Court held that these textual qualifications are exclusive and that neither Congress nor the States may supplement them. The same principle controls here.

This Court has repeatedly treated the Qualifications Clauses as a fixed and exhaustive list. In *U.S. Term Limits, Inc. v. Thornton*, 514 U.S. 779, 783-84, 827-35 (1995), the Court held that Arkansas could not deny ballot access to otherwise qualified congressional candidates who had already served the number of terms specified in Amendment 73. The Court reasoned that the power to prescribe qualifications for federal office does not belong to the States as part of their original sovereign powers and was not reserved to them by the Tenth Amendment. *Id.* at 801-05. Rather, when the Constitution created the National Legislature, it established national qualifications applicable in every State. *Id.* at 804-05.

That result follows from the structure of the Constitution and the Framers’ design. In *The Federalist No. 60*, Hamilton explained that the qualifications of those who are to represent the people in the federal legislature should be uniform. The Constitution’s text and history likewise show that the Framers rejected state power to add qualifications beyond those listed in Article I. *Thornton*, 514 U.S. at 795-806; *Powell*, 395 U.S. at 532-43. State-imposed term limits are therefore unconstitutional whether they operate directly as a disqualification or indirectly through ballot-access restrictions. A State “may not evade the Qualifications Clauses by dress[ing] up” an additional qualification as a ballot rule. *Thornton*, 514 U.S. at 828-29.

The Arkansas measure does exactly what the Constitution forbids. By providing that persons who have served three House terms or two Senate terms “shall not be certified as a candidate” and “shall not be eligible to have his/her name placed on the ballot,” Amendment 73 imposes an additional qualification for federal office. *U.S. Term Limits, Inc. v. Thornton*, 514 U.S. at 829-31. The practical effect is to disqualify a class of otherwise eligible candidates from seeking reelection based on prior service. That is an impermissible alteration of Article I qualifications, not a permissible regulation of election mechanics. *Id.* at 835-37; *Cook v. Gralike*, 531 U.S. 510, 523-24 (2001) (States may regulate elections, but not use ballot mechanisms to dictate electoral outcomes or favor or disfavor candidates on the basis of support for term limits).

Nor can Amendment 73 be defended as an exercise of the State’s general power over elections. The Constitution grants States authority to regulate the “Times, Places and Manner” of congressional elections, U.S. Const. art. I, § 4, cl. 1, but that power does not include authority to fix substantive qualifications for office. *Thornton*, 514 U.S. at 832-35; *Cook*, 531 U.S. at 523. Ballot-access rules are valid only when they serve election administration, not when they are designed to burden a disfavored class of candidates or to produce a substantive policy outcome. *Anderson v. Celebrezze*, 460 U.S. 780, 788-90 (1983); *Burdick v. Takushi*, 504 U.S. 428, 433-39 (1992). Amendment 73 is the latter: it is a term-limit qualification disguised as a ballot rule.

The Arkansas Supreme Court’s severability analysis likewise should be affirmed. Where a measure combines valid state-office term limits with an unconstitutional federal-office disqualification, severability depends on whether the valid portions would have been adopted independently. *Combs v. Glen Falls Insurance Co.*, 237 Ark. 745, 375 S.W.2d 809, 812 (1964). Here, the amendment was presented to voters as a single package. The unconstitutional federal restrictions were central to the public campaign for the measure, and there is no reliable basis to conclude the electorate would have enacted the state-office provisions standing alone. *Thornton*, 514 U.S. at 799-800, 837-38. At minimum, the reviewing court may not rewrite the people’s amendment to create a constitution the voters never approved. *Id.* at 837-38.

In short, the Constitution’s Qualifications Clauses are exclusive, and States may not add term limits for Members of Congress by any device. The judgment invalidating Amendment 73 should be affirmed.
