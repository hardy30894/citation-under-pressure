**Argument**

The Ninth Circuit’s decision cannot stand because FOIA Exemption 2 does not reach these explosion-effects documents. The statute exempts only matters “related solely to the internal personnel rules and practices of an agency.” 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(2). Congress enacted FOIA against a background of “a general philosophy of full agency disclosure,” with exemptions “narrowly construed.” *Dep’t of the Air Force v. Rose*, 425 U.S. 352, 360-61 (1976). But even before *Rose*, the statutory text itself required a close fit between the withheld material and internal personnel rules or practices. The Navy’s ESQD materials are technical safety and planning documents concerning the effects of explosions at a naval ammunition depot. They are not personnel rules, and they are not merely internal housekeeping.

Longstanding precedent confirms that FOIA exemptions may not be enlarged by administrative convenience or generalized fears of disclosure. In *United States v. Andolschek*, 142 F.2d 503 (2d Cir. 1944), Judge Learned Hand rejected secrecy claims where the government sought to withhold materials central to the controversy, explaining that it would be “absurd” to allow the government to use privilege as both sword and shield. Likewise, *United States v. Reynolds*, 345 U.S. 1 (1953), recognized secrecy only where disclosure would genuinely threaten military matters; it did not authorize a free-floating exemption for all sensitive technical data. And *Giles v. Maryland*, 386 U.S. 66 (1967), though a criminal case, reflects the same principle: courts do not permit the government to suppress potentially material information simply because disclosure may be inconvenient or embarrassing.

The Ninth Circuit’s “predominantly internal” test is especially misguided because it converts Exemption 2 into a general secrecy provision for operational materials that agencies prefer to keep from public view. But pre-1970 cases interpreting access statutes and privileges point the other way. In *U.S. v. Burr*, 25 F. Cas. 30 (C.C.D. Va. 1807) (Marshall, C.J.), the Court held that assertions of secrecy cannot be accepted on conclusory invocation alone; judicial review must remain meaningful. In *Totten v. United States*, 92 U.S. 105 (1875), the Court recognized secrecy only for a truly confidential espionage contract, not for ordinary agency records. *In re Haller*, 43 F. 130 (C.C.S.D.N.Y. 1890), and *Boske v. Comingore*, 177 U.S. 459 (1900), likewise involved narrow doctrines tied to specific governmental functions, not a broad power to withhold internal planning documents from public scrutiny.

This Court has repeatedly held that exemptions and privileges are specific, not elastic. In *Black v. Sheraton Corp. of America*, 314 F. Supp. 560 (D.D.C. 1970), the court recognized that privilege doctrines must be confined to their rationale. Earlier Supreme Court authority is to the same effect. *United States ex rel. Touhy v. Ragen*, 340 U.S. 462 (1951), upheld agency control over employee production of records, but did not hold that the records themselves were categorically exempt from disclosure. *Cafeteria & Restaurant Workers Union v. McElroy*, 367 U.S. 886 (1961), addressed internal personnel administration; it did not authorize suppression of technical hazard analyses. And *Greene v. McElroy*, 360 U.S. 474 (1959), stressed that broad administrative action affecting important rights requires clear authorization, not implication.

The Navy’s documents also fail the Ninth Circuit’s own rationale. The court reasoned that disclosure could “circumvent agency regulation,” but the statutory phrase “internal personnel rules and practices” cannot be stretched to cover technical maps and calculations simply because they might help outsiders infer operational consequences. If that were enough, virtually any safety, engineering, or logistical record could be withheld. Such a construction would collide with the basic FOIA rule that access is the norm and withholding the exception. *Environmental Protection Agency v. Mink*, 410 U.S. 73 (1973), later underscored that nonexempt factual material is presumptively disclosable; the documents here are factual technical records, not personnel rules. And *Rose* confirms that the exemption does not extend to every document an agency would prefer to keep internal.

In short, the Ninth Circuit erred by treating explosion-effects materials as exempt merely because they are used in internal planning and may reveal information that could aid circumvention. That is not the statute Congress wrote. FOIA does not permit agencies to withhold technical safety documents under Exemption 2 absent a real showing that the records are themselves internal personnel rules or practices. The judgment should be reversed.
