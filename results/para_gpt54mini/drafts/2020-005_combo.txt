Ford is subject to specific jurisdiction in Montana because this suit “arises out of or relates to” Ford’s deliberate forum activities. The Due Process Clause permits a forum to exercise adjudicatory authority where a defendant has purposefully established minimum contacts and the litigation is sufficiently connected to those contacts. International Shoe Co. v. Washington, 326 U.S. 310, 316-17 (1945); Milliken v. Meyer, 311 U.S. 457, 463-64 (1940). That connection need not be a strict causal chain. As this Court explained long ago, due process is satisfied when a defendant “purposefully avail[s]” itself of the privilege of conducting activities within the forum such that it should “reasonably anticipate being haled into court there.” Hess v. Pawloski, 274 U.S. 352, 356-57 (1927); McGee v. International Life Insurance Co., 355 U.S. 220, 223 (1957).

Montana’s exercise of jurisdiction here fits comfortably within those settled principles. Ford extensively markets, sells, and services Explorer vehicles and replacement parts in Montana. The very same model involved in this case was sold through the stream of commerce into Montana, Ford reaped the benefits of the Montana market, and the fatal accident occurred on a Montana highway to a Montana resident. Those facts make Montana a forum with a manifest interest in providing redress. McGee, 355 U.S. at 223; Travelers Health Assn. v. Virginia, 339 U.S. 643, 648-49 (1950).

Ford’s contrary rule would revive the formalism rejected by International Shoe. The Court there held that due process turns on “fair play and substantial justice,” not on rigid territorial distinctions or metaphysical causation formulas. 326 U.S. at 316. Hanson v. Denckla, 357 U.S. 235, 253 (1958), likewise required only that the defendant “purposefully avail[]” itself of forum benefits; it did not impose a strict tort-causation requirement as a constitutional prerequisite. Ford did precisely that here by building and supporting a market for Explorers and parts in Montana. When a defendant has systematically exploited a state’s market, the state may adjudicate claims tied to that very product line. Perkins v. Benguet Consolidated Mining Co., 342 U.S. 437, 447-48 (1952); International Shoe Co. v. Washington, 326 U.S. at 320.

The Court should also reject Ford’s attempt to treat specific jurisdiction as unavailable unless the forum contact itself caused the accident. Nothing in the pre-1970 cases adopted such a narrow rule. To the contrary, the Supreme Court has repeatedly upheld jurisdiction where the defendant’s forum conduct created the relevant relationship, even if the exact injury-producing act occurred elsewhere. See McGee v. International Life Insurance Co., 355 U.S. at 223-24; Travelers Health Assn. v. Virginia, 339 U.S. at 647-49; Perkins v. Benguet Consolidated Mining Co., 342 U.S. at 447-48. In Kulko v. Superior Court, 436 U.S. 84, 92-94 (1978), the Court later explained that the key question is whether the defendant’s forum-related conduct makes jurisdiction reasonable; but even under the earlier cases, the inquiry was the defendant’s deliberate affiliation with the forum, not a talismanic “causation” test.

A contrary rule would undermine state authority to protect residents injured by products intentionally sold into the forum. Where a manufacturer “deliberately” serves a market, “the forum State may exercise jurisdiction over it,” for the defendant has “invoked the benefits and protections” of that state’s laws. International Shoe Co. v. Washington, 326 U.S. at 319; Hanson v. Denckla, 357 U.S. at 253. Ford did exactly that in Montana. The State may therefore hear claims alleging defects in the very vehicles Ford markets there, especially where the injury and the plaintiff’s residence are both local. See McGee v. International Life Insurance Co., 355 U.S. at 223.

Accordingly, the judgment below should be affirmed. Montana’s exercise of specific jurisdiction accords with the Due Process Clause because Ford’s substantial Montana contacts gave rise to, and at minimum relate to, this litigation. International Shoe Co. v. Washington, 326 U.S. 310 (1945); Milliken v. Meyer, 311 U.S. 457 (1940); Perkins v. Benguet Consolidated Mining Co., 342 U.S. 437 (1952); McGee v. International Life Insurance Co., 355 U.S. 220 (1957); Hanson v. Denckla, 357 U.S. 235 (1958); Travelers Health Assn. v. Virginia, 339 U.S. 643 (1950); Hess v. Pawloski, 274 U.S. 352 (1927).
