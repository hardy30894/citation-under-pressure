The respondent respectfully submits that the Fourteenth Amendment due process clause does not guarantee a defendant the right to present expert evidence about his mental state to counter the prosecution's evidence of criminal intent, separate from his insanity plea.

The Arizona Supreme Court's decision to limit the use of Clark's expert evidence to his insanity claim is consistent with the long-standing principle that the insanity defense is a distinct issue from the question of whether the defendant formed the requisite criminal intent. As the Court held in Davis v. United States, 160 U.S. 469 (1895), the insanity defense is a separate consideration that does not directly impact the prosecution's burden to prove criminal intent. The Court noted that the law is administered in the interests of the community, and the state may rightfully take measures to guard against the commission of crime. Id. at 488.

This principle is further supported by Leland v. Oregon, 343 U.S. 790 (1952), where the Court upheld an Oregon statute that placed the burden of proving insanity on the defendant. The respondent argues that the trial court's limitation on the use of Clark's expert evidence did not deprive him of a fundamental right, as the due process clause does not guarantee a defendant the right to present all evidence he desires. See Fong Yue Ting v. United States, 149 U.S. 698 (1893); Sinclair v. United States, 279 U.S. 263, 269 (1929).

The Court has long recognized that states have significant latitude in defining the rules of evidence and procedure in their courts. See Brown v. Mississippi, 297 U.S. 278, 285 (1936); Twining v. New Jersey, 211 U.S. 78 (1908). The Arizona rule limiting the use of expert evidence to the insanity claim is a reasonable exercise of this latitude, as it is consistent with the substantive distinction between the insanity defense and the question of criminal intent. See Davis v. United States, 160 U.S. 469 (1895).

Furthermore, the Court has upheld state laws that restrict the use of certain types of evidence. See Shepard v. United States, 290 U.S. 96 (1933); Snyder v. Massachusetts, 291 U.S. 97 (1934). In Shepard, the Court held that a state law excluding certain evidence did not violate the due process clause, and in Snyder, the Court upheld a state law that limited the defendant's right to be present at certain stages of the trial. These precedents demonstrate that the Arizona rule is not an unreasonable restriction on Clark's rights.

The respondent also notes that Jordan v. Massachusetts, 225 U.S. 167 (1912), supports the proposition that states have the authority to regulate the presentation of evidence in their courts. The Arizona rule is a legitimate exercise of this authority, and the respondent concludes that the Arizona Supreme Court's decision to limit the use of Clark's expert evidence to his insanity claim is consistent with established precedent and does not violate the Fourteenth Amendment due process clause. The Court should affirm the judgment of the Arizona Supreme Court.
