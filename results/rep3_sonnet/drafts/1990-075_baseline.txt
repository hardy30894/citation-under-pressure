# ARGUMENT

## I. THE TEMPORARY SCHEDULING PROVISION SATISFIES THE INTELLIGIBLE PRINCIPLE TEST AND DOES NOT UNCONSTITUTIONALLY DELEGATE LEGISLATIVE POWER

Article I, Section 1 vests "[a]ll legislative Powers" in Congress, but this Court has long recognized that the Constitution does not require Congress to "solve *[e]very difficulty*" itself. *J.W. Hampton, Jr., & Co. v. United States*, 276 U.S. 394, 406 (1928). Rather, Congress may delegate authority to the Executive so long as it lays "down by legislative act an intelligible principle to which the person or body authorized to [act] is directed to conform." *Id.* at 409. This Court recently reaffirmed that "in our increasingly complex society, replete with ever changing and more technical problems, Congress simply cannot do its job absent an ability to delegate power under broad general directives." *Mistretta v. United States*, 488 U.S. 361, 372 (1989).

Section 811(h) satisfies *Hampton*'s intelligible-principle test with ease. The statute permits temporary scheduling only where necessary "to avoid an imminent hazard to the public safety," and requires the Attorney General to consider three enumerated statutory factors: the substance's history and current pattern of abuse, the scope and significance of that abuse, and the risk to public health. 21 U.S.C. § 811(h)(3) (incorporating § 811(c)(4)-(6)). This constrains executive discretion far more precisely than delegations this Court has upheld. In *Mistretta*, the Court sustained a delegation to the Sentencing Commission where Congress specified only general goals, a class of permissible outputs, and some limiting factors. 488 U.S. at 372-79. In *Yakus v. United States*, 321 U.S. 414 (1944), the Court upheld a wartime price-control delegation instructing the Administrator to fix prices that "will be generally fair and equitable," *id.* at 420, language considerably less confining than the multi-factor test Congress prescribed here. And in *Fahey v. Mallonee*, 332 U.S. 245 (1947), the Court upheld a delegation authorizing conservatorship of banks based on the existence of "well-known and generally acceptable standards" in the regulated field, *id.* at 250—a standard this Court found satisfied even without express statutory criteria.

The temporary-scheduling delegation is materially different from the delegations struck down in *Panama Refining Co. v. Ryan*, 293 U.S. 388 (1935), and *A.L.A. Schechter Poultry Corp. v. United States*, 295 U.S. 495 (1935). In *Panama Refining*, the statute "d[id] not state whether or in what circumstances or under what conditions" the President should act, and provided no "criterion to govern the President's course." 293 U.S. at 415. In *Schechter Poultry*, Congress had authorized the President to approve industry-drafted codes of "fair competition" without any definition of that standard, leaving "virtually unfettered" discretion to prescribe rules of conduct. 295 U.S. at 541-42. Section 811(h), by contrast, confines the Attorney General to a single, narrow purpose—avoiding imminent hazards to public safety—assessed through explicit statutory factors, for a strictly bounded duration of one year, extendable by only six months. 21 U.S.C. § 811(h)(2).

That the delegated authority concerns criminal penalties does not demand a more exacting standard. This Court in *Hampton* itself upheld a tariff delegation carrying criminal consequences under the ordinary intelligible-principle test, 276 U.S. at 411-12, and nothing in *Mistretta* suggests that delegations touching criminal sanctions receive heightened scrutiny; to the contrary, the Sentencing Commission's guidelines directly determine the length of criminal incarceration for every federal offense, yet the Court applied the conventional test. 488 U.S. at 378-79.

Additional safeguards further confirm the delegation's validity. The Attorney General must provide notice in the Federal Register and consult the Secretary of Health and Human Services before temporarily scheduling any substance. 21 U.S.C. § 811(h)(4). These procedural checks, combined with the substantive statutory factors and time limitation, provide precisely the sort of structure this Court found sufficient in *Mistretta*, where it emphasized that Congress had specified "the most relevant" characteristics guiding the delegate's exercise of discretion. 488 U.S. at 374-75.

## II. CONGRESS AUTHORIZED THE ATTORNEY GENERAL TO SUBDELEGATE TEMPORARY SCHEDULING AUTHORITY TO THE DEA

Separately, Congress expressly authorized subdelegation. Section 871(a) of the Controlled Substances Act provides that the Attorney General "may delegate any of his functions under this subchapter to any officer or employee of the Department of Justice." 21 U.S.C. § 871(a). This unambiguous statutory text forecloses petitioners' argument that subdelegation to the DEA was unauthorized.

This Court has recognized that subdelegation of executive authority to subordinate officers within an agency is a longstanding and unremarkable feature of administrative governance. Congress's express delegation clause here removes any doubt that such subdelegation was contemplated for the CSA's scheduling functions, including the temporary scheduling provisions added in 1984. Nothing in the text of § 811(h) or its legislative history excludes temporary scheduling from the general subdelegation authority of § 871(a); to the contrary, Congress added § 811(h) to the same statutory subchapter that § 871(a) already governed, and did so against the backdrop of the Attorney General's 1973 regulation subdelegating CSA functions generally to the DEA. The Attorney General's 1987 amendment to that regulation, assigning to the DEA Administrator the "[f]unctions vested in the Attorney General by the Comprehensive Drug Abuse Prevention and Control Act of 1970, as amended," 28 C.F.R. § 0.100(b) (1989), was therefore a proper and lawful exercise of subdelegation authority Congress had already granted.

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Court of Appeals should be affirmed.
