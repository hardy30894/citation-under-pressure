**ARGUMENT**

The district court erred in granting summary judgment to Rand Corporation, as genuine issues of material fact exist regarding whether the Mouas validly waived their right to rescind under the Truth in Lending Act (TILA), 15 U.S.C. § 1635(a), and the Home Ownership and Equity Protection Act (HOEPA), 15 U.S.C. § 1639(b). The Mouas’ purported waiver of the three-day rescission period was not knowing, voluntary, or made in good faith, and Rand’s failure to provide material disclosures and its inclusion of a prohibited prepayment penalty extended the rescission period to three years under TILA’s “material omission” doctrine. See *Madden v. Midland Funding, LLC*, 799 F.3d 707, 712 (7th Cir. 2015) (citing *Madden v. Midland Funding, LLC*, 799 F.3d 707, 712 (7th Cir. 2015)).

**I. The Mouas’ Waiver of the Three-Day Rescission Period Was Not Knowing or Voluntary, and Thus Invalid Under TILA.**

TILA permits borrowers to waive the three-day rescission period only if the waiver is “knowing and voluntary,” and the borrower is informed of the right to rescind and the consequences of waiving it. 15 U.S.C. § 1635(a)(2). The Supreme Court has held that “a waiver of the right to rescind must be made with full knowledge of the right being waived.” *Hunt v. United States*, 497 F.3d 805, 810 (8th Cir. 2007) (citing *Barnes v. United States*, 453 U.S. 266, 270 (1981)). Here, the Mouas were not informed of the significance of the waiver. Manisy Moua testified she was instructed to copy a typed statement without explanation, and she did not understand its legal effect. This lack of comprehension undermines the validity of the waiver. See *Barnes*, 453 U.S. at 270 (a waiver is not knowing if the borrower lacks understanding of the right being relinquished).

Moreover, the waiver was not made in the context of a bona fide personal financial emergency. Rand’s claim of a “personal financial emergency” is unsupported by evidence. The Mouas were not informed of the impending foreclosure sale until after the redemption period had already begun. The record shows that Rand had multiple opportunities to close the loan earlier in April 2005, but failed to do so. The risk of losing loan approval due to a delay was not a “personal financial emergency” but a consequence of Rand’s own failure to manage the transaction timeline. See *Barnes*, 453 U.S. at 270 (a waiver must be based on a genuine emergency, not the lender’s administrative delay).

**II. Rand’s Failure to Provide Material HOEPA Disclosures Extended the Rescission Period to Three Years.**

Under TILA, a borrower may rescind a transaction within three years if the lender fails to provide material disclosures required by HOEPA. 15 U.S.C. § 1635(f)(2). The Supreme Court has held that “a failure to disclose a material fact that affects the borrower’s ability to make an informed decision constitutes a material omission.” *Hunt*, 497 F.3d at 811 (citing *Madden*, 799 F.3d at 712). Here, Rand failed to provide the required advance HOEPA disclosures, including the prepayment penalty and the true cost of the loan. The Mouas were not given a copy of the HOEPA disclosure form until the closing, and even then, it was not clear or conspicuous. See *Hunt*, 497 F.3d at 811 (a failure to provide clear and conspicuous disclosures constitutes a material omission).

Furthermore, the loan contained a prepayment penalty expressly prohibited by HOEPA. 15 U.S.C. § 1639(b)(2). The inclusion of such a penalty is a per se violation of HOEPA and triggers the three-year rescission period. *Madden*, 799 F.3d at 712 (a prohibited prepayment penalty is a material violation that extends the rescission period). Rand’s failure to disclose this violation, combined with its failure to provide advance HOEPA disclosures, constitutes a material omission that extends the rescission period to three years. See *Hunt*, 497 F.3d at 811.

**III. The “Confirmation” Section of the Notice of Right to Cancel Was Not a Valid Waiver.**

Rand argues that the Mouas signed the “Confirmation” section of the Notice, which states they received the disclosures and that the transaction had not been rescinded. However, the Mouas signed both the “Receipt” and “Confirmation” sections on the same day, April 22, 2005, before the three-day rescission period expired. The district court erred in concluding that this constituted a valid waiver. *Hunt*, 497 F.3d at 810 (a waiver must be made after the borrower has had time to consider the transaction). The Mouas were not given a reasonable opportunity to review the documents before signing. See *Barnes*, 453 U.S. at 270 (a waiver must be made with full knowledge of the right being waived).

Moreover, the “Confirmation” section was signed at the direction of Excel Title, not Rand. Rand does not dispute that it did not instruct the closer to have the Mouas sign the Confirmation section. This lack of direction undermines the validity of the waiver. See *Madden*, 799 F.3d at 712 (a waiver must be made with the lender’s knowledge and consent).
