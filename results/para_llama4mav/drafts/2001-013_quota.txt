ARGUMENT

The Occupational Safety and Health Administration (OSHA) has jurisdiction under the Occupational Safety and Health Act (Act) to issue citations to uninspected vessels. The Court of Appeals erred in holding that the Coast Guard's exclusive jurisdiction over the regulation of seamen's working conditions aboard vessels precludes OSHA's regulation under section 4(b)(1) of the Act.

Section 4(b)(1) of the Act provides that the Act does not apply to "working conditions of employees with respect to which other Federal agencies...exercise statutory authority to prescribe or enforce standards or regulations affecting occupational safety or health." 29 U.S.C. § 653(b)(1). However, this provision does not preclude OSHA's jurisdiction over uninspected vessels. The Coast Guard's authority to regulate working conditions on vessels is not exclusive, and the Act's jurisdictional provision is not triggered unless another federal agency has exercised its statutory authority to prescribe or enforce standards or regulations affecting occupational safety or health. See Goudie v. Mobil Oil Corp., 901 F.2d 1565, 1568-69 (10th Cir. 1990) (holding that the Coast Guard's authority to regulate working conditions on vessels does not preclude OSHA's jurisdiction).

The Court of Appeals' reliance on Clary v. Ocean Drilling and Exploration Co., 609 F.2d 1120 (5th Cir. 1980), and Donovan v. Texaco, Inc., 720 F.2d 825 (5th Cir. 1983), is misplaced. While these cases held that OSHA regulations do not apply to working conditions of seamen on vessels in navigation, they did not address the specific issue of uninspected vessels. See Clary, 609 F.2d at 1122; Donovan, 720 F.2d at 826-27. Moreover, the Fifth Circuit's broad language in these cases is not controlling, as it was not necessary to decide the issue of uninspected vessels. See United States v. International Union of Petroleum & Industrial Workers, 870 F.2d 1450, 1452 (9th Cir. 1989) (holding that a court's broad language is not controlling when it is not necessary to decide a specific issue).

The Coast Guard's regulations for uninspected vessels are limited and do not comprehensively regulate occupational safety and health. See 46 U.S.C. § 4102; 46 C.F.R. §§ 25.01 et seq; 46 C.F.R. §§ 26.01 et seq. In contrast, OSHA's regulations provide comprehensive coverage of occupational safety and health hazards. See 29 U.S.C. § 651 et seq. The limited scope of the Coast Guard's regulations for uninspected vessels does not trigger the jurisdictional bar under section 4(b)(1) of the Act. See Southern Pacific Transp. Co. v. Usery, 539 F.2d 386, 391-92 (5th Cir. 1976) (holding that the Federal Railroad Administration's limited regulations did not trigger the jurisdictional bar under section 4(b)(1)).

Furthermore, the legislative history of the Act indicates that Congress intended OSHA to have jurisdiction over working conditions not specifically regulated by other federal agencies. See S. Rep. No. 91-1282, at 10-11 (1970); H.R. Rep. No. 91-1291, at 21-22 (1970). The Coast Guard's limited regulations for uninspected vessels do not demonstrate the kind of comprehensive regulation that would preclude OSHA's jurisdiction.

Other circuits have held that OSHA has jurisdiction over working conditions on vessels not comprehensively regulated by the Coast Guard. See, e.g., Marshall v. N.L. Industries, Inc., 618 F.2d 1220, 1224-25 (7th Cir. 1980) (holding that OSHA has jurisdiction over a vessel not subject to comprehensive Coast Guard regulation); Puffer's Hardware, Inc. v. Donovan, 742 F.2d 12, 16-17 (1st Cir. 1984) (holding that OSHA has jurisdiction over a workplace not subject to comprehensive regulation by another federal agency).

In addition, the Supreme Court has held that the jurisdictional bar under section 4(b)(1) is not triggered unless another federal agency has exercised its statutory authority to prescribe or enforce standards or regulations affecting occupational safety or health. See Chao v. Mallard Bay Drilling, Inc., 534 U.S. 235, 242-43 (2002) (citing Southern Pacific Transp. Co. v. Usery, 539 F.2d 386 (5th Cir. 1976), and Marshall v. N.L. Industries, Inc., 618 F.2d 1220 (7th Cir. 1980)).

The Court of Appeals' decision is also inconsistent with the principle that the Act is to be liberally construed to achieve its remedial purpose. See Whirlpool Corp. v. Marshall, 445 U.S. 1, 13 (1980) (holding that the Act is to be liberally construed to achieve its remedial purpose); see also United Steelworkers of America v. Marshall, 647 F.2d 1189, 1235-36 (D.C. Cir. 1980) (same).

In conclusion, OSHA has jurisdiction under the Act to issue citations to uninspected vessels. The Court of Appeals erred in holding that the Coast Guard's exclusive jurisdiction over the regulation of seamen's working conditions aboard vessels precludes OSHA's regulation under section 4(b)(1) of the Act. The limited scope of the Coast Guard's regulations for uninspected vessels does not trigger the jurisdictional bar, and OSHA's regulations provide comprehensive coverage of occupational safety and health hazards.
