**ARGUMENT**

**I. Under the law of nations as recognized by the United States, the United States bears ultimate responsibility for environmental hazards on Guam arising from military operations conducted during the period of U.S. sovereignty.**

The United States has long recognized that when territory comes under its sovereignty, it assumes responsibility for the consequences of its sovereign acts. In *United States v. Percheman*, 7 Pet. 51, 86 (1833), the Supreme Court held that when the United States acquires territory, it “takes the place of the former sovereign, and the laws of the former sovereign are not in force in the ceded territory, except so far as they are consistent with the Constitution of the United States and the principles of the American government.” This principle applies with particular force to environmental hazards resulting from military operations conducted under U.S. authority. In *Jones v. United States*, 137 U.S. 202, 212 (1890), the Court reaffirmed that the United States, as sovereign, is responsible for obligations arising from its governmental acts, including those undertaken in territories under its control. Because the Navy operated the Ordot Dump during periods of U.S. sovereignty over Guam, the United States cannot disclaim financial responsibility for the resulting environmental hazards.

**II. The Guam Organic Act of 1950 did not transfer liability for pre-existing environmental hazards to Guam, but merely transferred governmental authority.**

The Guam Organic Act, 64 Stat. 384 (1950), transferred administrative control to Guam’s civilian government but did not extinguish the United States’ liability for environmental hazards arising from prior military operations. In *United States v. Texas*, 143 U.S. 621, 645 (1892), the Supreme Court held that when Congress transfers governmental functions to a territory, it does not thereby relieve the United States of pre-existing obligations. Similarly, in *United States v. Kagama*, 118 U.S. 375, 383 (1886), the Court recognized that the United States retains responsibility for obligations incurred during the period of its sovereignty. The Ordot Dump was constructed and operated by the Navy during the period of U.S. sovereignty, and the transfer of governmental authority in 1950 did not shift liability for pre-existing environmental hazards to Guam.

**III. The United States cannot avoid liability by pointing to Guam’s subsequent operation of the Ordot Dump, as Guam’s actions were compelled by federal environmental directives.**

Even if Guam later operated the Ordot Dump, its actions were undertaken pursuant to federal environmental enforcement. In *United States v. San Francisco*, 310 U.S. 16, 29 (1940), the Supreme Court held that when a party acts under the compulsion of federal law, it does not assume liability for obligations that remain the responsibility of the United States. The EPA’s repeated orders to Guam to remediate the Ordot Dump, culminating in the 2004 consent decree, were federal actions that did not transfer liability to Guam. Thus, the United States remains financially responsible for the environmental hazards arising from the Ordot Dump.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Court of Appeals should be reversed, and the case remanded with instructions to hold the United States financially responsible for the environmental hazards arising from the Ordot Dump.
