ARGUMENT

I. Standard of Review

Whether transfers among Merco's affiliates constituted "bona fide indebtedness" running "directly" to Meruelo is a mixed question of law and fact. This Court reviews the Tax Court's factual findings for clear error but reviews de novo whether the Tax Court applied the correct legal standard in characterizing the transaction. See Commissioner v. Duberstein, 363 U.S. 278, 289–90 (1960) (holding that the "ultimate issue" in tax characterization cases, though largely factual, remains subject to correction where the trier of fact misapplies the governing legal standard).

II. Substance, Not the Form of Bookkeeping Entries, Governs Whether the Debt Ran Directly to Meruelo

The Tax Court erred by treating Merco's accounts-payable ledger as dispositive, rather than examining the economic substance of the transaction as a whole. It is axiomatic that "the incidence of taxation depends upon the substance of a transaction" and not upon the labels the parties or their accountants happen to apply. Commissioner v. Court Holding Co., 324 U.S. 331, 334 (1945). In Court Holding Co., the Supreme Court refused to permit a taxpayer to disguise a corporate sale by interposing a shareholder as nominal seller, explaining that "a sale by one person cannot be transformed for tax purposes into a sale by another by using the latter as a conduit through which to pass title." Id. That conduit principle applies with equal force here, where Meruelo's wholly and majority owned affiliates served merely as pass-through vehicles for capital that originated with, and was economically borne by, Meruelo himself.

This substance-over-form principle is not a one-way sword wielded only by the Commissioner. Where, as here, respecting mere paper form would defeat the real character of a transaction, courts have long refused "to exalt artifice above reality." Gregory v. Helvering, 293 U.S. 465, 470 (1935). Gregory teaches that a transaction must be evaluated according to what was actually done in economic fact, not according to the formal structure erected around it. Id. The Tax Court's mechanical reliance on Merco's internal accounts-payable entries—entries that Carreras himself testified were year-end computations designed to allocate net obligations—inverts Gregory's teaching by allowing bookkeeping artifice to override the economic reality that Meruelo's capital, not some abstract affiliate obligation, funded Merco's operations.

III. The Debt-Equity Inquiry Focuses on Economic Substance and Control, Not on the Formal Identity of the Nominal Creditor

Courts long have recognized that the question whether an advance constitutes bona fide debt turns on substance rather than formal labeling, particularly where the parties are related and the taxpayer exercises control over both ends of the transaction. See Fin Hay Realty Co. v. United States, 398 F.2d 694, 696 (3d Cir. 1968) (explaining that the many factors courts apply in the debt-equity inquiry are "only aids" to the ultimate question whether the transaction was, in substance, a loan). Where a common owner directs funds through affiliated entities that he controls, the formal identity of the transferring entity does not defeat a finding that the advance is, in substance, his own. See John Kelley Co. v. Commissioner, 326 U.S. 521, 530 (1946) (holding that instruments labeled otherwise may nonetheless constitute genuine indebtedness where the economic substance of the arrangement reflects a true creditor relationship). Similarly, in Gilbert v. Commissioner, 248 F.2d 399, 402 (2d Cir. 1957), the Second Circuit recognized that shareholder advances to a controlled corporation may be found to be loans notwithstanding the absence of contemporaneous formal notes, because the controlling shareholder's intent and the surrounding economic circumstances—not empty formalities—determine the character of the advance. The same reasoning compels the conclusion that Meruelo's advances, routed through affiliates he owned and controlled, were bona fide indebtedness running to him.

IV. Meruelo Made an Actual Economic Outlay Sufficient to Establish Basis

The Tax Court further erred in holding that Meruelo made no "actual economic outlay." An outlay is not confined to funds a shareholder personally disburses from his own checking account; it exists wherever the shareholder's capital is genuinely put at risk. See Raynor v. Commissioner, 50 T.C. 762, 770–71 (1968) (holding that basis requires an actual economic outlay by which the shareholder's own resources are placed at risk in the corporation); Perry v. Commissioner, 47 T.C. 159, 163 (1966) (same). Meruelo's ownership interests in Akoya and the other affiliates meant that when those entities transferred funds to Merco, Meruelo's own economic interest—not a stranger's—was diminished and placed at risk of loss, precisely the outlay Raynor and Perry require.

Nor does the corporate-separateness doctrine of Moline Properties, Inc. v. Commissioner, 319 U.S. 436 (1943), compel a contrary result. Moline Properties respects corporate identity "so long as the purpose for which it was created is the equivalent of business activity," id. at 439, but it does not forbid courts from tracing, for basis purposes, the ultimate economic source of capital that a controlling shareholder directs through his own affiliated entities. The Commissioner cannot invoke entity separateness to sever Meruelo from the very capital he supplied, while simultaneously ignoring that separateness when convenient to disallow his loss.

CONCLUSION

For these reasons, the Tax Court's judgment should be reversed.
