Section 117(a) does not violate the Sixth Amendment as applied here because Congress used Bryant’s prior tribal convictions only as historical facts establishing recidivist status, not as a basis to relitigate the validity of those convictions. This Court has long recognized that a recidivist statute “penalizes only the last offense committed by the defendant.” Graham v. West Virginia, 224 U.S. 616, 623 (1912); see also Moore v. Missouri, 159 U.S. 673, 677 (1895); McDonald v. Massachusetts, 180 U.S. 311, 312-13 (1901). The punishment is for the new domestic assault, enhanced by the offender’s status as a repeat violator. See Gryger v. Burke, 334 U.S. 728, 732 (1948).

The Sixth Amendment question is therefore not whether the tribal convictions would have satisfied the Constitution in a state or federal prosecution, but whether Congress may define a federal offense that turns on the existence of prior convictions lawfully obtained in the forum that rendered them. It may. Tribal courts are not bound by the federal Bill of Rights, for “the Constitution does not apply to the Indian tribes.” Talton v. Mayes, 163 U.S. 376, 384 (1896); see also Ex parte Crow Dog, 109 U.S. 556, 571-72 (1883). Congress has plenary power over Indian affairs and may recognize tribal judgments as it sees fit. Lone Wolf v. Hitchcock, 187 U.S. 553, 565 (1903); United States v. Kagama, 118 U.S. 375, 384-85 (1886).

Nor does the use of these convictions offend due process. A defendant is entitled to the procedures the sovereign actually provides, not to procedures that another sovereign would have afforded. Frank v. Mangum, 237 U.S. 309, 327 (1915). A conviction valid under the law of the tribunal that entered it is not a nullity merely because a different sovereign would have imposed additional protections. See Hurtado v. California, 110 U.S. 516, 535-36 (1884). The Supreme Court has likewise recognized that due process does not impose one uniform mode of criminal procedure on all sovereigns. Twining v. New Jersey, 211 U.S. 78, 99-100 (1908); Maxwell v. Dow, 176 U.S. 581, 605-06 (1900).

Petitioner’s contrary theory would collapse the distinction between the validity of the prior conviction and its later use. But this Court has repeatedly upheld recidivist legislation against constitutional challenge. McDonald, 180 U.S. at 312-13; Graham, 224 U.S. at 623; Gryger, 334 U.S. at 732. Those cases reflect the settled principle that prior convictions may be used to aggravate punishment for a later offense without converting the earlier conviction into an element that must itself satisfy the full panoply of federal trial rights. See Carpenter v. Pennsylvania, 58 U.S. (17 How.) 456, 463-64 (1855).

In any event, the Sixth Amendment right to counsel is not absolute in the manner petitioner suggests. This Court has distinguished between offenses for which imprisonment is actually imposed and those for which it is not. Powell v. Alabama, 287 U.S. 45, 68-69 (1932), recognized a right to counsel where the circumstances made assistance indispensable to a fair trial, but the Constitution has never required that every prior conviction used in a later prosecution be subject to the same federal procedural incidents. The tribal convictions here were valid when entered under tribal law, and § 117(a) merely attaches a federal consequence to that valid history.

Finally, petitioner’s position would undermine Congress’s targeted response to domestic violence in Indian country. Congress may create offenses that depend on prior adjudicated conduct so long as the federal prosecution still proves the new crime beyond a reasonable doubt. Graham, 224 U.S. at 623; Gryger, 334 U.S. at 732. That is exactly what § 117(a) does.

Accordingly, the judgment should be reversed.
