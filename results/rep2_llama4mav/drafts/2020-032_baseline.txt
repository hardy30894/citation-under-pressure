The Eleventh Circuit correctly held that a constitutional challenge to a school policy seeking nominal damages is rendered moot when the unconstitutional policy is revised during litigation, as the claim for nominal damages alone is insufficient to save the case from mootness.

The mootness doctrine is rooted in Article III's requirement that federal courts only decide live cases or controversies. Already, LLC v. Nike, Inc., 568 U.S. 85, 90-91 (2013). A case becomes moot when the issues presented are no longer "live" or the parties lack a legally cognizable interest in the outcome. Powell v. McCormack, 395 U.S. 486, 496 (1969). Here, GGC's revision of its "Freedom of Expression Policy" and removal of the challenged portion of its "Student Code of Conduct" rendered the claims for declaratory and injunctive relief moot, as there was no longer a live controversy regarding the superseded policies.

The Eleventh Circuit's decision in Flanigan's Enterprises, Inc. of Georgia v. City of Sandy Springs, 868 F.3d 1248 (11th Cir. 2017) (en banc), controls this case. In Flanigan's, this Court held that a prayer for nominal damages generally will not save an otherwise moot challenge to an allegedly unconstitutional policy or law. Id. at 1265-66. The Court reasoned that nominal damages are not sufficient to establish a concrete and particularized injury, as required by Article III. Id. at 1260-61.

Uzuegbunam and Bradford argue that their case is distinguishable from Flanigan's because it involves an as-applied challenge to the Prior Policies. However, this argument was not presented to the district court and is therefore not properly before this Court. Access Now, Inc. v. Southwest Airlines Co., 385 F.3d 1324, 1331 (11th Cir. 2004).

Furthermore, the district court did not abuse its discretion in denying Uzuegbunam and Bradford leave to amend their complaint. They failed to properly request leave to amend, instead embedding their request within their response to GGC's motion to dismiss. Cita Tr. Co. AG v. Fifth Third Bank, 879 F.3d 1151, 1157 (11th Cir. 2018). Moreover, they did not provide the district court with the substance of their proposed amendment, failing to specify what additional factual allegations they would have included to support their request for compensatory damages. Newton v. Duke Energy Fla., LLC, 895 F.3d 1270, 1277 (11th Cir. 2018).

In conclusion, the Eleventh Circuit correctly applied Flanigan's to hold that Uzuegbunam and Bradford's constitutional challenge to GGC's policies was rendered moot by the revision of those policies. The district court did not err in dismissing the case, and its judgment should be affirmed.
