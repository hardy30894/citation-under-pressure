### Argument

The District’s refusal to allow Lamb’s Chapel to use school premises for an after-hours film series violated the First Amendment. By opening its facilities for community use after hours, the District created a forum in which it could not exclude speech because of its religious viewpoint. The Constitution forbids that discrimination.

First, the School District’s property was not an enclave for exclusive governmental speech. When the government “by policy or by practice” opens property for expressive use, it may not discriminate against speech on the basis of content. *Perry Educ. Ass’n v. Perry Local Educators’ Ass’n*, 460 U.S. 37, 45-46 (1983) (describing forum principles grounded in earlier First Amendment cases). That rule derives from *Hague v. CIO*, 307 U.S. 496, 515-16 (1939), which recognized that streets and parks have “immemorially been held in trust for the use of the public” for assembly and discussion, and from *Schneider v. State*, 308 U.S. 147, 160 (1939), which held that expressive activity may not be suppressed simply because officials prefer order to speech. The Court repeated the same principle in *Jamison v. Texas*, 318 U.S. 413, 416 (1943), and *Marsh v. Alabama*, 326 U.S. 501, 506-09 (1946), confirming that government-controlled property used for public expression is subject to First Amendment limits.

Here, the District opened its premises for community use after school hours. Once it did so, it could not reserve access for secular messages while excluding a religious perspective. This Court has long held that the First Amendment protects against content discrimination in opened forums. *Cox v. Louisiana*, 379 U.S. 536, 551-55 (1965), invalidated selective suppression of peaceful expression based on its perceived viewpoint. So too in *Edwards v. South Carolina*, 372 U.S. 229, 235 (1963), the Court rejected punishment for expressive activity simply because officials disapproved of the message.

Second, Lamb’s Chapel’s proposed film series was protected speech on a matter of public concern: family values and child rearing. The First Amendment’s protection extends to discussion of social and moral questions as well as politics. *Cantwell v. Connecticut*, 310 U.S. 296, 310-11 (1940), held that religious advocacy is speech protected by the Free Exercise and Free Speech Clauses, and *Thomas v. Collins*, 323 U.S. 516, 537 (1945), emphasized that the right to speak on matters of public concern cannot be burdened by administrative discretion. Likewise, *Murdock v. Pennsylvania*, 319 U.S. 105, 111-12 (1943), recognized that religious dissemination is entitled to the same constitutional protection as other forms of advocacy.

The District’s response was not neutral. It did not deny use because the film series was too loud, too long, or too disruptive. It denied access because the films were “church related.” That is classic viewpoint discrimination. *Terminiello v. Chicago*, 337 U.S. 1, 4-5 (1949), held that speech may not be suppressed merely because it stirs dispute or challenges prevailing views. *West Virginia State Bd. of Educ. v. Barnette*, 319 U.S. 624, 642 (1943), remains the lodestar: no official may prescribe orthodoxy in matters of opinion. Excluding a film series because it presents family values from a Christian perspective is precisely the kind of orthodoxy the First Amendment forbids.

Nor can the District defend its policy as a valid exclusion from a nonpublic forum. Even where government property is not generally open, access restrictions must be reasonable and not a pretext for suppressing a disfavored view. *Marsh v. Alabama*, 326 U.S. at 509, and *Murdock*, 319 U.S. at 111-12, rejected schemes that burdened religious speakers while permitting comparable secular activity. The District allowed community use but singled out religious use for exclusion. That is not neutrality; it is censorship.

Finally, the Establishment Clause does not justify the exclusion. The Supreme Court has long distinguished between state sponsorship of religion and evenhanded access. *Everson v. Board of Educ.*, 330 U.S. 1, 17-18 (1947), held that government may not “favor one religion” over another or prefer religion over nonreligion, but it also explained that neutrality is the governing principle. Denying access because speech is religious does not avoid establishment; it creates it by preferring secular viewpoints over religious ones. *Cantwell*, 310 U.S. at 303-04, and *Everson*, 330 U.S. at 18, both reject that sort of discrimination.

The judgment should be reversed. The First Amendment does not permit a school district to open its facilities to the community and then close the door when the speaker’s viewpoint is religious.
