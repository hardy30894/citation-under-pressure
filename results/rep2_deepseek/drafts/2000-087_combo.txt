**ARGUMENT**

The Fifth Circuit’s construction of 8 U.S.C. § 1231(a)(6) to authorize indefinite detention of a resident alien after the 90-day removal period cannot be reconciled with this Court’s settled precedents. The statute must be read to avoid the serious constitutional questions that would arise if it were interpreted to permit limitless imprisonment.

**I. Indefinite Detention of a Resident Alien Violates Fundamental Due Process Protections.**

The Due Process Clause of the Fifth Amendment applies to all “persons” within the United States, including resident aliens. *Wong Wing v. United States*, 163 U.S. 228, 238 (1896). This Court has long held that the government may not imprison a resident alien without procedural safeguards that satisfy due process. *Id.* at 237. Unlike an alien stopped at the border, a resident alien like petitioner has “entered” the country and is entitled to the full protection of the Constitution. *The Japanese Immigrant Case (Yamataya v. Fisher)*, 189 U.S. 86, 100-01 (1903).

The Fifth Circuit’s holding that petitioner may be held indefinitely so long as “good faith efforts to effectuate . . . deportation continue” ignores this fundamental distinction. In *Shaughnessy v. United States ex rel. Mezei*, 345 U.S. 206 (1953), this Court upheld the detention of an excludable alien who had never effected entry into the United States. *Id.* at 212-13. But the Court expressly distinguished resident aliens, noting that “the alien’s presence within the country” triggers constitutional protections. *Id.* at 212. Petitioner, who has lived in the United States since 1956, is not an “excludable” alien at the border; he is a resident whose liberty cannot be forfeited without a showing that detention is necessary to effectuate removal.

**II. The Statute Must Be Construed to Avoid Serious Constitutional Doubts.**

This Court has repeatedly held that where a statute is ambiguous, it must be interpreted to avoid raising serious constitutional questions. *United States v. Delaware & Hudson Co.*, 213 U.S. 366, 408 (1909); *Crowell v. Benson*, 285 U.S. 22, 62 (1932). Section 1231(a)(6) does not explicitly authorize indefinite detention. It provides that certain aliens “may be detained beyond the removal period,” but it does not specify the duration of such detention. Read literally, the statute could permit detention for a day, a year, or a lifetime. This ambiguity requires the Court to adopt a construction that avoids constitutional infirmity.

The Fifth Circuit’s interpretation—that detention may continue as long as deportation is not “impossible”—would authorize imprisonment that is potentially permanent. This Court has never upheld the indefinite civil detention of a person who has been admitted to the country. In *Ludecke v. Watkins*, 335 U.S. 160 (1948), the Court upheld the detention of enemy aliens during wartime, but that case involved a declared war and a specific congressional authorization for detention. *Id.* at 164-65. No such exigency exists here. Petitioner is not an enemy alien; he is a lawful permanent resident who has committed crimes but has served his criminal sentence.

**III. The Fifth Circuit’s “Impossibility” Standard Imposes an Unconstitutional Burden on the Alien.**

The Fifth Circuit required petitioner to show that deportation is “impossible,” not merely unlikely or unforeseeable. This standard places an impossible burden on the alien, who cannot control whether a foreign country will accept him. In *Ng Fung Ho v. White*, 259 U.S. 276 (1922), this Court held that a resident alien facing deportation is entitled to a judicial determination of the facts supporting removal. *Id.* at 284-85. The Fifth Circuit’s standard effectively denies petitioner any meaningful opportunity to challenge his detention, because he can never prove a negative—that no country will ever accept him.

Moreover, the Fifth Circuit’s reliance on *Mezei* is misplaced. In *Mezei*, the alien was “assimilated” to the status of an alien at the border because he had never been admitted. 345 U.S. at 212. Petitioner, by contrast, was admitted as a resident alien and has lived in the United States for decades. The Court in *Mezei* expressly noted that “it is not disputed that the respondent is an alien” who had not “entered” the country. *Id.* at 210. Here, petitioner’s entry is undisputed.

**IV. The Government’s Interest in Detention Does Not Justify Indefinite Confinement.**

The government’s interest in ensuring an alien’s presence for removal is legitimate, but it does not justify indefinite detention when removal is not reasonably foreseeable. In *Carlson v. Landon*, 342 U.S. 524 (1952), this Court upheld the detention of aliens pending deportation, but only because the detention was “preliminary to a hearing” and “temporary in nature.” *Id.* at 538. The Court emphasized that “detention is not punitive” and must be “limited to the period necessary to effectuate removal.” *Id.* at 537. Here, petitioner has been detained for years after the removal period expired, with no end in sight. This is not temporary detention; it is permanent imprisonment.

The Fifth Circuit also relied on the government’s interest in preventing flight or danger to the community. But this Court has never held that a resident alien may be imprisoned indefinitely based solely on a risk of flight or dangerousness. In *United States v. Salerno*, 481 U.S. 739 (1987), the Court upheld pretrial detention only because it was “regulatory, not punitive,” and limited to “a specific period of time.” *Id.* at 747. The Court noted that “the Government’s regulatory interest in community safety can, in appropriate circumstances, outweigh an individual’s liberty interest.” *Id.* at 748. But *Salerno* involved criminal defendants facing trial, not resident aliens facing removal. And the detention there was limited to the period before trial, not indefinite.

**V. The Fifth Circuit’s Interpretation Would Render the Statute Unconstitutional.**

If Section 1231(a)(6) is read to authorize indefinite detention, it would violate the Due Process Clause. This Court has long recognized that “freedom from imprisonment—from government custody, detention, or other forms of physical restraint—lies at the heart of the liberty that Clause protects.” *Zadvydas v. Davis*, 533 U.S. 678, 690 (2001). The Fifth Circuit’s construction would permit the government to imprison a resident alien for life, even though he has served his criminal sentence and no country will accept him. Such a result is “a flagrant violation of the fundamental principles of due process.” *Wong Wing*, 163 U.S. at 237.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Fifth Circuit should be reversed, and the case remanded with instructions to order petitioner’s release under appropriate conditions of supervision.
