**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Board’s Polling Standard Is Irrational and Inconsistent with the National Labor Relations Act.**

The National Labor Relations Act encourages the free and fair determination of employee union preferences. *NLRB v. Curtin Matheson Scientific, Inc.,* 494 U.S. 775, 786 (1990). The Board’s rule—that an employer may poll employees only when it already possesses objective evidence sufficient to justify withdrawing recognition—frustrates that purpose. The Board itself acknowledges that polling is “potentially, if not inherently, both disruptive of the collective-bargaining relationship . . . and also unsettling to the employees involved.” *Texas Petrochemicals Corp.,* 296 N.L.R.B. 1057, 1061 (1989). Yet the Board’s standard forces employers to choose between two equally unpalatable options: withdraw recognition without any verification, risking an unfair labor practice; or seek a Board-conducted RM election, which the Board simultaneously declares the “preferred method” but then subjects to the same demanding evidentiary threshold. *Id.* at 1061. This makes polling a “useless and unnecessary tool.” *Mingtree Restaurant, Inc. v. NLRB,* 736 F.2d 1295, 1298 (9th Cir. 1984).

Three circuits have correctly rejected the Board’s approach. Those courts hold that an employer may poll employees when it has “substantial, objective evidence of a loss of union support, even if that evidence is insufficient by itself to justify withdrawal.” *A.W. Thompson, Inc. v. NLRB,* 651 F.2d 1141, 1145 (5th Cir. 1981); *Thomas Industries, Inc. v. NLRB,* 687 F.2d 863, 867 (6th Cir. 1982); *Mingtree,* 736 F.2d at 1299. That standard respects the Act’s balance: it prevents coercive polling while allowing employers to resolve legitimate doubts without unilaterally ending bargaining. The Board’s contrary rule is not entitled to deference. A rule that is “arbitrary, capricious, or manifestly contrary to the statute” must be set aside. *Chevron U.S.A. Inc. v. Natural Resources Defense Council, Inc.,* 467 U.S. 837, 844 (1984). The Board’s standard is precisely that.

**II. The Board’s Factual Finding That Allentown Lacked Good-Faith Reasonable Doubt Is Not Supported by Substantial Evidence.**

Even under the Board’s own flawed standard, its factual determination fails. Substantial evidence exists only when “a reasonable mind might accept as adequate to support a conclusion.” *Universal Camera Corp. v. NLRB,* 340 U.S. 474, 477 (1951). Here, the record is replete with objective indications of lost union support.

First, seven employees expressly repudiated the union. The Board improperly discounted three—Pete McArthur, Dennis Wehr, and Randy Zoltack—as not in the unit on January 25, 1991. But the Board’s use of that date was arbitrary. The company’s letter was sent only after the union’s demand; the company’s doubt arose earlier. Moreover, the Board excluded statements from Mike Ridgick and Dennis Marsh. Ridgick said “there was no need for a union.” The ALJ found this “at best equivocal,” but the Board’s own precedent holds that job-interview statements are not inherently unreliable when the employer has not engaged in misconduct. *Middleboro Fire Apparatus, Inc.,* 234 N.L.R.B. 888, 894 (1978), *enforced,* 590 F.2d 4 (5th Cir. 1978). Marsh’s complaint about paying $35 in dues for “not getting his money’s worth” is plainly a statement of opposition to continued representation. *Accord Wagon Wheel Bowl, Inc. v. NLRB,* 47 F.3d 332, 335-36 (9th Cir. 1995).

Second, the Board disregarded the hearsay testimony of Kermit Bloch and Ron Mohr. Bloch reported that the entire night shift—five or six employees—did not want the union. Mohr, the union steward, stated that “if a vote was taken, the union would lose.” The Board’s refusal to credit these statements because the declarants did not testify ignores the reality that employers often rely on such reports. The Board’s skepticism is not a permissible substitute for contrary evidence. *See Thomas Industries,* 687 F.2d at 868 (reversing Board where “the record contains no evidence contradicting the Company’s evidence of loss of union support”).

In total, the evidence showed that at least 12 of 32 employees—well over one-third—opposed the union. The Board’s conclusion that this was insufficient to raise a reasonable doubt is arbitrary. The Act does not require an employer to verify every employee’s view through testimony. *Curtin Matheson,* 494 U.S. at 788. The Board failed to apply the “substantial evidence” standard faithfully. Its order should be reversed.
