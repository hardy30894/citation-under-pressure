ARGUMENT

I. Section 241(a)(5) Does Not Apply to Reentries Effected Before Its Enactment.

The reinstatement provision operates only upon reentries occurring after its effective date. Statutes must be construed to operate prospectively unless the text admits of no other meaning. *United States v. Heth*, 3 Cranch 399, 413 (1806) (“words of a statute, if of doubtful import, ought never to be construed to have a retrospective operation”). The same presumption appears in *Reynolds v. McArthur*, 27 U.S. (2 Pet.) 417, 434 (1829), where the Court refused to give a land statute retroactive effect absent “clear and positive” language. Nothing in the 1996 amendment supplies such language with respect to reentries completed years earlier.

II. Application to Pre-1997 Reentries Is Impermissibly Retroactive.

Even if the text were read to reach earlier reentries, the statute cannot constitutionally attach new disabilities to conduct completed before its passage. *Calder v. Bull*, 3 Dall. 386, 390 (1798), condemned as legislative overreach any law that “changes the legal consequences of acts committed before its passage.” The same principle was applied to civil enactments in *Society for the Propagation of the Gospel in Foreign Parts v. Wheeler*, 22 F. Cas. 756, 767 (C.C.D.N.H. 1814) (Story, J.), which held that a statute “ought not to receive a construction which would give it a retrospective operation, unless the words are so clear and positive as to leave no room for doubt.” Petitioner’s 1982 reentry and subsequent residence created settled expectations under then-existing law; the later statute cannot lawfully divest those expectations.

III. The Denial of All Forms of Relief Confirms the Impropriety of Retroactive Application.

The provision’s bar on “any relief” under the INA imposes a new and severe consequence upon a completed act. *Fletcher v. Peck*, 6 Cranch 87, 135 (1810), recognized that a law altering rights already vested “is a violation of the first principles of the social compact.” Because the statute supplies no express command that it reach pre-enactment reentries, and because such an application would work precisely the sort of new disability forbidden by *Calder* and *Fletcher*, the Tenth Circuit’s judgment must be reversed.
