**ARGUMENT**

**I. Genuine Issues of Material Fact Preclude Summary Judgment on Whether the Mouas Knowingly and Voluntarily Relinquished Their Statutory Rescission Rights.**

A movant for summary judgment bears the burden of demonstrating the absence of a genuine dispute, and "all doubts as to the existence of a genuine issue as to a material fact must be resolved against the party moving for summary judgment." *Cities Service Oil Co. v. Dunlap*, 308 U.S. 208, 210 (1939). In assessing the record, a court "must be viewed in the light most favorable to the party opposing the motion," and every permissible inference drawn from the underlying facts must run in that party's favor. *United States v. Diebold, Inc.*, 369 U.S. 654, 655 (1962). Where, as here, the case turns on a party's state of mind—whether the Mouas *intentionally* waived rights they did not understand—summary disposition is particularly disfavored, because "summary procedures should be used sparingly" in cases "where motive and intent play leading roles." *Poller v. Columbia Broadcasting System, Inc.*, 368 U.S. 464, 473 (1962). So too, credibility disputes of the sort presented by Manisy Moua's account of the closing cannot be resolved on paper, for "[t]rial by affidavit is no substitute for trial by jury which so long has been the right in cases of this type." *Sartor v. Arkansas Natural Gas Corp.*, 321 U.S. 620, 627 (1944).

These principles control here. Waiver is not a formality; it is "ordinarily an intentional relinquishment or abandonment of a known right or privilege." *Johnson v. Zerbst*, 304 U.S. 458, 464 (1938). Manisy testified she was directed to copy, in her own hand, a typed statement purporting to waive the three-day HOEPA advance-disclosure period, and that she "did not understand the statement and did not know why she was asked to copy it." That testimony directly controverts any finding that the waiver was "intentional" within the meaning of *Zerbst*. Likewise, the Mouas' signatures on the Confirmation section—dated the very day of closing, before the three-day rescission period had even begun to run—raise, at minimum, a jury question as to whether they knowingly certified that the period had already elapsed. Rand's own submissions concede the Confirmation was signed prematurely and attempt to shift responsibility to the title company, which only underscores that the circumstances surrounding execution are genuinely disputed. Under *Diebold* and *Poller*, that dispute belongs to the trier of fact, not to summary judgment.

**II. Rand, as the Drafter of a Take-It-or-Leave-It Loan Package Presented to Financially Desperate, Unsophisticated Borrowers, Cannot Claim the Benefit of Ambiguous or Materially Deficient Disclosures.**

The Mouas signed these documents while facing the imminent expiration of their redemption period and the loss of their home. Such circumstances are the paradigm of a contract offered "on a take it or leave it basis," where there exists "an absence of meaningful choice on the part of one of the parties together with contract terms which are unreasonably favorable to the other party." *Williams v. Walker-Thomas Furniture Co.*, 350 F.2d 445, 449 (D.C. Cir. 1965). Rand's own account confirms the coercive timeline: it asserts the Mouas were told they had to sign that day or risk "losing loan approval" altogether, with only forty-one days remaining before the redemption period expired. That is not the voluntary, informed choice the waiver doctrine demands.

Nor can Rand escape responsibility for the Mouas' confusion by characterizing the typed waiver statement as self-explanatory. It is well established that "the fact that a false [or unexplained] statement may be obviously [clear] to those who are trained and experienced does not change its character, nor take away its power to deceive others less experienced. There is no duty resting upon a citizen to suspect the honesty of those with whom he transacts business." *FTC v. Standard Education Society*, 302 U.S. 112, 116 (1937). The Mouas, unsophisticated borrowers dealing with a lender's chosen closing agent, were entitled to rely on the accuracy and clarity of the documents placed before them—not to bear the risk of Rand's ambiguous or incomplete disclosures.

That risk allocation is reinforced by the remedial character of TILA and HOEPA themselves. Consumer-protection statutes of this kind must be "construed not technically and restrictively, but flexibly to effectuate [their] remedial purposes." *Tcherepnin v. Knight*, 389 U.S. 332, 336 (1967). Congress extended the ordinary three-day rescission window to three years precisely to protect borrowers from lenders who fail to make the disclosures TILA and HOEPA require, or who embed prohibited terms such as prepayment penalties in the loan instrument. Any doubt as to whether Rand's disclosures were adequate, or whether the prepayment penalty is prohibited, must be resolved in favor of the borrowers whom the statute was designed to shield—not in favor of the lender who drafted the documents and controlled the closing process.

**CONCLUSION**

For these reasons, the district court's grant of summary judgment should be reversed and the case remanded for trial on the disputed factual issues of waiver, disclosure adequacy, and the enforceability of the prepayment penalty.
