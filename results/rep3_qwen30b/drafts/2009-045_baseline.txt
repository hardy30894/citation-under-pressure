**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a reasonable attorney’s fee award under a federal fee-shifting statute may ever be enhanced based solely on the quality of performance and results obtained—must be answered in the negative. The Supreme Court has consistently held that the lodestar method, which calculates fees by multiplying hours reasonably expended by a reasonable hourly rate, is presumed to reflect a “reasonable” fee under statutes like 42 U.S.C. § 1988. *Blum v. Stenson*, 465 U.S. 886, 897, 104 S.Ct. 1541, 1549 (1984). Any deviation from that lodestar—particularly an enhancement based on “quality of representation” or “results obtained”—must be justified by specific, record-based evidence of exceptional circumstances, and even then, such enhancements are permitted only in the rarest of cases.

The Eleventh Circuit’s decision in this case, and the district court’s reliance on the judge’s own subjective assessment of counsel’s “stellar performance” and the “comprehensiveness” of the injunction, directly contravenes this settled precedent. The district court did not base its $4.5 million enhancement on any objective, record-supported evidence of superior performance or exceptional results. Instead, it relied on the judge’s personal observations—gleaned from 58 years of legal experience and familiarity with Atlanta’s legal market—and made comparisons to “other cases” that were never identified or described. *Kenny A. v. Perdue*, 454 F. Supp. 2d 1260, 1286 (N.D. Ga. 2006). This is not a reviewable finding; it is judicial self-testimony, uncorroborated by evidence, and thus violates due process.

The Supreme Court has made clear that enhancements based on quality or results are not automatic, nor are they permissible simply because a court believes counsel performed well. *Blum*, 465 U.S. at 899, 104 S.Ct. at 1549. The Court emphasized that “the outcome would have been different had the plaintiffs’ attorneys offered specific evidence to demonstrate that an enhancement was necessary to reflect counsel’s performance.” Id. Here, no such evidence was presented. The district court did not identify any particular case in which counsel’s performance exceeded the norm, nor did it compare the hours expended or the complexity of the work to other cases. It did not explain how the results achieved were “exceptional” in a way that justified a multiplier. Instead, it simply declared that the quality of representation “far exceeded what could reasonably be expected” and that the injunction was “comprehensive.” These are conclusory statements, not findings supported by the record.

The Court’s decision in *Pennsylvania v. Delaware Valley Citizens’ Council for Clean Air*, 478 U.S. 546, 106 S.Ct. 3088 (1986), reaffirmed *Blum* and made clear that upward adjustments to the lodestar are permissible only in “rare” and “exceptional” cases, and only when supported by “specific evidence” and “detailed findings.” *Id.* at 565, 106 S.Ct. at 3098. The Court did not overrule *Blum*, nor did it suggest that quality or results could serve as independent bases for enhancement. On the contrary, it reiterated that the lodestar “includes most, if not all, of the relevant factors constituting a ‘reasonable’ attorney’s fee,” and that “it is unnecessary to enhance the fee for superior performance in order to serve the statutory purpose of enabling plaintiffs to secure legal assistance.” *Id.* at 566, 106 S.Ct. at 3098 (emphasis added). This language forecloses the notion that quality or results can justify an enhancement when the lodestar already reflects a reasonable rate for the work performed.

The Eleventh Circuit’s reliance on *NAACP v. Evergreen*, 812 F.2d 1332 (11th Cir. 1987), and *Norman v. Housing Auth. of Montgomery*, 836 F.2d 1292 (11th Cir. 1988), is misplaced. Those cases did not hold that enhancements based on quality or results are mandatory or even permissible in the absence of specific evidence. *NAACP* vacated a fee award for failure to adequately explain the denial of an enhancement, not to affirm one. *Norman* reversed because the district court applied the wrong standard in evaluating results, not because it approved enhancements based on performance. Neither case held that a district court may enhance a fee based on a judge’s personal opinion of counsel’s excellence.

The dissenting opinion in this case correctly identifies the fatal flaw: the district court’s enhancement was based not on evidence, but on the judge’s own testimony. This is not reviewable, and it violates due process. A party is entitled to notice and an opportunity to respond to evidence used against it. *See Mathews v. Eldridge*, 424 U.S. 319, 335, 96 S.Ct. 893, 903 (1976). The defendants were not given notice that the judge would rely on his own subjective impressions, nor were they given a chance to challenge them. The enhancement, therefore, was not only an abuse of discretion—it was a denial of due process.

The Supreme Court has repeatedly warned against allowing judges to “testify” in fee determinations. *Blum*, 465 U.S. at 899, 104 S.Ct. at 1549. The lodestar is not a discretionary figure to be adjusted at the judge’s whim. It is a calculation based on objective market rates and documented hours. When a court departs from that calculation, it must do so with clear, record-based justification. Here, there was none.

For these reasons, the judgment of the Eleventh Circuit must be reversed. The district court abused its discretion by enhancing the lodestar based on the judge’s personal, unreviewable assessment of counsel’s performance and the results obtained. Such enhancements are not permitted under *Blum* or *Delaware Valley I*, and they are particularly impermissible when unsupported by specific evidence. The lodestar, properly calculated, is the benchmark. Any enhancement beyond it must be justified by exceptional circumstances—and this case does not present them.
