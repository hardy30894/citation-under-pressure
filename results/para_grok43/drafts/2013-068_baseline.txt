The First Circuit correctly concluded that Mass. Gen. Laws ch. 266, § 120E½ is a valid time-place-manner regulation. A restriction on speech in a traditional public forum is constitutional if it is “justified without reference to the content of the regulated speech,” “narrowly tailored to serve a significant governmental interest,” and “leave[s] open ample alternative channels for communication.” Ward v. Rock Against Racism, 491 U.S. 781, 791 (1989) (quoting Clark v. Community for Creative Non-Violence, 468 U.S. 288, 293 (1984)).

The Act satisfies each prong. Its fixed buffer zones apply equally to all speakers and are justified by the State’s interest in “ensuring public safety and order, promoting the free flow of traffic on streets and sidewalks, and protecting a woman’s freedom to seek pregnancy-related services.” McGuire v. Reilly (McGuire I), 260 F.3d 36, 43 (1st Cir. 2001) (quoting Schenck v. Pro-Choice Network of Western New York, 519 U.S. 357, 372 (1997)). These interests are “significant” and “unrelated to the content of expression.” Ward, 491 U.S. at 791.

The Act is narrowly tailored. It “promotes a substantial government interest that would be achieved less effectively absent the regulation” and does not “burden substantially more speech than is necessary.” Ward, 491 U.S. at 799. The thirty-five-foot zones are “reasonable” in light of the record of congestion and harassment at clinic entrances. Hill v. Colorado, 530 U.S. 703, 727 (2000). Exemptions for clinic employees, patients, and passersby do not render the law content-based; they “merely facilitate the Act’s purpose.” McGuire I, 260 F.3d at 47.

The Act leaves open ample alternative channels. Petitioners may “speak at a normal conversational distance,” display signs, distribute literature, and pray outside the zones. Hill, 530 U.S. at 726-27. The district court’s factual findings confirm that these avenues remain effective. McCullen v. Coakley (McCullen III), 844 F. Supp. 2d 206, 213-25 (D. Mass. 2012).

Hill v. Colorado directly controls. The Colorado statute upheld there imposed an eight-foot floating buffer within one hundred feet of clinic entrances. 530 U.S. at 707. The Massachusetts fixed zones are “even more protective of speech.” McCullen v. Coakley (McCullen I), 571 F.3d 167, 180 (1st Cir. 2009). Principles of stare decisis therefore require adherence to Hill absent “special justification.” Arizona v. Rumsey, 467 U.S. 203, 212 (1984). No such justification exists; the decision has not proved “unworkable” and has generated no “inconsistent precedents.” Planned Parenthood of Southeastern Pennsylvania v. Casey, 505 U.S. 833, 854-55 (1992).

The law-of-the-case doctrine bars relitigation of facial validity. United States v. Matthews, 643 F.3d 9, 13 (1st Cir. 2011). Petitioners’ reliance on Sorrell v. IMS Health Inc., 564 U.S. 552 (2011), and Citizens United v. FEC, 558 U.S. 310 (2010), is misplaced; those decisions address speaker- or content-based restrictions in dissimilar contexts and do not disturb Hill’s holding on clinic buffer zones.

The judgment should be affirmed.
