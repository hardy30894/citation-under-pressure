The Sixth Circuit's decision in this case is consistent with established Supreme Court precedent. In Coleman v. Thompson, 501 U.S. 722 (1991), the Court held that a federal habeas court may not review a state prisoner's procedurally defaulted claim unless the prisoner can demonstrate cause for the default and prejudice resulting from it. Id. at 750. The Court further explained that "cause" is "something external to the petitioner, something that cannot fairly be attributed to him." Id. at 753 (quoting Murray v. Carrier, 477 U.S. 478, 488 (1986)).

In this case, the Court of Appeals correctly held that Carpenter's ineffective-assistance-of-counsel claim can serve as cause to excuse the procedural default of his sufficiency-of-the-evidence claim, regardless of whether the ineffective-assistance-of-counsel claim itself was procedurally defaulted. As the Court noted in Martinez v. Ryan, 566 U.S. 1 (2012), a "substantial" claim of ineffective assistance of trial counsel can be cause to excuse the procedural default of another claim. Id. at 14. The same principle applies to ineffective assistance of appellate counsel. See Davila v. Davis, 137 S. Ct. 2058, 2065 (2017) (explaining that the Martinez rule is limited to ineffective assistance of trial counsel, but suggesting that other types of ineffective assistance claims may still serve as cause).

Moreover, the Court has long recognized that ineffective assistance of counsel can be cause to excuse a procedural default. See Murray v. Carrier, 477 U.S. 478, 488 (1986) ("[C]onstitutionally ineffective assistance of counsel ... is cause"). The fact that Carpenter's ineffective-assistance-of-counsel claim was itself procedurally defaulted does not preclude it from serving as cause. As the Sixth Circuit correctly noted, the Supreme Court has never held that a claim of ineffective assistance of counsel must be exhausted or not procedurally defaulted in order to serve as cause.

The Court should affirm the judgment of the Court of Appeals. Carpenter's ineffective-assistance-of-counsel claim can serve as cause to excuse the procedural default of his sufficiency-of-the-evidence claim, and the District Court's finding of prejudice from counsel's failure to raise the sufficiency claim on direct appeal is supported by the record. The Sixth Circuit's decision is consistent with the Supreme Court's precedent on procedural default and ineffective assistance of counsel.
