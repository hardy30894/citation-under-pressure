The National Labor Relations Board's (NLRB) "good-faith reasonable doubt" standard for polling employees on union support is not rational and consistent with the National Labor Relations Act (NLRA). Furthermore, the NLRB's factual determination that Allentown Mack Sales, Inc. lacked such doubt is not supported by substantial evidence in the record.

The NLRA grants the NLRB the primary responsibility for developing and applying national labor policy, and its decisions are entitled to deference. NLRB v. Curtin Matheson Scientific, Inc., 494 U.S. 775, 786 (1990). However, the NLRB's "good-faith reasonable doubt" standard for polling employees is too strict and renders employer polls useless. Three courts of appeals have rejected this standard, holding that an employer may poll its employees if it has "substantial, objective evidence of a loss of union support." Mingtree Restaurant, Inc. v. NLRB, 736 F.2d 1295, 1297 (9th Cir. 1984); Thomas Indus., Inc. v. NLRB, 687 F.2d 863, 867 (6th Cir. 1982); A.W. Thompson, Inc. v. NLRB, 651 F.2d 1144, 1145 (5th Cir. 1981).

The NLRB's justification for its standard is that polling is "potentially disruptive and unsettling." Texas Petrochemicals Corp., 296 N.L.R.B. 1057, 1061-62 (1989). However, this concern is mitigated when an employer follows the procedural safeguards set out in Struksnes Constr. Co., 165 N.L.R.B. 1062 (1967). Allentown Mack Sales, Inc. followed these safeguards, and its poll was conducted in a fair and objective manner.

The NLRB's factual determination that Allentown Mack Sales, Inc. lacked a good-faith reasonable doubt about the union's majority status is not supported by substantial evidence. The record shows that several employees expressed anti-union sentiments, and the company's poll results indicated that a majority of employees did not support the union. The NLRB's decision to discount certain employee statements as unreliable is not supported by precedent. For example, the NLRB discounted the statements of employees who expressed anti-union sentiments during job interviews, citing Middleboro Fire Apparatus, Inc., 234 N.L.R.B. 888 (1978). However, this precedent is distinguishable, as the employer in Middleboro Fire Apparatus, Inc. had indicated that there would be no union during the job interview.

In contrast, the Fifth, Sixth, and Ninth Circuits have held that an employer's good-faith doubt about a union's majority status can be based on substantial, objective evidence, even if that evidence is not sufficient to justify withdrawal of recognition. A.W. Thompson, Inc., 651 F.2d at 1145; Mingtree Restaurant, Inc., 736 F.2d at 1299; Thomas Indus., Inc., 687 F.2d at 867. The evidence presented by Allentown Mack Sales, Inc. meets this standard, and the NLRB's decision to the contrary is arbitrary and capricious.

The Supreme Court has held that a successor employer inherits the bargaining obligation of its predecessor. Fall River Dyeing & Finishing Corp. v. NLRB, 482 U.S. 27, 36-41 (1987). However, the presumption of continuing majority status is weaker when the bargaining unit contains less than all of the previously certified employee group. In this case, the emerging bargaining unit at Allentown Mack Sales, Inc. had never been literally sampled for majority support, and the factual underpinnings for the presumption of continuing status are therefore weaker.

In conclusion, the NLRB's "good-faith reasonable doubt" standard for polling employees is too strict and not supported by the NLRA. The NLRB's factual determination that Allentown Mack Sales, Inc. lacked such doubt is not supported by substantial evidence, and the company's poll was conducted in a fair and objective manner. The Court should reverse the NLRB's decision and hold that Allentown Mack Sales, Inc. did not commit an unfair labor practice.
