# ARGUMENT

**Due Process Does Not Require Arizona to Permit Mental-Disease Evidence to Negate Mens Rea Outside the Insanity Defense**

## I. States Possess Broad Authority to Define the Insanity Defense and to Channel Evidence of Mental Abnormality Accordingly

This Court has long recognized that the definition of criminal responsibility, including the role that evidence of mental disease plays within it, is primarily a matter of state concern. In *Leland v. Oregon*, 343 U.S. 790 (1952), the Court rejected a due process challenge to Oregon's rule requiring a defendant to prove insanity beyond a reasonable doubt, holding that the choice of a test for criminal responsibility, and the allocation of the burden of proving it, does not offend the Fourteenth Amendment. The Court explained that questions concerning the proper legal significance of psychiatric evidence remain the subject of continuing medical and legal disagreement, and that such disagreement counsels against constitutionalizing any single approach. *Id.* at 800-01. If a State may constitutionally confine the *ultimate* question of criminal responsibility to a particular test and burden, it follows that a State may likewise confine the *use* of psychiatric evidence to that same defense, rather than permitting it to operate independently as a means of negating the mental element the prosecution must otherwise prove.

Petitioner's claim—that due process guarantees him a *separate* avenue for introducing mental-disease evidence to negate intent, apart from the insanity defense—asks this Court to constitutionalize a particular evidentiary theory of "diminished capacity" that has never commanded uniform acceptance. *Leland* forecloses that result.

## II. The Common-Law and Federal Practice of Confining Mental-Abnormality Evidence to the Insanity Defense Satisfies Due Process

This Court's decision in *Fisher v. United States*, 328 U.S. 463 (1946), is directly on point. There, a defendant charged with first-degree murder sought to introduce psychiatric evidence of a mental deficiency short of insanity to negate the premeditation and deliberation required for that offense—precisely the theory Petitioner advances here. The trial court refused to instruct the jury that it could consider such evidence to reduce the offense, confining psychiatric evidence to the insanity defense under the prevailing rule in the District of Columbia. This Court affirmed, holding that "the rule remains the settled law of this jurisdiction" that partial mental responsibility is not a recognized basis for reducing the grade of an offense, and that the refusal to depart from that rule was not error. *Id.* at 466. The Court declined to require the District of Columbia courts to adopt a "partial responsibility" doctrine, notwithstanding the defendant's argument that exclusion of the evidence for that purpose was fundamentally unfair. *Id.* at 473-76.

*Fisher* establishes that a jurisdiction may lawfully restrict evidence of mental abnormality falling short of legal insanity to the insanity inquiry, without offending fundamental fairness. Arizona's rule does no more than what this Court sanctioned in *Fisher*: it recognizes that evidence of mental disease is relevant to whether a defendant is legally insane, while declining to convert that same evidence into a freestanding doctrine for negating the mental state elements the prosecution must prove beyond a reasonable doubt.

## III. Due Process Affords States Wide Latitude in Formulating Doctrines of Criminal Responsibility Given the Absence of Scientific or Legal Consensus

In *Powell v. Texas*, 392 U.S. 514 (1968), this Court again emphasized that, absent a clear constitutional command, the formulation of criminal responsibility doctrine belongs to the States. The plurality observed that "traditional common-law concepts of personal accountability and essential considerations of federalism militate against the... adoption of a constitutional doctrine" that would displace a State's chosen framework for assessing criminal responsibility. *Id.* at 535-36. The Court further noted the "large number of variations of the M'Naghten rule, the Durham rule, and the recently promulgated rule of the American Law Institute" as evidence that "there is no such consensus" on the proper legal treatment of mental abnormality that would justify constitutionalizing one approach over another. *Id.* at 536-37.

That reasoning applies with full force here. Arizona has chosen to test criminal responsibility through its insanity defense, within which mental-disease evidence is properly considered, rather than through a supplemental mens rea-negation doctrine of the kind rejected in *Fisher*. Given the enduring absence of consensus among courts and the medical profession identified in both *Powell* and *Leland*, due process does not compel Arizona to adopt Petitioner's preferred framework.

## IV. Arizona's Rule Reflects a Reasonable Accommodation of the Competing Interests at Stake

The rule Arizona applied below—channeling psychiatric evidence to the insanity inquiry, on which the defendant bears the burden, while leaving the prosecution's burden on the elements of the offense undisturbed by that same evidence—reflects precisely the kind of legislative line-drawing that this Court has repeatedly upheld against due process attack. As in *Leland*, Arizona has not eliminated consideration of mental illness from the criminal process; it has simply required that such evidence be evaluated under the defense designed to address it. As in *Fisher*, Arizona has declined to recognize a freestanding partial-responsibility doctrine that would allow the same evidence to do double duty in defeating the prosecution's mens rea case. And as in *Powell*, Arizona's choice reflects the considered judgment of a sovereign State addressing a subject on which reasoned minds continue to differ.

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should hold that the Fourteenth Amendment does not guarantee criminal defendants a right, independent of the insanity defense, to introduce expert mental-state evidence to negate the prosecution's proof of criminal intent, and should affirm the judgment below.
