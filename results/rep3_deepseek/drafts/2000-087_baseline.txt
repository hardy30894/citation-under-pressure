**ARGUMENT**

The post-removal-period detention statute, 8 U.S.C. § 1231(a)(6), does not authorize the Attorney General to detain a removable alien indefinitely. Properly construed, the statute permits detention only for a period reasonably necessary to secure the alien’s removal. Any broader reading would raise serious constitutional concerns that this Court must avoid.

**I. The Statute’s Text and Purpose Require a Reasonable-Time Limitation**

Section 1231(a)(6) provides that an alien ordered removed “may be detained beyond the removal period.” The statute does not say “indefinitely” or “permanently.” Its purpose is to “assur[e] the alien’s presence at the moment of removal.” *Zadvydas v. Davis*, 533 U.S. 678, 699 (2001). Once removal is no longer reasonably foreseeable, detention ceases to serve that purpose and becomes unauthorized. The Fifth Circuit’s contrary reading—that detention is lawful as long as “good faith efforts to effectuate . . . deportation continue,” *Zadvydas v. Underdown*, 185 F.3d 279, 297 (5th Cir. 1999)—would permit indefinite confinement even when removal is impossible. That interpretation cannot be squared with the statute’s limited objective.

**II. Indefinite Detention of a Resident Alien Raises Serious Constitutional Concerns**

Petitioner Kestutis Zadvydas is a resident alien who has been admitted to the United States. As such, he is a “person” entitled to due process protection under the Fifth Amendment. *Wong Wing v. United States*, 163 U.S. 228, 238 (1896). Indefinite civil detention—without a finding of dangerousness or mental illness—violates substantive due process. *Foucha v. Louisiana*, 504 U.S. 71, 80 (1992). Unlike the excludable alien in *Shaughnessy v. United States ex rel. Mezei*, 345 U.S. 206 (1953), who had never effected entry, Zadvydas has lived in this country since childhood. His detention is not a brief holding at the border but a potentially permanent imprisonment.

The government’s interest in ensuring removal is legitimate, but that interest evaporates when removal is impossible. Continued detention then becomes punitive, not regulatory. *United States v. Salerno*, 481 U.S. 739, 746 (1987) (preventive detention must be “regulatory, not punitive”). Here, Germany and Lithuania have refused to accept Zadvydas, and no other country will take him. The Fifth Circuit acknowledged that deportation is “difficult at best,” *Zadvydas*, 185 F.3d at 291, but required Zadvydas to prove that deportation is “impossible”—a standard that demands the impossible. Such a rule would allow the government to detain a resident alien for life without any meaningful judicial review, a result that “would raise serious constitutional concerns.” *Zadvydas*, 533 U.S. at 690.

**III. The Canon of Constitutional Avoidance Compels a Narrow Construction**

This Court has long held that “where an otherwise acceptable construction of a statute would raise serious constitutional problems, the Court will construe the statute to avoid such problems.” *INS v. St. Cyr*, 533 U.S. 289, 299-300 (2001). The Fifth Circuit’s interpretation—that § 1231(a)(6) authorizes detention until removal is “impossible”—would permit indefinite confinement of a resident alien without the procedural protections required for civil commitment. *See Kansas v. Hendricks*, 521 U.S. 346, 356 (1997). To avoid that constitutional threat, the statute must be read to contain an implicit “reasonable time” limitation. *Zadvydas*, 533 U.S. at 689.

**IV. The Proper Standard Is Reasonable Foreseeability of Removal**

The habeas court should determine whether detention exceeds a period reasonably necessary to secure removal. If removal is not reasonably foreseeable, continued detention is unauthorized. The alien may be released under supervision, with conditions to ensure compliance if removal later becomes possible. *Id.* at 699-700. This standard respects Congress’s intent while protecting fundamental liberty. The Fifth Circuit’s “good faith efforts” test, by contrast, places an impossible burden on the alien and effectively sanctions indefinite detention.

**CONCLUSION**

The judgment of the Fifth Circuit should be reversed, and the district court’s order releasing Zadvydas should be affirmed.
