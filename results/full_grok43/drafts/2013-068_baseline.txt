I. THE ACT IS A VALID TIME-PLACE-MANNER RESTRICTION.

The Act, Mass. Gen. Laws ch. 266, § 120E½ (2007), is content-neutral because it “does not draw content-based distinctions on its face” and “cannot be justified without reference to the content of the regulated speech.” Hill v. Colorado, 530 U.S. 703, 719 (2000). It applies equally to all speakers within the fixed 35-foot zones and serves the significant interests of “protecting a woman’s freedom to seek pregnancy-related services” and “ensuring public safety and order.” Id. at 715.

The Act is narrowly tailored. It “promotes a substantial government interest that would be achieved less effectively absent the regulation” and does not “burden substantially more speech than is necessary.” Ward v. Rock Against Racism, 491 U.S. 781, 799 (1989). The zones are limited to entrances, exits, and driveways; they leave the vast majority of public sidewalks and streets open; and they permit “oral speech, signs, and distribution of literature” outside the zones. Hill, 530 U.S. at 729-30. The exemptions for clinic employees and passersby do not render the statute content-based; they are “based on the need to ensure safe access,” not on the speaker’s viewpoint. McGuire v. Reilly, 386 F.3d 45, 57 (1st Cir. 2004).

The Act leaves open ample alternative channels. Petitioners may “communicate at a normal conversational distance” from the sidewalk immediately adjacent to the zones, display signs, use amplification, and distribute literature. Hill, 530 U.S. at 729. “The First Amendment does not guarantee the right to communicate one’s views at all times and places or in any manner that may be desired.” Heffron v. International Society for Krishna Consciousness, Inc., 452 U.S. 640, 647 (1981).

II. HILL V. COLORADO CONTROLS AND SHOULD NOT BE OVERRULED.

Hill upheld a 100-foot floating buffer zone that imposed greater restrictions than the Act. 530 U.S. at 707-08. Stare decisis requires adherence: the decision “has not been undermined by later decisions” and “continues to provide a workable framework.” Citizens United v. FEC, 558 U.S. 310, 362 (2010). Recent cases addressing content- or speaker-based restrictions in other contexts do not “reconfigure the First Amendment landscape” for fixed zones protecting clinic access. Sorrell v. IMS Health Inc., 564 U.S. 552, 565 (2011), concerned commercial speech; Snyder v. Phelps, 562 U.S. 443, 451 (2011), addressed public-issue speech far from a captive audience. No subsequent authority has held that fixed, viewpoint-neutral buffer zones around healthcare facilities are unconstitutional.

The judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
