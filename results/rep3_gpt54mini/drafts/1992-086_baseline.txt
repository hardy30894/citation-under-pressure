## Argument

The District’s refusal to allow Lamb’s Chapel to use school premises for an after-hours film series violated the First Amendment. When a public school opens its facilities for community use, it may not exclude a speaker because the proposed presentation is religious in viewpoint. The controlling cases require reversal.

### I. The school facilities were at least a limited public forum for community use, and Lamb’s Chapel could not be excluded based on the religious viewpoint of its speech.

Public property may be regulated according to the forum it has been made to serve. In *Perry Education Ass’n v. Perry Local Educators’ Ass’n*, 460 U.S. 37, 44-46 (1983), the Court explained that once government opens property for expressive activity, it may not impose viewpoint-based restrictions inconsistent with the First Amendment. That principle was reaffirmed in *Cornelius v. NAACP Legal Defense & Educational Fund, Inc.*, 473 U.S. 788, 802-06 (1985), and *Hazelwood School Dist. v. Kuhlmeier*, 484 U.S. 260, 267-70 (1988).

Here, the District made its premises available after hours for community programs. The facilities were not a traditional public forum, but they were at least a limited forum for outside groups. The Second Circuit recognized that the District had permitted various non-school uses, including civic, educational, musical, and cultural programs. Once the government permits such access, it may not draw lines based on the speaker’s viewpoint. *Perry*, 460 U.S. at 46; *Cornelius*, 473 U.S. at 806.

### II. The District engaged in unconstitutional viewpoint discrimination.

The reason Lamb’s Chapel was rejected was not that film series on family values were barred from the premises. It was that the same subject matter, when presented from a Christian perspective, was disfavored. That is viewpoint discrimination. The First Amendment does not permit government to allow discussion of a subject and then suppress a particular perspective on that subject. *Perry*, 460 U.S. at 46-47; *Rosenberger v. Rector and Visitors of Univ. of Virginia*, 515 U.S. 819, 829-31 (1995).

This Court’s decision in *Lamb’s Chapel v. Center Moriches Union Free School Dist.*, 959 F.2d 381 (2d Cir. 1992), erred in treating the exclusion as neutral. The proposed films addressed family issues and child rearing, topics plainly within the scope of uses the District had allowed. Denying access solely because the films would present those topics “from a Christian perspective” amounted to the suppression of a viewpoint, not a permissible limitation on subject matter. See *Rosenberger*, 515 U.S. at 831; *Good News Club v. Milford Cent. Sch.*, 533 U.S. 98, 107-12 (2001).

The Court’s later decision in *Good News Club* is particularly instructive. There, as here, a school district opened its facilities to community groups but denied access to a religious group seeking to address children on moral and family issues from a Christian standpoint. The Court held that exclusion of the religious group because of the religious nature of its viewpoint violated the Free Speech Clause. *Good News Club*, 533 U.S. at 103-12.

### III. The Establishment Clause did not justify exclusion.

The District cannot defend discrimination by invoking the Establishment Clause. The Court has repeatedly held that allowing equal access to religious speakers in a limited forum does not amount to an endorsement of religion. *Widmar v. Vincent*, 454 U.S. 263, 274-75 (1981); *Board of Educ. of Westside Cmty. Schs. v. Mergens*, 496 U.S. 226, 248-53 (1990); *Rosenberger*, 515 U.S. at 845-46. In *Widmar*, the Court rejected the argument that permitting religious student speech would violate the Establishment Clause, explaining that neutral access does not confer sponsorship. 454 U.S. at 274-75. In *Mergens*, the Court likewise held that equal access for a student religious club did not create an establishment problem. 496 U.S. at 248-53.

The same rule applies here. Lamb’s Chapel sought after-hours access open to the public on neutral terms. Permitting the film series would have signaled neither endorsement nor coercion; it would have reflected neutrality. *Widmar*, 454 U.S. at 274-75; *Rosenberger*, 515 U.S. at 839-46.

### IV. The judgment below should be reversed.

The First Amendment forbids a public school district from opening its facilities to community expression while excluding religious viewpoints on the same subject. Because the District denied Lamb’s Chapel access based on the religious perspective of its films, the exclusion cannot stand. The judgment should be reversed.
