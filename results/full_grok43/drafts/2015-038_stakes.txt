**ARGUMENT**

Section 103(c) of the Alaska National Interest Lands Conservation Act, Pub. L. No. 96-487, 94 Stat. 2371 (codified at 16 U.S.C. § 3103(c)), does not divest the National Park Service of authority to enforce generally applicable regulations, such as the hovercraft prohibition, 36 C.F.R. § 2.17(e), on lands and waters within the boundaries of Conservation System Units in Alaska.

The statute provides that non-federal lands “shall not be subject to the regulations applicable solely to public lands within such units.” 16 U.S.C. § 3103(c). This language addresses only regulations that apply exclusively to public lands; it leaves untouched rules of general applicability that govern all lands and waters administered by the Service. See, e.g., Connecticut Nat’l Bank v. Germain, 503 U.S. 249, 253-54 (1992) (“[C]ourts must presume that a legislature says in a statute what it means and means in a statute what it says there.”).

The Service’s organic authority extends to “all lands” within System units, 54 U.S.C. § 100751(a), and has long been understood to reach navigable waters and adjacent lands regardless of ownership. See, e.g., United States v. California, 436 U.S. 32, 40-41 (1978) (federal navigational servitude and regulatory power over waters within federal reservations). Nothing in ANILCA repeals that authority “except as specifically set forth.” 16 U.S.C. § 3207.

Sturgeon’s contrary reading would require the Court to insert the word “all” before “regulations,” contrary to the settled rule that courts “ordinarily resist reading words or elements into a statute that do not appear on its face.” Bates v. United States, 522 U.S. 23, 29 (1997). The Ninth Circuit correctly held that the hovercraft ban is not a regulation “applicable solely to public lands,” and therefore remains enforceable on the Nation River within Yukon-Charley.

The judgment should be affirmed.
