The Massachusetts law creating a 35-foot buffer zone around abortion clinics is a constitutional time, place, and manner regulation under the First Amendment. The First Circuit correctly upheld the law, as it is content-neutral, narrowly tailored, and leaves open adequate alternative channels of communication.

The buffer zone is a reasonable measure to ensure public safety and facilitate access to healthcare facilities. The Supreme Court has previously upheld similar buffer zones in Hill v. Colorado, 530 U.S. 703 (2000). In Hill, the Court found that a Colorado statute creating a buffer zone around healthcare facilities was constitutional because it was content-neutral and did not burden more speech than necessary to achieve the state's interests. Id. at 725-26. Similarly, the Massachusetts law is content-neutral, as it applies to all individuals, regardless of their viewpoint or message.

The law is also narrowly tailored to achieve the state's interests in public safety and access to healthcare. The buffer zone is limited to 35 feet around the entrances, exits, and driveways of abortion clinics, and it exempts certain individuals, such as clinic employees, law enforcement, and individuals accessing the clinic. Mass. Gen. Laws ch. 266, § 120E 1/2(b), (c). This narrow tailoring ensures that the law does not burden more speech than necessary to achieve the state's interests.

Furthermore, the law leaves open adequate alternative channels of communication. The petitioners can still engage in sidewalk counseling, distribute literature, and display signs outside the buffer zone. They can also use other methods, such as wearing evocative garments or costumes, to convey their message. As the First Circuit noted, the petitioners have achieved success in their counseling efforts, persuading women to refrain from seeking abortions, even with the buffer zone in place. McCullen v. Coakley, 708 F.3d 1, 12-13 (1st Cir. 2013).

The petitioners argue that the law is not content-neutral because it disproportionately affects their ability to communicate with patients. However, the law applies equally to all individuals, regardless of their viewpoint or message. The exemptions in the law are also viewpoint-neutral, as they are based on the individual's purpose or status, rather than their message. See Mass. Gen. Laws ch. 266, § 120E 1/2(b), (c).

The petitioners also argue that the law is not narrowly tailored because it burdens more speech than necessary to achieve the state's interests. However, the buffer zone is a reasonable measure to ensure public safety and facilitate access to healthcare facilities. The state has a legitimate interest in protecting patients and staff from harassment and intimidation, and the buffer zone is a reasonable means of achieving that interest.

The Supreme Court's decision in Hill v. Colorado controls this case. The Colorado statute at issue in Hill is similar to the Massachusetts law, and the Court's reasoning in Hill applies equally here. The petitioners' attempts to distinguish Hill are unpersuasive, and the Court should follow the same reasoning here.

In conclusion, the Massachusetts law creating a 35-foot buffer zone around abortion clinics is a constitutional time, place, and manner regulation under the First Amendment. The law is content-neutral, narrowly tailored, and leaves open adequate alternative channels of communication. The First Circuit correctly upheld the law, and this Court should affirm that decision.
