The Equal Protection Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment secures to all persons "the protection of equal laws." Yick Wo v. Hopkins, 118 U.S. 356, 369 (1886). It "is essentially a direction that all persons similarly situated should be treated alike." Barbier v. Connolly, 113 U.S. 27, 31 (1885). From its adoption, the Clause has been understood to forbid "class legislation" that singles out a defined group for adverse treatment while leaving others untouched. The Slaughter-House Cases, 83 U.S. 36, 81 (1873); see also Strauder v. West Virginia, 100 U.S. 303, 307-08 (1880) (the amendment "contain[s] a necessary implication of a design to favor the class of persons" subjected to discriminatory state action).

This Court has consistently required that an equal-protection plaintiff demonstrate membership in a class or group that has been treated differently from others similarly situated. In Yick Wo, the petitioners prevailed only by showing that the challenged ordinance was "applied and administered... with a mind so unequal and oppressive as to amount to a practical denial by the State of... equal protection of the laws" to persons of Chinese ancestry. 118 U.S. at 373. The same class-based showing was required in cases alleging discriminatory enforcement of facially neutral statutes. See, e.g., Sunday Lake Iron Co. v. Township of Wakefield, 247 U.S. 350, 352-53 (1918) (no violation absent proof that assessment officials "intentionally and arbitrarily undervalued" property of a particular class).

A claim that state action was motivated solely by personal animus toward an individual, without more, falls outside the Clause's protection. As the Court explained in Snowden v. Hughes, 321 U.S. 1 (1944), "the unlawful administration by state officers of a state statute fair on its face, resulting in its unequal application to those who are entitled to be treated alike, is not a denial of equal protection unless there is shown to be present in it an element of intentional or purposeful discrimination." Id. at 8. The plaintiff there alleged he had been singled out for adverse treatment; the Court held that such an allegation, standing alone, stated no constitutional claim. Id. at 7-10.

The same principle governs here. Because the complaint alleges neither membership in a protected class nor differential treatment of any defined group, it fails to state a violation of the Equal Protection Clause. The judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
