The Ninth Circuit's decision in this case misinterprets the "actual knowledge" requirement under 29 U.S.C. § 1113(2). To trigger the three-year statute of limitations, a plaintiff must have actual knowledge of the breach or violation, not just the underlying transaction. The courts have long recognized that "actual knowledge" requires more than mere awareness of the facts; it demands a genuine understanding of the significance of those facts.

In United States v. Wurts, 303 U.S. 414 (1938), this Court held that the government's right to sue is independent of statute, and that a statute of limitations does not grant a new right but rather limits an existing one. Similarly, in Curtis v. Connly, 257 U.S. 260 (1921), the Court emphasized that a statute of limitations is a defense that must be strictly construed. These principles are relevant here, as the "actual knowledge" requirement is a critical component of the statute of limitations under ERISA.

The Ninth Circuit's reliance on Blanton v. Anzalone, 760 F.2d 989 (9th Cir. 1985), is misplaced. While Blanton held that knowledge of the transaction is sufficient to trigger the statute of limitations, it did so in the context of a pre-1987 version of § 1113, which included a constructive knowledge provision. The 1987 amendment, which repealed the constructive knowledge provision, suggests that Congress intended to heighten the standard for triggering the statute of limitations.

In Exploration Co. v. United States, 247 U.S. 435 (1918), this Court held that a statute of limitations does not bar an action if the plaintiff was unaware of the facts underlying the claim due to the defendant's concealment. Similarly, in American Surety Company v. Pauly, 170 U.S. 160 (1898), the Court emphasized the importance of the plaintiff's knowledge of the facts in determining when the statute of limitations begins to run. These cases suggest that "actual knowledge" requires more than mere disclosure of information; it demands that the plaintiff have a genuine understanding of the significance of that information.

Furthermore, in Unexcelled Chemical Corp. v. United States, 345 U.S. 59 (1953), the Court held that a statute of limitations begins to run when the cause of action accrues, not when the plaintiff discovers the facts underlying the claim. However, this principle is tempered by the requirement that the plaintiff have actual knowledge of the breach or violation. In United States v. A. S. Kreider Co., 313 U.S. 443 (1941), the Court emphasized the importance of strictly construing statutes of limitations.

The Ninth Circuit's decision is also inconsistent with the principles enunciated in Baker v. Cummings, 181 U.S. 117 (1901), and Peak v. United States, 353 U.S. 43 (1957). In Baker, the Court held that a plaintiff's claim is barred by a prior adjudication, even if the plaintiff did not fully understand the implications of the prior decision. In Peak, the Court emphasized the importance of the plaintiff's knowledge of the facts in determining the timeliness of the action. These cases suggest that "actual knowledge" requires a genuine understanding of the facts, not just awareness of the underlying transaction.

In Jaben v. United States, 381 U.S. 214 (1965), the Court held that a complaint must satisfy the probable-cause requirement of Rule 4 to trigger the statute of limitations. Similarly, in United States v. Goldman, 277 U.S. 229 (1928), the Court emphasized the importance of strict compliance with procedural requirements. These cases support the proposition that "actual knowledge" requires a high level of awareness and understanding.

In light of these authorities, it is clear that the Ninth Circuit's decision misinterprets the "actual knowledge" requirement under § 1113(2). To trigger the three-year statute of limitations, a plaintiff must have actual knowledge of the breach or violation, not just the underlying transaction. The mere disclosure of information is insufficient; the plaintiff must have a genuine understanding of the significance of that information. We urge this Court to reverse the Ninth Circuit's decision and hold that "actual knowledge" requires a plaintiff to have a genuine understanding of the breach or violation, not just awareness of the underlying transaction.
