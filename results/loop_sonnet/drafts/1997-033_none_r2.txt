# ARGUMENT

Section 1407(a) commands that the Panel "shall" remand a transferred action "at or before the conclusion of" pretrial proceedings. The District of Arizona ignored that command by "transferring" this case to itself under Section 1404(a) for trial. Because Section 1407(a)'s remand duty is mandatory, and because Section 1404(a) supplies no license to override it, the judgment below must be reversed.

## I. The plain, mandatory text of Section 1407(a) forecloses any transferee court's use of Section 1404(a) to retain a case for trial.

Congress's use of "shall" in Section 1407(a) is not advisory. As this Court has recognized, such language is "the language of command." *Escoe v. Zerbst*, 295 U.S. 490, 493 (1935). Where Congress speaks in mandatory terms, courts must give that language effect according to its terms, for "the meaning of a statute must, in the first instance, be sought in the language in which the act is framed, and if that is plain, ... the sole function of the courts is to enforce it according to its terms." *Caminetti v. United States*, 242 U.S. 470, 485 (1917). And "there is, of course, no more persuasive evidence of the purpose of a statute than the words by which the legislature undertook to give expression to its wishes." *United States v. American Trucking Ass'ns*, 310 U.S. 534, 543 (1940). Here, Congress's words are unambiguous: remand must occur "at or before" the conclusion of pretrial proceedings—not "unless the transferee court prefers otherwise."

Milberg's countermotion asked the District of Arizona to convert a temporary pretrial assignment into a permanent trial venue by invoking Section 1404(a)'s discretionary convenience standard. But Congress did not leave the duration of a Panel-ordered transfer to the discretion of the transferee judge; it fixed that duration by statute. A court may not nullify a mandatory legislative command merely because it judges that departure would serve efficiency. Whatever hardship the remand requirement may occasionally impose is a matter for Congress to remedy, not for courts to correct through ad hoc revision of unambiguous text.

## II. Because Congress specifically confined multidistrict transfer to pretrial proceedings, the general transfer authority of Section 1404(a) cannot override that specific limitation.

Sections 1404(a) and 1407(a) serve distinct purposes and were drafted with different scope. Section 1404(a) is a "federal judicial housekeeping measure," dealing generally with "the placement of litigation in the federal courts" and "intended ... to protect litigants, witnesses, and the public against unnecessary inconvenience and expense." *Van Dusen v. Barrack*, 376 U.S. 612, 636–37 (1964). It operates only where the transferee forum is one in which the action "might have been brought" by the plaintiff at the outset—a limitation this Court has enforced strictly, even against arguments grounded in convenience or party consent. *Hoffman v. Blaski*, 363 U.S. 335, 343–44 (1960). Section 1407(a), by contrast, is a specialized mechanism enacted for the narrow purpose of coordinating pretrial proceedings among related actions pending in different districts, with the Panel's authority expressly bounded by the remand command. The general "convenience of parties and witnesses, in the interest of justice" standard that governs ordinary venue transfer, *Continental Grain Co. v. Barge FBL-585*, 364 U.S. 19, 26 (1960), cannot be read to swallow the specific temporal limit Congress placed on multidistrict transfer.

This Court's decision in *Fourco Glass Co. v. Transmirra Products Corp.*, 353 U.S. 222 (1957), is directly instructive. There, the Court held that the general venue statute could not be used to expand the specific venue provision governing patent infringement suits, explaining that "however inclusive may be the general language of a statute, it 'will not be held to apply to a matter specifically dealt with in another part of the same enactment.'" *Id.* at 228–29 (quoting *Rodgers v. United States*, 185 U.S. 83, 87–89 (1902)). The same principle controls here. Section 1407(a) specifically and exclusively governs the temporal scope of a Panel-ordered transfer; it cannot be "controlled or nullified" by resort to the general transfer authority of Section 1404(a)—a statute of general applicability that says nothing whatever about multidistrict litigation. *Id.*

Nor has this Court ever understood Section 1404(a) to authorize a court to displace a plaintiff's chosen forum for trial merely because the case happens to be before it for another, limited purpose. Section 1404(a) is "a federal statute dealing with the placement of litigation in the federal courts," designed for ordinary venue disputes between forums in which an action could independently have been brought—not a vehicle by which a transferee court may entrench itself in a case Congress committed to it solely for pretrial coordination. *Ex parte Collett*, 337 U.S. 55, 61 (1949). Lexecon selected the Northern District of Illinois as its forum. Nothing in Section 1404(a)'s convenience calculus permits a court sitting only by virtue of the Panel's pretrial assignment to substitute its own trial-venue preference for the plaintiff's chosen forum, particularly where doing so directly contradicts the specific remand command of Section 1407(a).

## III. The court below erred in treating this conflict as unresolved.

The Ninth Circuit acknowledged "what appears to be a clear statutory mandate" yet declined to enforce it, resting instead on a Panel-promulgated procedural rule and non-binding circuit precedent that, by the dissenting judge's own account, developed "willy-nilly" over a quarter century without grappling with the statutory text. But an administrative rule cannot rewrite the words Congress enacted, and no court may relax a mandatory statutory command merely because compliance proves inconvenient in a particular case. Under *American Trucking*, 310 U.S. at 543, and *Caminetti*, 242 U.S. at 485, the "sole function" of a court confronting plain statutory text is to enforce it—not to weigh policy trade-offs that Congress has already resolved. Because Section 1407(a) required remand of this action to the Northern District of Illinois "at or before" the conclusion of pretrial proceedings, and because Section 1404(a) supplies no authority to override that specific command, the judgment below should be reversed.
