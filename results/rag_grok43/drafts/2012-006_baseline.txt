The government bears the burden to prove beyond a reasonable doubt that each defendant remained a member of the charged conspiracy during the limitations period. Once a combination is shown, liability does not automatically extend forward; the prosecution must establish that the defendants, "by combination and conspiracy, procured" continuation of the unlawful agreement into the actionable window. McDaniel v. Traylor, 212 U.S. 428, 431 (1909). A general verdict of guilt on an earlier indictment supplies prima facie evidence only of "the conspiracy and also of the performance of such acts in accomplishing it as the jury in the criminal case, in rendering a verdict of guilty, necessarily found to have occurred." Emich Motors Corp. v. General Motors Corp., 340 U.S. 558, 568 (1951). It does not relieve the government of proving, through evidence independent of the verdict, that the same combination persisted after any claimed withdrawal.

Where the record shows the leaders "split up before the relevant statute of limitations period," the government cannot rely on a presumption of continuity. Instead, it must demonstrate that the defendants "adhered" to the conspiracy through overt acts or other conduct within the limitations window. First Nat. Bank of Ariz. v. Cities Service Co., 391 U.S. 253, 287 (1968). The statute of limitations is not tolled indefinitely by the initial agreement; it runs from the last overt act attributable to each participant. Zenith Radio Corp. v. Hazeltine Research, Inc., 401 U.S. 321, 338 (1971). Withdrawal therefore places on the government the affirmative obligation to prove continued membership, rather than requiring the defendant to negate it.

Because the evidence here failed to establish that petitioners remained participants after the split, the convictions cannot stand.
