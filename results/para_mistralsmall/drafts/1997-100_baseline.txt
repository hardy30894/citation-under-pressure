**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Double Jeopardy Clause Bars Retrial of Prior Conviction Allegations in Noncapital Sentencing Proceedings**

The Double Jeopardy Clause of the Fifth Amendment—made applicable to the states through the Fourteenth Amendment—provides that no person shall “be subject for the same offence to be twice put in jeopardy of life or limb.” U.S. Const. amend. V. This constitutional protection extends beyond the guilt phase of a criminal prosecution to bar retrial of sentencing enhancements where the prosecution fails to prove its case. *Bullington v. Missouri*, 451 U.S. 430, 438 (1981) (“[T]he Double Jeopardy Clause bars a second attempt by the State to put a defendant in jeopardy for the same offense after an acquittal.”); *Rumsey v. Maryland*, 467 U.S. 203, 209 (1984) (applying *Bullington* to sentencing proceedings).

The Supreme Court has recognized that sentencing proceedings may implicate double jeopardy protections when they resemble a trial on guilt or innocence. *Bullington*, 451 U.S. at 439 (“[W]e must determine whether the sentencing proceeding involves characteristics that make it resemble a trial for purposes of the Double Jeopardy Clause.”); *Rumsey*, 467 U.S. at 209-10 (same). In *Bullington*, the Court held that Missouri’s capital sentencing scheme—where the jury was required to find specific aggravating factors beyond a reasonable doubt—triggered double jeopardy protections because the proceeding “was like a trial on the issue of guilt or innocence.” 451 U.S. at 445. Similarly, in *Rumsey*, the Court applied *Bullington* to a noncapital sentencing proceeding where the state was required to prove a statutory aggravating factor beyond a reasonable doubt. 467 U.S. at 210.

Here, California law requires that prior conviction allegations be proved beyond a reasonable doubt. *People v. Tenner*, 6 Cal.4th 559, 566 (1993) (“[T]he prosecution must prove a prior conviction allegation beyond a reasonable doubt.”); *In re Yurko*, 10 Cal.3d 857, 862 (1974) (same). The California Court of Appeal correctly concluded that the Double Jeopardy Clause bars retrial of the prior conviction allegation after an acquittal. The Supreme Court of California erred in holding otherwise.

**II. The Supreme Court Has Not Limited *Bullington* to Capital Cases**

The California Supreme Court’s decision rests on the erroneous premise that *Bullington* and its progeny are limited to capital sentencing proceedings. *Monge v. California*, No. S065370 (Cal. Feb. 4, 1999) (slip op. at 12-13). This misreading of precedent is unsupported by the law.

The Supreme Court has never held that *Bullington* applies only in capital cases. In *Caspari v. Bohlen*, 510 U.S. 383, 392 (1994), the Court noted that *Bullington* and *Rumsey* were capital cases but did not suggest that their reasoning was so limited. Rather, the Court in *Caspari* was addressing whether the rule announced in *Bullington* was a “new rule” under *Teague v. Lane*, 489 U.S. 288 (1989), not whether *Bullington* applied only in capital cases. *Caspari*, 510 U.S. at 392-93.

Moreover, the Supreme Court has repeatedly applied *Bullington*’s reasoning to noncapital sentencing schemes. In *United States v. Watts*, 519 U.S. 148 (1997), the Court cited *Bullington* with approval in the context of a noncapital sentencing proceeding under the federal sentencing guidelines. *Id.* at 154-55. The Court’s reliance on *Bullington* in *Watts* demonstrates that the principles articulated in *Bullington* are not confined to capital sentencing.

**III. The Procedural Protections Afforded to Defendants in Prior Conviction Allegations Make Them Function Like a Trial on Guilt or Innocence**

The Supreme Court has emphasized that the critical factor in applying double jeopardy protections to sentencing proceedings is whether the proceeding resembles a trial on guilt or innocence. *Bullington*, 451 U.S. at 439; *Rumsey*, 467 U.S. at 209-10. In California, prior conviction allegations are tried to a jury (or, as here, to the court) with the prosecution bearing the burden of proving the allegations beyond a reasonable doubt. Cal. Penal Code § 1025; *Tenner*, 6 Cal.4th at 566. The defendant enjoys the right to confront and cross-examine witnesses, to present evidence, and to remain silent. *People v. Reed*, 13 Cal.4th 217, 224 (1996); *In re Yurko*, 10 Cal.3d at 863, fn. 5.

These procedural protections transform the prior conviction allegation into a proceeding that closely resembles a trial on guilt or innocence. The Supreme Court has recognized that when a sentencing proceeding includes such protections, it may trigger double jeopardy protections. *Bullington*, 451 U.S. at 439; *Rumsey*, 467 U.S. at 209-10. The California Supreme Court’s contrary conclusion ignores this precedent.

**IV. The California Supreme Court’s Decision Undermines the Integrity of the Double Jeopardy Clause**

The Double Jeopardy Clause serves to protect defendants from the governmental oppression of repeated prosecutions. *Green v. United States*, 355 U.S. 184, 187 (1957). The California Supreme Court’s decision permits the state to retry prior conviction allegations ad infinitum, subjecting defendants to the risk of repeated prosecutions for the same enhancement. This undermines the core purpose of the Double Jeopardy Clause.

The Supreme Court has warned against interpretations of the Double Jeopardy Clause that would permit such governmental overreaching. *United States v. DiFrancesco*, 449 U.S. 117, 134 (1980) (“[T]he Double Jeopardy Clause does more than prevent a second trial for the same offense; it protects against multiple punishments for the same offense.”). The California Supreme Court’s decision contravenes this principle.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the Double Jeopardy Clause bars retrial of prior conviction allegations in noncapital sentencing proceedings. The judgment of the California Supreme Court should be reversed.
