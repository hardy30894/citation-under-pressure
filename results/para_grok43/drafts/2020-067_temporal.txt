The Eleventh Circuit properly consulted the full record in determining that any Rehaif error neither affected Greer’s substantial rights nor seriously undermined the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of the proceedings. Plain-error review has always permitted an appellate court to examine the entire cause, not merely the evidence formally introduced before the jury.

In United States v. Atkinson, 297 U.S. 157 (1936), this Court held that an appellate court may notice an unpreserved error “if the errors are obvious, or if they otherwise seriously affect the fairness, integrity or public reputation of judicial proceedings.” Id. at 160. That inquiry necessarily extends beyond the trial transcript; the Court has long recognized that an appellate court “may examine the whole record” to decide whether an asserted defect warrants reversal. Wiborg v. United States, 163 U.S. 632, 658 (1896). The same principle appears in cases addressing the effect of an error on substantial rights. In Kotteakos v. United States, 328 U.S. 750 (1946), the Court instructed that the question is whether the error “had substantial influence” on the outcome, a determination made by considering “the proceedings in their entirety.” Id. at 764-765. Nothing in that formulation confines the court to the evidence the jury actually heard.

The same breadth of review governs the fourth prong of plain-error analysis. In Johnson v. United States, 318 U.S. 189 (1943), the Court looked to matters developed after verdict—specifically, the defendant’s subsequent conduct—to decide whether reversal would serve the public interest. Id. at 196. Likewise, in Silber v. United States, 370 U.S. 717 (1962), the Court examined the entire course of the prosecution, not merely the trial record, before declining to reverse for an unpreserved defect. These decisions confirm that an appellate court may consider facts outside the four corners of the trial evidence when assessing whether an error “seriously affect[ed] the fairness, integrity or public reputation of judicial proceedings.” Atkinson, 297 U.S. at 160.

Greer’s prior felony convictions, though not introduced at trial, were part of the record properly before the court of appeals. Under the authorities above, that court was entitled to consult them in determining that any instructional omission did not call the integrity of the proceedings into question. The judgment should therefore be affirmed.
