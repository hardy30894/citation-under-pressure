**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE EQUAL FOOTING DOCTRINE REQUIRES TITLE TO NAVIGABLE WATERS AT THE TIME OF STATEHOOD**

When a state enters the Union, it holds title to the beds of navigable waters within its borders. This principle, known as the Equal Footing Doctrine, mandates that a state's title to submerged lands is determined by the condition of the waterway at the time of the state's admission. *United States v. Utah*, 283 U.S. 64, 78 (1931). Consequently, the State of Montana acquired title to the riverbeds underlying the Missouri, Madison, and Clark Fork Rivers at the moment of its admission to the Union in 1889. The State’s ownership is not dependent on the current navigability of these waterways, but rather on their navigable status at the time of statehood.

**II. THE TEST FOR NAVIGABILITY FOR TITLE IS HISTORICAL, NOT CURRENT**

The Supreme Court has consistently held that the test for determining navigability for title is whether the waters were navigable in fact at the time of the state's admission. *United States v. Oregon*, 295 U.S. 1, 20 (1935). This historical inquiry is distinct from the regulatory test used for the Clean Water Act, which looks at current conditions. *United States v. Riverside Bayview Homes, Inc.*, 474 U.S. 121, 131 (1985). In *Riverside Bayview*, the Court explained that the test for navigability for title is "whether the waters were navigable in fact at the time of the admission of the state," whereas the test for navigability for regulatory purposes is "whether the waters are navigable in fact at the time the question is presented." *Id.* at 131. Because the State asserts a claim to title based on the Equal Footing Doctrine, the trial court was correct to apply the historical test and determine whether the rivers were navigable in 1889.

**III. THE MISSOURI, MADISON, AND CLARK FORK RIVERS WERE NAVIGABLE AT THE TIME OF STATEHOOD**

The record demonstrates that the Missouri, Madison, and Clark Fork Rivers were capable of supporting commerce at the time of Montana's admission in 1889. *United States v. Appalachian Electric Power Co.*, 311 U.S. 377, 407 (1940). The Court in *Appalachian Electric* defined navigability for title as "the capacity to support commerce." *Id.* at 407. The historical evidence presented to the trial court established that these rivers were used for travel and trade in 1889, satisfying the historical test for title. *United States v. Oregon*, 295 U.S. at 20. Therefore, the State holds valid title to the riverbeds underlying PPL’s hydroelectric facilities, and PPL is obligated to compensate the State for the use of that land.
