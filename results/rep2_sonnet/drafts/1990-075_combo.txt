# ARGUMENT

## I. CONGRESS'S DELEGATION TO THE ATTORNEY GENERAL OF TEMPORARY SCHEDULING AUTHORITY SATISFIES THE INTELLIGIBLE PRINCIPLE STANDARD

Article I vests "all legislative powers" in Congress, U.S. Const. art. I, § 1, but this Court has long recognized that Congress may enlist the assistance of the Executive Branch in filling in the details of a regulatory scheme. As Chief Justice Marshall explained over a century and a half ago, "the line has not been exactly drawn which separates those important subjects, which must be entirely regulated by the legislature itself, from those of less interest, in which a general provision may be made, and power given to those who are to act under such general provisions to fill up the details." *Wayman v. Southard*, 23 U.S. (10 Wheat.) 1, 43 (1825). This flexible approach has governed delegation doctrine ever since.

The touchstone of the inquiry is whether Congress has laid "down by legislative act an intelligible principle to which the person or body authorized to [act] is directed to conform." *J.W. Hampton, Jr. & Co. v. United States*, 276 U.S. 394, 409 (1928). So long as Congress establishes that intelligible principle, "such legislative action is not a forbidden delegation of legislative power." *Id.* This Court has applied that test with considerable deference to the practical necessities of modern governance, recognizing that "the Constitution has never been regarded as denying to the Congress the necessary resources of flexibility and practicality." *American Power & Light Co. v. SEC*, 329 U.S. 90, 105 (1946).

Indeed, this Court has sustained delegations far less confined than the one at issue here. In *Yakus v. United States*, 321 U.S. 414 (1944), the Court upheld a delegation authorizing the Price Administrator to fix commodity prices that were "generally fair and equitable" and would effectuate the purposes of the Emergency Price Control Act—a standard considerably more open-ended than the eight specific factors Congress enumerated in 21 U.S.C. § 811(c) and incorporated into the temporary scheduling provision. The Court there emphasized that "the essentials of the legislative function are the determination of the legislative policy and its formulation as a rule of conduct," and that Congress satisfies this function when it prescribes the standard by which delegated authority must be exercised. *Id.* at 424, 426. Congress did precisely that here: § 811(h) requires the Attorney General to consider the substance's history and current pattern of abuse, the scope and significance of that abuse, and the risk to public health—three of the eight statutory factors that have governed permanent scheduling since 1970.

Similarly, in *Fahey v. Mallonee*, 332 U.S. 245 (1947), the Court upheld a delegation to the Federal Home Loan Bank Board to promulgate rules governing the conservatorship of savings and loan associations, reasoning that the delegation was permissible because it operated against a backdrop of "well-known and generally acceptable standards" in the regulated field. *Id.* at 250. The same is true here: temporary scheduling operates against the backdrop of the permanent scheduling criteria that Congress itself specified in § 811(c), criteria with which the Attorney General, the DEA, and regulated parties have been familiar for over a decade.

This Court's decision in *Lichter v. United States*, 334 U.S. 742 (1948), forecloses any suggestion that the delegation here is unconstitutionally broad merely because the subject matter is of great importance. The Court there upheld delegation to renegotiate excessive wartime profits under a standard no more precise than "excessive profits," explaining that "[l]oose as the necessary statutory expression of legislative policy may be, the courts are still able to review its exercise." *Id.* at 785-86. The temporary scheduling provision is materially tighter: it requires findings of an "imminent hazard to public safety," articulated criteria for that finding, notice in the Federal Register, consultation with the Secretary of Health and Human Services, and a strict durational limit of one year (extendable by six months). 21 U.S.C. § 811(h).

## II. THE DELEGATION IS NOT RENDERED UNCONSTITUTIONAL MERELY BECAUSE IT TOUCHES CRIMINAL ENFORCEMENT

Petitioners' reliance on *A.L.A. Schechter Poultry Corp. v. United States*, 295 U.S. 495 (1935), and *Panama Refining Co. v. Ryan*, 293 U.S. 388 (1935), is misplaced. Those decisions—the only two instances in this Court's history in which a delegation has been invalidated—involved standardless grants of authority. In *Panama Refining*, the statute "d[id] not require any finding by the President as a condition of his action," and failed to establish "any rule of conduct." 293 U.S. at 415, 430. In *Schechter Poultry*, the codes of "fair competition" were promulgated with "no standards prescribed for [their] formulation." 295 U.S. at 541. Section 811(h) suffers no such infirmity: it commands specific factual findings, incorporates enumerated statutory factors, and imposes procedural checks including publication and expert consultation.

That the delegated authority intersects with criminal liability does not demand a different standard. In *United States v. Grimaud*, 220 U.S. 506 (1911), this Court upheld criminal penalties for violation of Forest Service regulations promulgated pursuant to a broad delegation, holding that "from the beginning of the Government, various acts have been passed conferring upon executive officers power to make rules and regulations,—not for the government of their departments, but for administering the laws which did affect private rights." *Id.* at 517. The Court further explained that Congress "could not delegate its legislative power to make a law, but it could make a law delegating power to determine some fact or state of things upon which the law makes, or intends to make, its own action depend." *Id.* at 517 (quoting *Field v. Clark*, 143 U.S. 649, 694 (1892)). Section 811(h) does precisely this: it makes criminal liability turn upon the Attorney General's factual determination that a substance poses an imminent hazard, a determination Congress itself has already declared warrants Schedule I treatment under the permanent scheduling factors.

## III. THE ATTORNEY GENERAL'S SUBDELEGATION TO THE DEA WAS AUTHORIZED AND PROPER

Congress expressly authorized the Attorney General to delegate his functions under the CSA. 21 U.S.C. § 871(a). This Court has long recognized that heads of executive departments may distribute their statutory duties among subordinate officers absent a clear congressional prohibition. *Cudahy Packing Co. v. Holland*, 315 U.S. 357 (1942), is not to the contrary; there, the Court found subdelegation improper only because the specific statute, unlike § 871(a), evinced no congressional intent to permit it and required personal exercise of investigatory subpoena power. Here, by contrast, Congress's grant of general subdelegation authority in § 871(a), coupled with the Attorney General's longstanding practice—dating to 1973—of delegating CSA functions to the DEA Administrator, confirms that the subdelegation was both authorized and properly exercised.
