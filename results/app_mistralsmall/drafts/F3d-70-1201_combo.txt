**ARGUMENT**

I. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN PERMITTING THE INTRODUCTION OF SUBSEQUENT REMEDIAL MEASURES FOR IMPEACHMENT PURPOSES IN A STRICT PRODUCTS LIABILITY CASE.

Rule 407 of the Federal Rules of Evidence provides that evidence of subsequent remedial measures is not admissible to prove negligence, culpable conduct, a defect in a product, or a need for a warning or instruction. *Fed. R. Evid. 407*. The Advisory Committee Notes to Rule 407 explain that the rule is designed to encourage remedial action by removing the disincentive of potential liability. *Id.* Advisory Committee’s Note (1972). The rule applies equally in strict products liability cases. *See* *Dart v. Wiebe Mfg., Inc.*, 504 F.2d 1245, 1248 (8th Cir. 1974) (applying Rule 407 in a strict products liability case).

The district court erred in permitting the introduction of subsequent remedial measures for impeachment purposes. The court allowed evidence that the infeed chute had been lengthened after the accident, reasoning that this evidence was admissible to impeach Morbark’s counsel’s implication that the seventeen-inch chute was the safest length available. *See* R4-56. However, this reasoning is flawed. The impeachment exception to Rule 407 is exceedingly narrow and does not permit the introduction of subsequent remedial measures to contradict a party’s litigation position. *See* *United States v. Miles*, 369 F.2d 693, 697 (6th Cir. 1966) (holding that impeachment must be based on a prior inconsistent statement, not on subsequent remedial measures).

Moreover, the district court’s ruling effectively nullified the protections of Rule 407. Once the court permitted the introduction of subsequent remedial measures, it invited the jury to consider this evidence as proof of the product’s defectiveness. *See* *Shepherd v. Red Lobster Inns of America*, 340 F.2d 634, 637 (6th Cir. 1965) (noting that the admission of subsequent remedial measures can prejudice a defendant by suggesting fault). This is precisely the type of evidence Rule 407 seeks to exclude.

II. THE DISTRICT COURT ABUSED ITS DISCRETION IN DENYING WOOD’S MOTION FOR A NEW TRIAL.

A district court’s denial of a motion for a new trial is reviewed for abuse of discretion. *See* *Mercadel v. Rhode Island Hospital Trust Nat’l Bank*, 344 F.2d 723, 726 (1st Cir. 1965). A new trial is warranted where the verdict is against the great weight of the evidence or where the court committed prejudicial error. *Id.* Here, the district court committed prejudicial error by permitting the introduction of subsequent remedial measures, which improperly influenced the jury’s verdict. *See* *Shepherd*, 340 F.2d at 637.

The district court’s error was compounded by its admonition to the jury to disregard the testimony concerning subsequent remedial measures. *See* R4-56. This admonition was insufficient to cure the prejudice caused by the improper introduction of the evidence. *See* *United States v. Socony-Vacuum Oil Co.*, 310 U.S. 150, 239 (1940) (holding that curative instructions are not always sufficient to remedy prejudicial error). The district court’s error in admitting the evidence and its failure to grant a new trial constitute an abuse of discretion. *See* *Mercadel*, 344 F.2d at 726.

III. THE DISTRICT COURT’S RULING VIOLATED THE DUE PROCESS CLAUSE OF THE FOURTEENTH AMENDMENT.

The district court’s ruling violated Wood’s right to a fair trial under the Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment. *See* *Shepherd*, 340 F.2d at 637 (noting that the admission of subsequent remedial measures can violate due process). The court’s error in permitting the introduction of subsequent remedial measures deprived Wood of a fair trial by allowing the jury to consider evidence that was irrelevant and highly prejudicial. *See id.* This violation of due process warrants a new trial. *See* *Patterson v. New York*, 344 F.2d 713, 716 (2d Cir. 1965) (holding that a violation of due process requires a new trial).

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, Wood respectfully requests that this Court reverse the district court’s judgment and remand the case for a new trial.
