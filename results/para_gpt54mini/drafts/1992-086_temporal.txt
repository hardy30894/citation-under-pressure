The District’s refusal to allow Lamb’s Chapel to use its facilities for an after-hours film series violated the First Amendment because it imposed a content-based exclusion from a public forum without sufficient justification. A school district that opens its property for community expressive use may not single out speech for exclusion because of its religious character. See Hague v. Committee for Industrial Organization, 307 U.S. 496, 515 (1939); Fowler v. Rhode Island, 345 U.S. 67, 69-70 (1953).

This Court has long held that the government may not suppress speech in a public place merely because officials disapprove its subject matter or viewpoint. In Hague, the Court explained that streets and parks have “immemorially been held in trust for the use of the public.” 307 U.S. at 515. When the State opens property for expressive activity, it cannot then discriminate against a speaker based on the ideas conveyed. See Schneider v. State, 308 U.S. 147, 160-61 (1939). The First Amendment forbids officials from making access turn on “the thought” expressed. See Saia v. New York, 334 U.S. 558, 562-63 (1948).

The exclusion here was not a neutral time, place, or manner rule. The District did not deny Lamb’s Chapel because of scheduling conflicts alone; it denied access because the films were “church related” and “for religious purposes.” That is classic content discrimination. In Fowler, the Court invalidated an ordinance enforced to stop Jehovah’s Witnesses from speaking in a public park while allowing other uses of the same place. 345 U.S. at 69-70. The constitutional defect was that officials had “made the statute a means of suppressing the exercise of the right.” Id. at 70. So too here: the District used its facilities policy as a means of suppressing religious expression.

Nor may the District justify discrimination by invoking the Establishment Clause. This Court has repeatedly recognized that government neutrality toward religion is the constitutional norm. Everson v. Board of Education, 330 U.S. 1, 18 (1947). Neutral accommodation of religious speech is not establishment; rather, hostility to religious expression is itself inconsistent with the First Amendment. See McCollum v. Board of Education, 333 U.S. 203, 211-12 (1948). The District’s refusal to open school premises for a film series discussing family values and child rearing from a Christian perspective did not prevent establishment; it imposed exclusion because the speech was religious.

The public character of the property strengthens Lamb’s Chapel’s claim. This Court has held that when the government makes property generally available for public expressive activity, it may not impose selective exclusions that are not justified by the use of the forum. See Marsh v. Alabama, 326 U.S. 501, 506-09 (1946). Even where the State retains ownership, the First Amendment protects expressive access when the place has been opened for community communication. See Schneider, 308 U.S. at 160-61. The District’s premises were made available after hours for community use; once it extended that privilege, it could not reserve the use for favored secular messages while excluding religious ones.

The District’s categorical refusal also infringed the freedom of speech because it burdened the right to convey a message to willing listeners in a place otherwise open for that purpose. This Court has repeatedly protected the right to distribute, discuss, and advocate ideas in public settings against official suppression. See Lovell v. City of Griffin, 303 U.S. 444, 452-53 (1938); Cantwell v. Connecticut, 310 U.S. 296, 303-04 (1940). Lamb’s Chapel sought only to show films on family issues to a voluntary audience after school hours. Nothing in the record suggests disruption, coercion, or any concrete harm that would justify exclusion.

The Constitution does not permit the government to define “religious” speech out of the forum. In Cantwell, the Court held that the First Amendment protects religious advocacy as speech. 310 U.S. at 303-04. In Tucker v. Texas, 326 U.S. 517, 520-21 (1946), the Court again rejected a local rule that burdened religious expression in a public place open to other speakers. The principle is the same here: the District may not bar speech because it is religiously oriented while allowing comparable secular presentations on family life, morality, or social values.

Because the District excluded Lamb’s Chapel based on the religious content of its films, the judgment should be reversed and the requested access ordered.
