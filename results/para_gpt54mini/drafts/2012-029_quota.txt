**Argument**

The Texas Supreme Court erred in holding that 28 U.S.C. § 1338(a) deprives state courts of jurisdiction whenever a legal-malpractice claim requires application of patent law. This Court’s cases establish a narrower rule: federal jurisdiction exists only when a state-law claim necessarily raises a actually disputed and substantial federal issue, and only if federal adjudication will not upset the congressionally approved balance of state and federal responsibilities. *Grable & Sons Metal Products, Inc. v. Darue Engineering & Mfg.*, 545 U.S. 308, 314 (2005); *Christianson v. Colt Industries Operating Corp.*, 486 U.S. 800, 808-09 (1988). That standard is not met here.

First, a malpractice suit does not “arise under” patent law merely because patent law supplies one element of the case-within-a-case analysis. In *Christianson*, the Court held that § 1338 reaches only cases in which “federal patent law creates the cause of action” or the plaintiff’s right to relief “necessarily depends on resolution of a substantial question of federal patent law.” 486 U.S. at 808-09. But the Court also emphasized that jurisdiction must be assessed from the well-pleaded complaint, not from anticipated defenses. *Id.* at 809. Here, Minton’s pleaded claim is classic state legal malpractice, governed by state standards of duty, breach, and causation. *Alexander v. Turtur & Associates, Inc.*, 146 S.W.3d 113, 117 (Tex. 2004); *Schaeffer v. O’Brien*, 39 S.W.3d 719, 720 (Tex. App.—Eastland 2001, pet. denied). Patent law may be evidence in the causation inquiry, but that does not convert the whole action into a patent case.

Second, *Grable* confirms that embedded federal issues do not automatically create federal jurisdiction. The Court described the proper inquiry as whether a state claim “necessarily raise[s] a stated federal issue, actually disputed and substantial, which a federal forum may entertain without disturbing” the federal-state balance. 545 U.S. at 314. In *Merrell Dow Pharmaceuticals Inc. v. Thompson*, 478 U.S. 804, 813-17 (1986), the Court rejected federal jurisdiction where the federal statute supplied an element of a state claim but Congress had not created a federal cause of action. *Grable* preserved that federalism-sensitive approach, limiting federal jurisdiction to “a special and small category” of cases. 545 U.S. at 313-14. Legal malpractice claims are not that category.

Third, the Texas Supreme Court’s rule conflicts with this Court’s repeated warning that federal jurisdiction cannot be based on every case that “involves” a federal issue. *Gully v. First National Bank in Meridian*, 299 U.S. 109, 117-18 (1936), rejected broad “arising under” jurisdiction and instead directed courts to identify only the “substantial” federal controversies. *Shulthis v. McDougal*, 225 U.S. 561, 569 (1912), likewise held that a suit does not arise under federal law unless it “really and substantially involves” a federal dispute whose resolution controls the result. A malpractice action about a lawyer’s litigation choices may require analysis of patent doctrine, but it does not thereby become a federal patent suit.

Fourth, the Federal Circuit’s contrary line of cases should not control because it dilutes *Grable* and expands § 1338 beyond this Court’s precedents. In *Air Measurement Technologies, Inc. v. Akin Gump Strauss Hauer & Feld, L.L.P.*, 504 F.3d 1262, 1272-73 (Fed. Cir. 2007), and *Immunocept, LLC v. Fulbright & Jaworski, L.L.P.*, 504 F.3d 1281, 1285-86 (Fed. Cir. 2007), the Federal Circuit treated patent-malpractice claims as necessarily within exclusive federal jurisdiction. But those cases did not adequately account for *Grable*’s federalism inquiry. Later Federal Circuit decisions repeated that error. *Davis v. Brouse McDowell, L.P.A.*, 596 F.3d 1355, 1360-63 (Fed. Cir. 2010); *Warrior Sports, Inc. v. Dickinson Wright, P.L.L.C.*, 631 F.3d 1367, 1372-73 (Fed. Cir. 2011). By contrast, courts outside the Federal Circuit have correctly recognized that incidental patent issues do not create federal jurisdiction. *New Tek Manufacturing, Inc. v. Beehner*, 270 Neb. 264, 702 N.W.2d 336, 345-46 (2005); *E-Pass Technologies, Inc. v. Moses & Singer, LLP*, 189 Cal. App. 4th 1140, 1152-53, 117 Cal. Rptr. 3d 516 (2010); *Roof Technical Services, Inc. v. Hill*, 679 F. Supp. 2d 749, 753-54 (N.D. Tex. 2010); *Genelink Biosciences, Inc. v. Colby*, 722 F. Supp. 2d 592, 595-97 (D.N.J. 2010); *Danner, Inc. v. Foley & Lardner, LLP*, No. 09-1220-JE, 2010 WL 2608292, at *4-*5 (D. Or. Mar. 15, 2010).

Fifth, jurisdiction here would upset the balance between state and federal courts. Legal malpractice is a core area of state regulation. *Gunn v. Minton*, 568 U.S. 251, 264-66 (2013), later confirmed that adjudicating a patent issue as a “case within a case” does not threaten the uniform body of patent law because the issue is backward-looking and its resolution has no broader precedential effect on patent validity or infringement. That reasoning is consistent with *Sperry v. Florida ex rel. Florida Bar*, 373 U.S. 379, 402 (1963), which recognized state authority over professional regulation except where Congress clearly displaces it. It also aligns with *Morrison v. National Australia Bank Ltd.*, 561 U.S. 247, 254-55 (2010), which cautioned against reading federal statutes to displace state-law causes of action absent clear congressional direction.

This Court should hold that the presence of a patent-law issue in a state legal-malpractice case does not automatically confer exclusive federal jurisdiction. The judgment below should be reversed.
