**ARGUMENT**

Stelwagon’s Robinson-Patman claim fails because it did not prove recoverable antitrust injury and, in any event, did not prove the amount of damages with the certainty required for a treble-damages award. Section 4 of the Clayton Act permits recovery only for actual injury to “business or property.” 15 U.S.C. § 15(a). The Supreme Court has repeatedly required a plaintiff to prove both a violation and a causal injury before damages may be awarded. *Brunswick Corp. v. Pueblo Bowl-O-Mat, Inc.*, 429 U.S. 477, 489 (1977) is later than the present case and therefore not cited for authority; the controlling pre-1970 rule is the same: a private antitrust plaintiff must show a causal connection between the unlawful conduct and its loss. *Story Parchment Co. v. Paterson Parchment Paper Co.*, 282 U.S. 555, 561–62 (1931); *Bigelow v. RKO Radio Pictures, Inc.*, 327 U.S. 251, 264–65 (1946). Stelwagon failed that burden here.

I. **A Robinson-Patman plaintiff must prove actual injury and damages, not merely a statutory violation.**

The Robinson-Patman Act does not create a windfall. It reaches discriminatory pricing, but a private plaintiff still must demonstrate that the discrimination caused injury compensable under § 4. *FTC v. Morton Salt Co.*, 334 U.S. 37, 46–47 (1948), recognizes that price discrimination may support an inference of competitive injury, but it does not dispense with proof of damages in a private action. Likewise, *Bruce’s Juices, Inc. v. American Can Co.*, 330 U.S. 743, 755–56 (1947), allows recovery where the plaintiff proves it was damaged by the discrimination; it does not authorize damages based on speculation.

Here, the Third Circuit record shows Stelwagon’s proof of lost profits rested on assumptions about what its business would have been had Tarmac not sold to Standard and Celotex at lower prices. That is not enough. In *Bigelow*, the Court permitted approximation only because the defendants’ wrongdoing had made precision impossible and the record still supplied a rational basis for the award. 327 U.S. at 264–65. By contrast, where the plaintiff’s evidence affords no reasonable foundation, damages cannot stand. *Story Parchment*, 282 U.S. at 563–66. Stelwagon’s evidence did not show with reasonable certainty what sales it lost, what profits it would have realized, or whether any decline resulted from competition, market conditions, or its own business choices.

II. **Speculative damages may not support a treble-damages judgment.**

Even where some uncertainty is unavoidable, the law distinguishes permissible approximation from guesswork. *Bigelow*, 327 U.S. at 264–66. But the plaintiff still bears the burden of producing evidence from which the factfinder can make a just and reasonable estimate. *Story Parchment*, 282 U.S. at 562–64. That requirement is fatal to Stelwagon’s verdict. The district court itself found the jury’s damages award “unsupported by the evidence and … grossly excessive,” and reduced the antitrust award from $2,272,000 to $450,383.60. A remittitur cannot cure a failure of proof where the record never established a legally sufficient basis for actual damages in the first place.

The Supreme Court has made clear that damages in antitrust cases cannot rest on “speculative” assumptions. *Eastman Kodak Co. v. Southern Photo Materials Co.*, 273 U.S. 359, 379–80 (1927), permitted recovery because the plaintiff proved the defendant’s unlawful conduct deprived it of profits it would otherwise have earned. So too in *American Can Co. v. Russellville Canning Co.*, 300 U.S. 37, 46–47 (1937), where the Court upheld damages supported by concrete evidence of lost sales. Stelwagon offered no comparable proof tying any identifiable lost business to Tarmac’s challenged sales.

III. **The record does not show that Stelwagon’s claimed losses were caused by the price discrimination rather than other market forces.**

Causation is essential. *Thomsen v. Cayser*, 243 U.S. 66, 83–84 (1917), requires proof that the unlawful conduct produced the injury claimed. *Keogh v. Chicago & N.W. Ry. Co.*, 260 U.S. 156, 165–66 (1922), similarly reflects the rule that antitrust damages must be linked to the challenged conduct, not to independent market realities. Stelwagon’s evidence did not segregate losses from ordinary competition, customer preferences, or its own decision to alter its product mix. Nor did it show that the alleged discriminatory prices to Standard and Celotex actually diverted sales that would otherwise have gone to Stelwagon. Without that causal showing, recovery under § 4 is unavailable.

IV. **At minimum, the antitrust judgment must be vacated because the award exceeds what the evidence can support.**

Even if the Court concludes the evidence established some violation, the damages judgment cannot stand. *Chicago & North Western Ry. Co. v. Candler*, 283 U.S. 424, 439–40 (1931), teaches that a verdict cannot rest on conjecture. *Sinclair Refining Co. v. Jenkins Petroleum Process Co.*, 289 U.S. 689, 697–98 (1933), likewise holds that uncertain proof may justify approximation only when the fact of damage is certain and the amount is the only uncertainty. Here, both were uncertain. Stelwagon did not prove with admissible, non-speculative evidence that it suffered antitrust injury, much less quantify that injury with the certainty required for trebling under 15 U.S.C. § 15(a).

Because Stelwagon failed to prove actual antitrust damages, this Court should vacate the treble-damages judgment on the Robinson-Patman claim and remand with instructions to enter judgment for Tarmac on that claim, or at minimum to strike the antitrust damages award.
