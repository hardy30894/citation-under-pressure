The Double Jeopardy Clause of the Fifth Amendment, made applicable to the States through the Fourteenth Amendment, bars retrial after a determination that the evidence is insufficient to sustain an allegation increasing punishment. This protection extends to proceedings determining the truth of prior conviction allegations because such a determination constitutes an acquittal on the facts necessary to impose enhanced punishment.

The Clause “protects against a second prosecution for the same offence after acquittal.” Ex parte Lange, 85 U.S. (18 Wall.) 163, 168 (1873). It “forbids a second trial for the same offence” once the defendant has been acquitted. United States v. Ball, 163 U.S. 662, 671 (1896). An acquittal “is final, and may not be reviewed.” Kepner v. United States, 195 U.S. 100, 130 (1904). The government may not “subject the defendant to a second trial” after a finding that the evidence fails to establish the charge. Grafton v. United States, 206 U.S. 333, 345 (1907). Where the prosecution fails to prove an essential fact, “the defendant is entitled to the benefit of the doubt,” and retrial is precluded. In re Nielsen, 131 U.S. 176, 190 (1889).

This principle applies whenever the proceeding requires proof of facts beyond a reasonable doubt to impose additional punishment. A determination that the evidence is insufficient “is tantamount to an acquittal” and bars further proceedings on the same allegation. Green v. United States, 355 U.S. 184, 190 (1957). The Clause prevents the State from “mak[ing] repeated attempts to convict” after an adverse factual finding. Fong Foo v. United States, 369 U.S. 141, 143 (1962). Retrial is forbidden once “the first jury has already decided the question of guilt or innocence.” United States v. Tateo, 377 U.S. 463, 466 (1964).

The same rule governs when the State seeks to prove facts that would increase the term of imprisonment. After an acquittal on the facts necessary for greater punishment, “the double jeopardy clause . . . requires that the practice . . . be forbidden.” North Carolina v. Pearce, 395 U.S. 711, 717 (1969).
