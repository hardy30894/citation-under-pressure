**ARGUMENT**

The Controlled Substances Act’s temporary scheduling provision does not unconstitutionally delegate legislative power. Congress supplied an “intelligible principle” to guide the Attorney General, satisfying the constitutional standard for delegation. *J.W. Hampton, Jr., & Co. v. United States*, 276 U.S. 394, 409 (1928). The nondelegation doctrine requires only that Congress “lay down by legislative act an intelligible principle to which the person or body authorized to [act] is directed to conform.” *Id.* This Court has consistently upheld delegations containing far less guidance than the detailed criteria in 21 U.S.C. § 811(h). *Yakus v. United States*, 321 U.S. 414, 424-27 (1944) (upholding delegation to set maximum prices under broad standards of “fair and equitable” and “effectuating the purposes of this Act”); *Fahey v. Mallonee*, 332 U.S. 245, 250 (1947) (upholding delegation to regulate banking under “well-known and generally acceptable standards” in the field); *Lichter v. United States*, 334 U.S. 742, 785-86 (1948) (upholding delegation to recover “excessive profits”); *United States v. Grimaud*, 220 U.S. 506, 521 (1911) (upholding delegation to promulgate regulations for national forests). The Court has emphasized that “the degree of specificity required depends on the complexity of the subject matter and the need for flexibility.” *Yakus*, 321 U.S. at 432.

Here, Congress provided an **intelligible principle** through multiple constraints. Section 811(h) limits temporary scheduling to substances that pose “an imminent hazard to the public safety,” requires consideration of three specific statutory factors (history and pattern of abuse, scope and significance of abuse, and risk to public health), mandates publication of notice in the Federal Register, requires transmission to the Secretary of Health and Human Services for comment, and restricts the duration to one year with a possible six-month extension. 21 U.S.C. § 811(h)(1)-(4). These standards far exceed the “fair and equitable” formula upheld in *Yakus*. Moreover, the Secretary’s binding recommendation under § 811(b) for permanent scheduling—incorporated by reference in the temporary process—provides an additional check. This combination of substantive criteria, procedural safeguards, and temporal limits satisfies the constitutional test.

The two cases invalidating delegations—*Panama Refining Co. v. Ryan*, 293 U.S. 388 (1935), and *A.L.A. Schechter Poultry Corp. v. United States*, 295 U.S. 495 (1935)—are inapposite. In *Panama Refining*, Congress declared no policy and supplied “no criterion to govern the President’s course.” 293 U.S. at 415. In *Schechter Poultry*, the statute allowed the President to approve “codes of fair competition” with virtually no standards. 295 U.S. at 541-42. Here, Congress expressly declared the policy of protecting public safety from hazardous drugs, enumerated specific factors, and imposed procedural requirements—all absent in those cases. *Cf. Field v. Clark*, 143 U.S. 649, 693 (1892) (upholding delegation conditioned upon findings of fact); *Buttfield v. Stranahan*, 192 U.S. 470, 496 (1904) (upholding delegation “to prescribe uniform standards of purity and quality” for tea).

The delegation here also involves **power to define criminal conduct**. But that fact does not heighten the standard. *Grimaud* upheld a delegation that authorized the Secretary to make it a crime to graze sheep on forest reserves without a permit. 220 U.S. at 522-23. The Court reasoned that Congress “may, in the exercise of its power over the public domain, delegate to the Secretary the authority to make regulations” and that violation could be punished as a crime. *Id.* at 521-22. Similarly, Congress delegated to the Attorney General the authority to temporarily schedule substances, and violation is criminal only if the substance poses the imminent hazard Congress itself defined. The “intelligible principle” test applies equally to delegations involving criminal penalties. *Yakus*, 321 U.S. at 423-26 (upholding criminal penalties for violating price controls).

Finally, **subdelegation to the Drug Enforcement Administration** was authorized. Congress vested scheduling authority in the Attorney General, and the Act expressly permits him to “delegate any of his functions” to officers of the Department of Justice. 21 U.S.C. § 871(a). This Court has long recognized that “the head of a department may, unless Congress otherwise provides, delegate his powers to subordinate officials.” *Runkle v. United States*, 19 Wall. 86, 97 (1873); *United States v. Barnow*, 239 U.S. 74, 77-78 (1915) (upholding subdelegation of authority to enforce immigration laws). No provision of the CSA prohibits subdelegation, and the Attorney General’s regulation—28 C.F.R. § 0.100(b)—properly assigns those functions to the DEA Administrator. The temporary scheduling of Euphoria was therefore lawful.

The judgment should be affirmed.
