# ARGUMENT

## I. Exemption 2's Plain Text Limits It to Rules and Practices Concerning Agency Personnel, Not Explosives Data

FOIA embodies "a general philosophy of full agency disclosure unless information is exempted under clearly delineated statutory language." *Dep't of the Air Force v. Rose*, 425 U.S. 352, 360-61 (1976). Exemption 2 covers matters "related solely to the internal personnel rules and practices of an agency." 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(2). The words matter. "Personnel rules and practices" means rules and practices about *personnel*—how employees are hired, trained, disciplined, and managed—not any information an agency happens to keep for internal use. The Ninth Circuit's "High 2" doctrine, borrowed from *Crooker v. Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco & Firearms*, 670 F.2d 1051 (D.C. Cir. 1981) (en banc), abandons this textual anchor by asking only whether material is "predominantly internal" and whether disclosure risks "circumvention of agency regulation." That test has no mooring in the statute Congress wrote.

This Court has instructed that FOIA's nine exemptions are "explicitly exclusive," *U.S. Dep't of Justice v. Tax Analysts*, 492 U.S. 136, 151 (1989) (quoting *Adm'r, FAA v. Robertson*, 422 U.S. 255, 262 (1975)), and must be construed narrowly to preserve "the basic policy that disclosure, not secrecy, is the dominant objective of the Act." *Rose*, 425 U.S. at 361. The Ninth Circuit's approach inverts that principle. ESQD data—arcs showing the blast radius of stored ordnance—says nothing about "personnel." It is technical, physical information about explosives and geography. That the Navy finds it useful for internal planning does not transform it into a personnel rule any more than a building's structural blueprints become "personnel" material because engineers use them internally.

*Rose* itself confirms the narrow scope Congress intended. There, the Court explained that Exemption 2's primary concern was mundane matters like "parking facilities, lunch hours, and sick leave"—"Low 2" material of no "genuine and significant public interest." *Rose*, 425 U.S. at 369. The Court reserved, but did not decide, whether more substantial "personnel" matters might also qualify. It never suggested that Exemption 2 could swallow whole categories of substantive agency data merely because release might complicate the agency's operations.

## II. The Ninth Circuit's "Predominantly Internal" Standard Improperly Expands Exemption 2 Beyond Any Coherent Limiting Principle

The Ninth Circuit's decision below, adopting *Crooker*'s "predominantly internal" gloss, creates a standard so elastic that virtually any document an agency prefers not to release can be recast as an "internal" matter presenting a "risk of circumvention." That is precisely the danger this Court warned against in *Tax Analysts*, where it rejected expansive readings of FOIA exemptions in favor of interpretations tied to statutory text. 492 U.S. at 151.

Courts applying the "predominantly internal" test have struggled to cabin it. In *Schiller v. NLRB*, 964 F.2d 1205, 1208 (D.C. Cir. 1992), the D.C. Circuit stretched Exemption 2 to cover litigation strategy documents—materials with no connection whatsoever to personnel administration. If litigation strategy qualifies as a "personnel rule or practice," then the term has lost all meaning. The Ninth Circuit's extension of this logic to blast-radius calculations for munitions storage confirms the point: once courts abandon the statutory anchor of "personnel," Exemption 2 becomes a freestanding "sensitive information" exemption that Congress never enacted.

This Court's decision in *United States Department of State v. Ray*, 502 U.S. 164, 173 (1991), places the burden squarely on the agency to justify withholding under a "clearly delineated" exemption—not on the requester to disprove some amorphous internal-use rationale. The Navy cannot satisfy that burden by showing only that ESQD data assists internal planning; it must show the data constitutes a rule or practice governing agency personnel. It cannot.

Notably, Ninth Circuit precedent prior to the decision below recognized the proper limits of Exemption 2. In *Hardy v. Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco & Firearms*, 631 F.2d 653, 656 (9th Cir. 1980), the court held that "law enforcement materials, the disclosure of which may risk circumvention of agency regulation, are exempt under Exemption 2"—a holding tied to the specific context of law-enforcement manuals, not a general license to withhold any internally useful document. Similarly, in *Maricopa Audubon Society v. United States Forest Service*, 108 F.3d 1082, 1085 (9th Cir. 1997), the court held that goshawk nesting-site data did not "relate 'solely,' or even predominantly, 'to the internal personnel rules and practices of an agency,'" reflecting an understanding that Exemption 2 requires a genuine nexus to personnel matters. The decision below abandons that careful lineage and, as the dissent recognized, risks converting Exemption 2 into a substitute for exemptions—like Exemption 1's classification regime—that Congress designed with far more rigorous procedural safeguards. As the dissenting judge observed, the Navy's failure to classify the ESQD maps under Exemption 1 undercuts its claim that disclosure would truly jeopardize security, and the majority's approach "distorts Congress's careful balance" between transparency and legitimate secrecy.

## III. The Circumvention Rationale Cannot Substitute for the Statutory "Personnel" Requirement

Even if some risk-of-circumvention concern is relevant, it cannot operate independently of the statute's personnel requirement. This Court's framework requires that withheld information first fall within the "clearly delineated statutory language," *Rose*, 425 U.S. at 361, before any circumvention rationale comes into play. The Ninth Circuit's test collapses these two independent requirements into one balancing inquiry, allowing the circumvention concern to do work the "personnel" limitation was designed to prevent.

ESQD arc data is not a "personnel rule." It is scientific and geographic information about explosive effects—information of substantial public interest to residents living near Naval Magazine Indian Island who have a direct stake in understanding the risks in their community. Because Exemption 2, properly construed, does not reach this material, the judgment below should be reversed.
