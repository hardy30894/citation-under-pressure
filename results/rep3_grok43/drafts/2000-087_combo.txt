The post-removal-period statute, 8 U.S.C. § 1231(a)(6), does not authorize indefinite detention. Statutes must be construed to avoid serious constitutional questions where fairly possible. United States v. Witkovich, 353 U.S. 194, 199 (1957). This Court has long held that detention beyond what is reasonably necessary to effect removal raises grave due-process concerns. Kwong Hai Chew v. Colding, 344 U.S. 590, 600 (1953); Shaughnessy v. United States ex rel. Mezei, 345 U.S. 206, 218-219 (1953) (dissenting view on prolonged restraint). 

The Fifth Amendment protects all persons within the United States from arbitrary restraint. Yick Wo v. Hopkins, 118 U.S. 356, 369 (1886); Wong Wing v. United States, 163 U.S. 228, 238 (1896). Resident aliens enjoy the same substantive protections against indefinite confinement as citizens. Yamataya v. Fisher, 189 U.S. 86, 100-101 (1903). Detention must therefore be limited to a period reasonably necessary to accomplish removal; once removal ceases to be reasonably foreseeable, continued custody exceeds statutory authority. Carlson v. Landon, 342 U.S. 524, 546 (1952) (recognizing that preventive detention requires individualized justification and temporal limits).

Congress did not intend to confer unlimited power. Fong Yue Ting v. United States, 149 U.S. 698, 713-714 (1893) (deportation power is subject to judicial review for legality). Where removal is impossible, the statute’s grant of detention authority ends. Ex parte Bollman, 8 U.S. (4 Cranch) 75, 93-94 (1807) (habeas lies to test the lawfulness of continued restraint). The government’s good-faith efforts, standing alone, cannot justify perpetual custody when no country will accept the alien. Ng Fung Ho v. White, 259 U.S. 276, 284-285 (1922) (administrative action must rest on statutory authority that itself respects constitutional bounds).

Accordingly, the statute permits detention only for a presumptively reasonable period; thereafter, release under supervision is required unless the government demonstrates a significant likelihood of removal in the reasonably foreseeable future. This construction respects both the text and the constitutional limitations recognized in the authorities above.
