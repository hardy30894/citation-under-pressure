I. The Ohio Adult Parole Authority's Clemency Procedure Implicates a Protected Life and Liberty Interest.

The Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment prohibits a state from depriving any person of life or liberty without due process of law. The Court has recognized that certain state-created liberty interests are protected by the Due Process Clause. See Board of Pardons v. Allen, 482 U.S. 369 (1987); Greenholtz v. Inmates of the Nebraska Penal & Correctional Complex, 442 U.S. 1 (1979). While the Court has held that a state's clemency procedure does not itself create a protected liberty interest, see Connecticut Board of Pardons v. Dumschat, 452 U.S. 458 (1981), it has also suggested that a state's clemency procedure can be an integral part of its adjudicatory process, implicating due process protections. See Evitts v. Lucey, 469 U.S. 387 (1985).

Ohio's clemency procedure is an integral part of its death penalty adjudicatory process. The Ohio Constitution empowers the governor to grant reprieves, commutations, and pardons, and the Ohio Adult Parole Authority plays a critical role in the clemency process. See Ohio Const. art. III, § 11; Ohio Rev. Code § 2967.07. The Authority's clemency review is a mandatory step in the process, occurring 45 days prior to the scheduled execution date. See Ohio Dep't of Rehab. & Corr. Reg. (Death Penalty Clemency Procedure). This process is not merely a matter of executive grace, but rather an essential component of the state's system for adjudicating the guilt or innocence of a defendant.

As the Court noted in Evitts, when a state elects to create a procedure as an integral part of its adjudicatory process, that procedure must meet the demands of the Due Process Clause. 469 U.S. at 393. Ohio's clemency procedure, as an integral part of its death penalty adjudicatory process, implicates a protected life and liberty interest. The minimal due process protections that attach to this interest are not "barely perceptible," as the Sixth Circuit suggested. Rather, they are essential to ensuring that the clemency process is fair and not arbitrary.

II. The Ohio Adult Parole Authority's Clemency Procedure Violates the Petitioner's Fifth Amendment Rights.

The Fifth Amendment protects an individual from being compelled to be a witness against himself. The Ohio Adult Parole Authority's clemency procedure, which offers a petitioner the option to participate in a personal interview, raises the specter of an unconstitutional condition. By participating in the interview, a petitioner may risk incriminating himself, potentially prejudicing his future collateral attacks on his conviction or sentence.

The Court has recognized that certain government actions can impermissibly burden a constitutional right, such as the Fifth Amendment privilege against self-incrimination. See United States v. Washington, 431 U.S. 181 (1977). While the Ohio clemency regulation on its face does not compel a prisoner to answer questions, the reality is that a petitioner may feel pressured to participate in the interview to demonstrate contrition or to present mitigating evidence.

The Sixth Circuit's suggestion that the risk of self-incrimination is "premature" and "purely academic" is unfounded. The petitioner has a legitimate concern that his statements during the interview could be used against him in future proceedings. The Court should remand this case to the District Court to analyze the petitioner's specific arguments and determine the nature of the APA's clemency procedures.

In conclusion, the Ohio Adult Parole Authority's clemency procedure implicates a protected life and liberty interest and violates the petitioner's Fifth Amendment rights. The Court should reverse the Sixth Circuit's judgment and remand this case for further proceedings consistent with the principles outlined above.
