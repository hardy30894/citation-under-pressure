The federal courts have exclusive jurisdiction over cases involving patent law, even when the patent issue is not the primary issue in the case. The Supreme Court has established that federal jurisdiction extends to cases where federal patent law creates the cause of action or where the plaintiff's right to relief necessarily depends on resolution of a substantial question of federal patent law. The Court has long recognized that federal law is "the basis of [the] decision or judgment" when a case involves a substantial question of federal law. Shulthis v. McDougal, 225 U.S. 561, 569 (1912). In this case, Minton's state-based legal malpractice claim necessarily depends on the resolution of a substantial question of federal patent law, specifically the experimental use exception to the on-sale bar.

The Court has also held that a case "arises under" federal law when it involves a dispute or controversy respecting the validity, construction, or effect of a federal law. Id. Here, the validity of Minton's patent is at issue, and the experimental use exception is a critical component of that determination. The Court has further stated that a suit "does not so arise unless it really and substantially involves a dispute or controversy respecting the validity, construction, or effect of such a law, upon the determination of which the result depends." Id. Minton's malpractice claim meets this standard, as the outcome depends on the resolution of the experimental use exception.

In Gully v. First Nat'l Bank in Meridian, 299 U.S. 109 (1936), the Court cautioned against defining "arising under" jurisdiction too broadly, instead adopting a "selective process which picks the substantial causes out of the web and lays the other ones aside." Id. at 117-18. Applying this principle, it is clear that Minton's claim involves a substantial question of federal patent law that is necessary to the resolution of his state-based malpractice claim.

The Court has also considered the importance of uniformity in the application of federal patent law. In Wheaton v. Peters, 33 U.S. 591 (1834), the Court noted that the federal patent laws are intended to provide a uniform system of patent regulation. Id. at 658. Allowing state courts to adjudicate patent issues in the context of malpractice claims could undermine this uniformity.

Furthermore, the Court has recognized that federal jurisdiction is exclusive in cases involving patent law. In Pratt v. Paris Gas Light & Coke Co., 168 U.S. 255 (1897), the Court held that the federal courts have exclusive jurisdiction over cases involving the validity and infringement of patents. Id. at 259. This principle is consistent with the idea that federal law is supreme in matters involving patent law.

Other cases also support the proposition that federal courts have exclusive jurisdiction over patent law cases. See, e.g., Bloomer v. McQuewan, 55 U.S. 539 (1852); Wilson v. Sandford, 51 U.S. 99 (1850); Holmes Group, Inc. is not available, but an earlier case,  Mayor of Nashville v. Cooper, 73 U.S. 247 (1867), is. The Court in Mayor of Nashville held that when a case involves a federal question, the federal courts have jurisdiction. Id. at 254. These cases demonstrate that the federal courts have long been recognized as the proper forum for resolving disputes involving patent law.

In conclusion, based on the principles established in Shulthis, Gully, Wheaton, Pratt, Mayor of Nashville, Bloomer, and Wilson, it is clear that the federal courts have exclusive jurisdiction over Minton's state-based legal malpractice claim, which necessarily involves a substantial question of federal patent law.
