**Argument**

**I. Ohio’s clemency process does not create a protected life or liberty interest.**  
The Due Process Clause protects against deprivation of life, liberty, or property without due process, but not every state procedure gives rise to a federally enforceable entitlement. In *Connecticut Board of Pardons v. Dumschat*, the Court held that a prisoner has no liberty interest in commutation because “pardon and commutation decisions” are not traditionally judicial matters and are “rarely, if ever, appropriate subjects for judicial review.” 452 U.S. 458, 464 (1981). The Court rejected the claim that a state’s clemency practice itself could trigger due process merely because the prisoner hoped for a favorable result. Id. at 464-67.

That controlling principle defeats respondent’s due process claim here. Ohio’s Constitution vests clemency in the Governor’s discretion, and nothing in the Ohio scheme cabins that discretion with substantive standards creating a legitimate expectation of relief. As *Dumschat* makes clear, absent a state-created entitlement, “a unilateral hope” of clemency is not a protected interest. 452 U.S. at 465. The Sixth Circuit correctly recognized that respondent had no life or liberty interest “in Ohio’s clemency procedure itself.” *Ohio Adult Parole Authority v. Woodard*, 107 F.3d 1178, 1186 (6th Cir. 1997), vacated, 523 U.S. 272 (1998).

Respondent’s attempt to recast clemency as part of the adjudicatory process fares no better. In *Evitts v. Lucey*, the Court held that when a State creates a first appeal as of right, due process applies because that appeal is “an integral part of the ... system for finally adjudicating the guilt or innocence of a defendant.” 469 U.S. 387, 393 (1985). But *Evitts* expressly addressed an appeal as of right, not executive clemency. Id. at 393-94. The Court has never extended *Evitts* to discretionary executive mercy, and doing so would collide with *Dumschat*’s core holding. See also *Ross v. Moffitt*, 417 U.S. 600, 611-12 (1974) (due process does not require States to duplicate all appellate safeguards beyond the first appeal as of right).

**II. Providing a voluntary clemency interview does not violate the Fifth Amendment.**  
The Fifth Amendment is violated only when the State compels a person to be a witness against himself. U.S. Const. amend. V; *Baxter v. Palmigiano*, 425 U.S. 308, 316-18 (1976). Ohio’s procedure does not compel testimony. As the regulation described below shows, the inmate may request an interview, but he is not required to answer any question, much less to waive the privilege. The mere opportunity to speak in support of clemency is not compulsion.

This Court’s decisions distinguish between permissible pressure to choose whether to speak and unconstitutional coercion. In *Baxter*, the Court held that adverse consequences may follow from silence in a civil context without violating the Fifth Amendment, because the privilege protects against compelled self-incrimination, not all incentives to speak. 425 U.S. at 318-20. Likewise, in *McKune v. Lile*, the Court later reaffirmed that the Constitution does not prohibit all difficult choices incident to prison administration; the relevant question is whether the State has imposed compulsion in the constitutional sense. 536 U.S. 24, 41-48 (2002).

Ohio’s clemency interview does not rise to that level. The prisoner may decline the interview, or may attend and remain silent. There is no forced testimony, no mandated waiver, and no penalty for invoking the privilege built into the regulation. A voluntary interview that may assist the Governor in exercising discretion is not the functional equivalent of compelled self-incrimination. See *Minnesota v. Murphy*, 465 U.S. 420, 427-34 (1984).

Nor does the possibility that a prisoner’s voluntary statements might later be used in some future proceeding establish a present Fifth Amendment violation. Speculation about downstream use cannot substitute for actual compulsion. See *United States v. Verdugo-Urquidez*, 494 U.S. 259, 264 (1990). As Judge Nelson observed below, Ohio’s regulation “simply does not require a waiver.” *Woodard*, 107 F.3d at 1191 (Nelson, J., concurring in part and dissenting in part).

**Conclusion**  
The Court should hold that respondent has no protected life or liberty interest in clemency proceedings and that Ohio’s voluntary interview option does not violate the Fifth Amendment. The judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
