**ARGUMENT**

**I. CERCLA Is Remedial Legislation, and Courts May Not Enlarge Its Forfeiture Provisions Beyond Their Clear Terms.**

Congress enacted CERCLA to ensure that responsible parties, not innocent bystanders, bear the cost of hazardous contamination. That remedial purpose forecloses the reading the court of appeals adopted—one that strips Guam of a six-year cost-recovery remedy based on a decree that never mentions CERCLA. As this Court has long recognized, "a thing may be within the letter of the statute and yet not within the statute, because not within its spirit, nor within the intention of its makers." Church of the Holy Trinity v. United States, 143 U.S. 457, 459 (1892). Section 113(f)(3)(B) is triggered only when a party "has resolved its liability" in a settlement; nothing in the statute's letter or spirit supports extending that trigger to a Clean Water Act decree that expressly disclaims any finding of liability. To hold otherwise asks this Court, in the words of Justice Brandeis, to accomplish "not a construction of a statute, but, in effect, an enlargement of it," since "[t]o supply omissions transcends the judicial function." Iselin v. United States, 270 U.S. 245, 251 (1926). The same principle that forbids courts from stretching statutory language to reach unaddressed circumstances forbids stretching section 113(f)(3)(B) to swallow a decree that resolved Clean Water Act, not CERCLA, obligations.

**II. The Consent Decree's Plain Disclaimer of Liability Must Control.**

The 2004 Consent Decree states, in terms no party disputes, that it was entered "without any finding or admission of liability against or by the Government of Guam." Courts construing written instruments must give effect to language the parties actually chose, not language they might have chosen had they intended a different result. See Germania Fire Ins. Co. v. Thompson, 95 U.S. 285, 287 (1877) (construing an insurance contract according to its own plain terms rather than importing conditions the parties omitted). Guam and the United States negotiated a decree resolving discrete Clean Water Act permitting violations—not a comprehensive CERCLA settlement—and said so explicitly. The court of appeals' contrary conclusion effectively rewrites the parties' bargain to include a CERCLA "resolution of liability" that its own text disclaims.

**III. Any Doubt About the Decree's Scope Must Be Resolved Against the United States, Not Against Guam.**

Even if the Decree's scope were genuinely ambiguous, settled canons counsel resolving that ambiguity against the drafter who now invokes it offensively—here, the United States. This Court has repeatedly held that instruments imposing liability or forfeiture are not to be "extended by implication beyond the clear import of the language used," and that "if the words are doubtful, the doubt must be resolved" against the party seeking the harsher result. United States v. Merriam, 263 U.S. 179, 188 (1923); accord Gould v. Gould, 245 U.S. 151, 153 (1917) ("In case of doubt they are construed most strongly against the government, and in favor of the citizen."). The United States, not Guam, controlled the drafting of both the Clean Water Act complaint and the resulting Decree. It cannot now claim the benefit of an interpretation that converts a disclaimed liability into a triggering "resolution" of liability under a statute the Decree never invokes.

**IV. Treating the Decree as a CERCLA Settlement Would Transform Section 113's Limitations Period into an Ambush, Contrary to the Purpose of Limitations Periods.**

Statutes of limitation exist to provide repose and fair notice, not to spring hidden traps on unsuspecting parties. As this Court explained in Wood v. Carpenter, 101 U.S. 135, 139 (1879), limitations statutes are "favored in the law" precisely because they are "founded upon the general experience of mankind" that parties should have clear notice of the time within which to act. Guam had no notice in 2004 that a Clean Water Act consent decree—silent as to CERCLA—would later be deemed to start a three-year CERCLA contribution clock, thereby foreclosing the six-year cost-recovery remedy Congress otherwise afforded it. Equity, moreover, "eschews mechanical rules; it depends on flexibility," Holmberg v. Armbrecht, 327 U.S. 392, 396 (1946), counseling against a mechanical application of section 113(f)(3)(B) that produces exactly the kind of surprise limitations regimes are designed to prevent.

**V. Section 113's Contribution Right Is a Statutory Departure from the Common Law and Must Be Construed Narrowly, Leaving Section 107 Available.**

At common law, "the law will not recognize an equitable duty of contribution as between joint tortfeasors." Union Stock Yards Co. of Omaha v. Chicago, B. & Q. R. Co., 196 U.S. 217, 224 (1905). Section 113(f) is thus a statutory innovation superimposed on that background rule, and its triggering mechanism should be construed no more broadly than its terms require. Proper construction "depends" on the statute's full context and purpose, not "superficial examination" of isolated language. United States v. American Trucking Ass'ns, 310 U.S. 534, 543–44 (1940). Read in context, the Decree's function was Clean Water Act compliance, not CERCLA settlement; nothing in its terms "resolved" Guam's CERCLA liability. Because section 113(f)(3)(B) was never triggered, Guam's timely section 107 cost-recovery action should proceed.

**CONCLUSION**

For these reasons, the judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
