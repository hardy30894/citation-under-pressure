The United States respectfully submits that the Court of Appeals correctly determined that Guam's claim under CERCLA section 107 is barred because the 2004 Consent Decree triggered Guam's right to pursue a contribution claim under section 113(f)(3)(B). As the D.C. Circuit held, the Consent Decree "resolve[d] [Guam's] liability to the United States for some or all of a response action," thereby precluding Guam from seeking cost recovery under section 107.

The history of Guam's relationship with the United States is relevant to understanding the context of this case. As the Supreme Court has noted, when the United States acquires territory through conquest or cession, the municipal laws of the former sovereign remain in force until abrogated or changed by the new ruler (Vilas v. City of Manila, 220 U.S. 345, 357 (1911)). Upon acquiring Guam, the United States treated it as a naval ship, exercising military rule until the passage of the Guam Organic Act in 1950 (Burns v. Wilson, 346 U.S. 137, 141 (1953)). Guam remains an "unincorporated territory of the United States" to this day (48 U.S.C. § 1421a).

The Ordot Dump, constructed and operated by the Navy, was used for the disposal of municipal and military waste, including hazardous substances. The Navy's actions, taken during a time when Guam was under U.S. military rule, are attributable to the United States. As the Supreme Court has held, the United States is responsible for the actions of its military authorities, even when they are taken in a territory that is not a state (Duncan v. Kahanamoku, 327 U.S. 304, 314 (1946)).

The Comprehensive Environmental Response, Compensation, and Liability Act (CERCLA) provides a framework for addressing environmental hazards such as those posed by the Ordot Dump. Section 107 allows for cost-recovery actions, while section 113(f) permits contribution claims. The distinction between these two provisions is critical, as the statute of limitations for a contribution action is three years, compared to six years for a remedial section 107 action (CERCLA § 113(g)(3); id. § 113(g)(2)(B)).

The 2004 Consent Decree between Guam and the EPA, which resolved Guam's liability for Clean Water Act violations related to the Ordot Dump, triggered Guam's right to pursue a contribution claim under section 113(f)(3)(B). As the D.C. Circuit correctly held, the Consent Decree's substantive provisions, including the requirement that Guam close the Ordot Dump and design a cover system, demonstrate that Guam's liability was resolved (see Kern-Limerick, Inc. v. Scurlock, 347 U.S. 110, 122 (1954) (examining the terms of an agreement to determine its effect)).

The Supreme Court has long recognized that settlements between the government and potentially responsible parties can have significant consequences for the rights and obligations of all parties involved (see National Labor Relations Board v. Jones & Laughlin Steel Corp., 331 U.S. 416, 428 (1947) (noting the importance of considering the "practical implications" of a settlement)). In this case, the Consent Decree's resolution of Guam's liability has the effect of barring Guam's section 107 claim, as Guam is now limited to pursuing a contribution claim under section 113(f)(3)(B).

The Court should affirm the D.C. Circuit's judgment, as it is consistent with the principles of statutory interpretation and the relevant case law. The decision is also supported by the long-standing precedent that the United States is responsible for the actions of its military authorities in territories under its control (see Pollard's Lessee v. Hagan, 44 U.S. 212, 223 (1845) (noting the United States' responsibility for its actions in territories)). Furthermore, the Court has recognized that the United States has a unique relationship with its territories, and that this relationship can affect the application of federal laws (see Gonzales v. Williams, 192 U.S. 1, 10 (1904) (discussing the status of Puerto Rico as a territory)).

In light of these authorities, the Court should uphold the D.C. Circuit's decision, as it is grounded in a proper understanding of the relevant statutes and case law. The United States respectfully requests that the Court affirm the judgment below.
