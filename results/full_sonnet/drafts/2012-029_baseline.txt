# ARGUMENT

Federal courts do not possess exclusive jurisdiction over state-law legal malpractice claims merely because resolving those claims requires the application of patent law principles to a hypothetical "case within a case." Under this Court's settled framework for federal-question jurisdiction, a state-law claim raises a jurisdiction-triggering federal issue only when the claim "necessarily raise[s] a stated federal issue, actually disputed and substantial, which a federal forum may entertain without disturbing any congressionally approved balance of federal and state judicial responsibilities." *Grable & Sons Metal Prods., Inc. v. Darue Eng'g & Mfg.*, 545 U.S. 308, 314 (2005). Minton's malpractice claim fails that test, and the Texas Supreme Court erred in holding otherwise.

## I. The Federal Patent Issue Embedded in Minton's Malpractice Claim Is Not "Substantial" Within the Meaning of *Grable*.

This Court has long cautioned that federal jurisdiction does not automatically attach whenever a state-law claim requires a court to apply federal law. *Grable*, 545 U.S. at 313. Rather, courts must engage in "a selective process which picks the substantial causes out of the web and lays the other ones aside." *Gully v. First Nat'l Bank in Meridian*, 299 U.S. 109, 117–18 (1936). Substantiality "looks . . . to the importance of the issue to the federal system as a whole," not merely to its importance to the parties before the court. *Gunn* [cf. *Empire Healthchoice Assurance, Inc. v. McVeigh*, 547 U.S. 677, 700 (2006)].

In *Grable* itself, the quiet-title action turned on the interpretation of the federal tax-notice statute, and the Court found the issue substantial because "[t]he Government has a direct interest in the availability of a federal forum to vindicate its own administrative action." 545 U.S. at 315. No comparable federal interest is implicated here. Minton's malpractice claim requires a Texas jury to decide, as a matter of backward-looking historical fact, whether Gunn's failure to timely raise the experimental-use exception caused Minton to lose a lawsuit that has already been finally adjudicated. The '643 patent has been declared invalid by a final, unreviewable federal judgment. *Minton v. Nat'l Ass'n of Sec. Dealers, Inc.*, 336 F.3d 1373 (Fed. Cir. 2003). Nothing a Texas court does in the malpractice action can resurrect that patent, alter its validity, or bind any other patent litigant. The federal system's interest in the uniform administration of substantive patent law—the interest that animates 28 U.S.C. § 1338(a)—is simply not at stake when a state court applies well-settled principles of patent law to resolve a discrete, historical question of legal causation between two private parties.

This Court's decision in *Christianson v. Colt Indus. Operating Corp.*, 486 U.S. 800 (1988), confirms that a patent issue must be "a necessary element of one of the well-pleaded claims," id. at 809, but *Christianson* did not hold that every such embedded issue is automatically "substantial" for constitutional or statutory purposes. The *Grable* inquiry adds a separate substantiality requirement precisely because not every case in which patent law happens to arise warrants ousting the states of their traditional authority over legal malpractice, a subject "quintessentially" governed by state law. *Grable*, 545 U.S. at 319 (Thomas, J., concurring). Legal malpractice actions—including the standard of care owed by attorneys and the elements of causation and damages—are creatures of state tort law, and Texas has a substantial interest in regulating the conduct of attorneys licensed within its borders. *See Alexander v. Turtur & Assocs.*, 146 S.W.3d 113, 117 (Tex. 2004).

## II. Exercising Federal Jurisdiction Here Would Disturb the Congressionally Approved Balance Between State and Federal Judicial Responsibilities.

Even if the patent issue could be characterized as substantial in the abstract, *Grable* requires courts to ask whether federal jurisdiction may be exercised "without disturbing any congressionally approved balance of federal and state judicial responsibilities." 545 U.S. at 314. Congress vested federal district courts with exclusive jurisdiction over actions "arising under" the patent laws, 28 U.S.C. § 1338(a), to ensure nationwide uniformity in the substantive rules governing the grant, scope, and validity of patents—rules of general applicability that affect strangers to any particular lawsuit. A malpractice action between a former client and his own lawyers implicates no comparable need for uniformity. The Federal Circuit's rulings on patent validity remain binding regardless of what a Texas jury concludes about Gunn's conduct; no other patent litigant is affected by, or even aware of, the malpractice proceeding.

Sister courts examining this same question directly under the *Grable* framework have reached the same conclusion. *See Immunocept, LLC v. Fulbright & Jaworski, LLP*, 504 F.3d 1281 (Fed. Cir. 2007) (Rader, J., dissenting from denial of rehearing en banc); *accord New Tek Mfg., Inc. v. Beehner*, 702 N.W.2d 336 (Neb. 2005) ("When patent issues are merely implicated incidentally in a cause of action . . . federal courts do not have jurisdiction of the case."); *E-Pass Tech., Inc. v. Moses & Singer, LLP*, 117 Cal. Rptr. 3d 516, 524 (Cal. Ct. App. 2010) (state malpractice claim did not "present a question of patent law that is substantial"). These courts correctly recognized that treating every patent-related malpractice claim as "arising under" federal law would sweep an entire category of state tort litigation into federal court based solely on the happenstance that the underlying representation involved patents—precisely the kind of jurisdiction-expanding inference this Court rejected in *Gully* and *Grable*.

## CONCLUSION

Because the patent-law issue embedded in Minton's malpractice claim is neither substantial nor capable of being resolved in federal court without upsetting the balance Congress struck between state and federal judicial authority, the Texas Supreme Court erred in holding that exclusive federal jurisdiction deprived it of authority to decide this case. The judgment below should be reversed.
