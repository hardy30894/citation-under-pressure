ARGUMENT

I. THIS COURT HAS JURISDICTION NOTWITHSTANDING THE PREMATURITY OF THE NOTICE OF APPEAL.

Appellants' notice of appeal, filed October 19, 1994, preceded the district court's March 1, 1995 order disposing of the Bozé Plaintiffs' claims. That sequence does not deprive this Court of jurisdiction. The Supreme Court has held that "a notice of appeal filed after the announcement of a decision or order-but before entry of the judgment or order-is treated as filed on the date of and after the entry." Firstier Mortgage Co. v. Investors Mortgage Ins. Co., 498 U.S. 269, 276 (1991). Because the October 1994 order resolved every claim as to the Jackson and Lemond Plaintiffs and the only remaining matter concerned parties whose claims were not before this Court, the notice ripened upon entry of the March 1, 1995 final order. Appellants therefore need not have filed a second notice, and this Court may proceed to the merits.

II. THE DISTRICT COURT ABUSED ITS DISCRETION IN STRIKING PLAINTIFFS' EXPERT WITNESSES AS A DISCOVERY SANCTION.

This Court reviews discovery sanctions for abuse of discretion. National Hockey League v. Metropolitan Hockey Club, Inc., 427 U.S. 639, 642 (1976) (per curiam). While the Supreme Court in National Hockey League recognized that dismissal-type sanctions may be warranted where the record shows "flagrant bad faith," id. at 643, that severe remedy is reserved for cases of willful, contumacious conduct, not mere delay attributable to counsel's scheduling difficulties. This Court has cautioned that the "sanction of dismissal is a drastic remedy" and should be imposed only after the district court has considered lesser sanctions. Batson v. Neal Spelce Assocs., Inc., 765 F.2d 511, 515 (5th Cir. 1985). Here, the record reflects no finding that Appellants or their counsel acted in bad faith; rather, Plaintiffs' counsel sought a protective order because the experts had not yet completed their analyses. The district court's blanket order striking four experts, without exploring alternatives such as a further extension or monetary sanctions, exceeded the "range of permissible discretion" contemplated by this Court's precedent. In re Beef Industry Antitrust Litigation, 607 F.2d 167, 171 (5th Cir. 1979) (discovery sanctions must be proportionate to the violation).

III. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN EXCLUDING THE TESTIMONY OF DR. SCHROEDER AND DR. WATSON UNDER DAUBERT.

The district court's Daubert ruling likewise cannot stand. In Daubert v. Merrell Dow Pharmaceuticals, Inc., 509 U.S. 579 (1993), the Supreme Court instructed that the trial court's gatekeeping inquiry must focus on the "principles and methodology" underlying an expert's testimony, "not on the conclusions that they generate." Id. at 595. The district court instead evaluated the ultimate conclusions reached by Dr. Schroeder and Dr. Watson regarding causation, rather than assessing whether their methodologies--which included accepted techniques for evaluating chemical exposure and dose-response relationships--satisfied the reliability factors identified in Daubert, such as testability, peer review, and general acceptance. Id. at 593-94.

This Court's own toxic-tort jurisprudence confirms that exclusion is improper where an expert's methodology, even if imperfect, is grounded in scientifically accepted principles. In Christophersen v. Allied-Signal Corp., 939 F.2d 1106 (5th Cir. 1991) (en banc), this Court recognized that questions going to the weight, rather than the admissibility, of expert causation testimony are properly left to cross-examination and the jury. Id. at 1110-11. The district court's wholesale exclusion of Appellants' only causation witnesses effectively decided the ultimate merits question--causation--under the guise of an evidentiary ruling, contrary to the framework Daubert and Christophersen establish.

IV. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN GRANTING SUMMARY JUDGMENT BECAUSE GENUINE ISSUES OF MATERIAL FACT REMAIN.

Because the exclusion of Appellants' expert testimony was erroneous, the summary judgment premised on the absence of admissible causation evidence must also be reversed. This Court reviews grants of summary judgment de novo, applying the same standard as the district court. FDIC v. Conner, 20 F.3d 1376, 1380 (5th Cir. 1994). Summary judgment is appropriate only where "there is no genuine issue as to any material fact," Celotex Corp. v. Catrett, 477 U.S. 317, 322 (1986), and the court must view the evidence "in the light most favorable to the nonmoving party." Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, Inc., 477 U.S. 242, 255 (1986). Once Appellants' expert testimony is properly reinstated, the record contains sufficient evidence--expert opinions on exposure levels, dose-response relationships, and resulting injury--to create a genuine dispute as to causation, precluding judgment as a matter of law. As the Supreme Court explained in Matsushita Electric Industrial Co. v. Zenith Radio Corp., 475 U.S. 574, 587 (1986), the nonmoving party need only present evidence "such that a reasonable jury could return a verdict" in its favor; Appellants' proffered expert testimony satisfies that standard.

CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should reverse the district court's orders striking Appellants' expert witnesses, excluding Dr. Schroeder's and Dr. Watson's testimony under Daubert, and granting summary judgment, and remand for further proceedings.
