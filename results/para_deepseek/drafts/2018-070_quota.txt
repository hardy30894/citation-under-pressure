**ARGUMENT**

The Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act (OCSLA) adopts state law only to fill gaps in federal law, not whenever state law is merely not inconsistent with federal law. The Ninth Circuit’s contrary reading misreads the statute’s text, ignores this Court’s precedent, and would disrupt the uniform federal regime Congress created for the Outer Continental Shelf.

**I. The Text and Structure of OCSLA Require a Gap-Filling Approach**

OCSLA’s choice-of-law provision declares that state law applies “[t]o the extent that [it is] applicable and not inconsistent with this subchapter or with other Federal laws.” 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(2)(A). The phrase “[t]o the extent” is inherently limiting. It does not say state law applies whenever it is “applicable and not inconsistent”; it says state law applies only *to the extent* those conditions are met. That language signals a narrow incorporation, not a broad delegation. *See Rodrigue v. Aetna Cas. & Sur. Co.*, 395 U.S. 352, 365–66 (1969) (applying state law under OCSLA only after concluding that federal admiralty law did not cover the claim). In *Rodrigue*, the Court applied Louisiana wrongful-death law because the Death on the High Seas Act left a “gap” for deaths on fixed platforms. *Id.* at 366. The Court did not suggest that state law could apply whenever it was merely not inconsistent with federal law.

The Fifth Circuit has long recognized this gap-filling principle. In *Continental Oil Co. v. London S.S. Owners’ Mut. Ins. Ass’n*, 417 F.2d 1030, 1036 (5th Cir. 1969), the court held that state law applies under OCSLA only “to fill a significant void or gap” in federal law. That interpretation is consistent with the statute’s purpose: Congress enacted OCSLA because federal law “was never designed to be a complete body of law in and of itself.” 99 Cong. Rec. 6963 (1953). Congress did not intend to displace federal law with state law wherever the two coexist; it intended to borrow state law only where federal law is deficient.

**II. The FLSA Leaves No Gap for State Wage-and-Hour Law**

The Fair Labor Standards Act (FLSA) is a comprehensive statutory scheme governing wages, hours, and overtime. It sets minimum wage and overtime standards and expressly permits states to enact more protective laws within their own territory. 29 U.S.C. § 218(a). But that savings clause operates only within state jurisdiction. On the OCS, there is no state jurisdiction; OCSLA makes the OCS “an area of exclusive Federal jurisdiction.” 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(1). The FLSA’s savings clause does not create a gap in federal law; it merely acknowledges that states may apply their own laws where they have authority. On the OCS, the FLSA is the sole source of wage-and-hour law, and it leaves no room for state supplementation.

This Court’s decision in *Pacific Operators Offshore, LLP v. Valladolid*, 565 U.S. 207 (2012), confirms that OCSLA adopts state law only when federal law does not cover the field. There, the Court held that California’s workers’ compensation law could apply because the Longshore and Harbor Workers’ Compensation Act did not provide a remedy for the specific injury at issue. *Id.* at 220–21. The Court did not hold that state law applies whenever it is not inconsistent; it held that state law fills a gap left by federal law. *Accord Gulf Offshore Co. v. Mobil Oil Corp.*, 453 U.S. 473, 480 (1981) (applying state law under OCSLA only after finding no conflicting federal law and no comprehensive federal scheme).

The FLSA, by contrast, is a comprehensive scheme. It regulates minimum wage, overtime, recordkeeping, and child labor. The Department of Labor has issued extensive regulations elaborating these requirements. *See* 29 C.F.R. Parts 516, 778, 785. Those regulations do not require meal periods or premium pay for controlled standby time. There is no gap for state law to fill. *See Barker v. Hercules Offshore, Inc.*, 713 F.3d 208, 214 (5th Cir. 2013) (holding that maritime law, not state law, applies under OCSLA because federal law covers the field).

**III. The Ninth Circuit’s Interpretation Would Undermine Uniformity**

Congress designed OCSLA to create a uniform body of federal law for the OCS. *See Shell Oil Co. v. Iowa Dep’t of Revenue*, 488 U.S. 19, 26–27 (1988). If state law could apply whenever it is not inconsistent, then workers on adjacent platforms off different states would be subject to different wage-and-hour regimes. That would defeat the uniformity Congress sought. The gap-filling approach ensures that federal law governs unless it is truly deficient.

The Ninth Circuit relied on the FLSA’s savings clause, but that clause does not apply on the OCS. The savings clause allows states to enact more protective laws within their own borders; it does not authorize the incorporation of state law into a federal enclave. *See Chevron U.S.A. Inc. v. Hammond*, 726 F.2d 483, 490 (9th Cir. 1984) (recognizing that OCSLA creates a federal enclave). The proper analogy is the Assimilative Crimes Act, 18 U.S.C. § 13, which adopts state law only when federal law is deficient. *See United States v. Sharpnack*, 355 U.S. 286, 293–94 (1958). OCSLA’s similar language should be interpreted similarly.

**IV. The Ninth Circuit’s Decision Conflicts with This Court’s Precedent**

The Ninth Circuit held that state law applies under OCSLA as long as it is “applicable and not inconsistent,” rejecting any gap requirement. That holding cannot be squared with *Rodrigue*, which applied state law only after finding that federal law did not cover the claim. 395 U.S. at 366. Nor can it be squared with *Valladolid*, which required a showing that federal law left a gap. 565 U.S. at 220–21. The Ninth Circuit’s approach would allow state law to displace federal law even where federal law is comprehensive, contrary to the statute’s text and purpose.

**CONCLUSION**

The judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
