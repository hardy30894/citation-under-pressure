**ARGUMENT**

The Sixth Amendment does not prohibit the use of an uncounseled tribal court conviction as a predicate offense under 18 U.S.C. § 117(a), provided the conviction is valid under the applicable constitutional standards governing tribal sovereignty and due process. The Ninth Circuit’s decision in *United States v. Bryant, Jr.*, 769 F.3d 671 (9th Cir. 2014), and its subsequent refusal to rehear the matter en banc, rest on a fundamental misreading of the scope of the Sixth Amendment and a disregard for the settled principles of federal Indian law. The Court should reverse, for the following reasons.

**I. The Sixth Amendment Does Not Apply to Tribal Court Proceedings, and Thus Cannot Render an Uncounseled Tribal Conviction Inadmissible as a Predicate Offense**

The Sixth Amendment’s guarantee of counsel applies only to federal and state criminal proceedings. It does not extend to Indian tribal courts, which are sovereign entities exercising inherent authority over their members. *United States v. Wheeler*, 435 U.S. 313, 323 (1978), reaffirmed that “the sovereign power to punish tribal offenders” is “the continued exercise of retained tribal sovereignty.” This principle was long established before 1970. In *United States v. Kagama*, 118 U.S. 375, 384 (1886), the Court held that Congress may “regulate the relations between the Government and the Indians,” and that tribal courts are “independent tribunals” with authority to adjudicate crimes committed by tribal members. Because tribal courts are not subject to the Sixth Amendment, a conviction obtained in such a court—even without appointed counsel—does not violate that constitutional provision. *See also* *United States v. Sandoval*, 231 U.S. 28, 40 (1913) (recognizing tribal courts as “independent judicial bodies” with jurisdiction over crimes committed by Indians on reservations).

The Ninth Circuit’s conclusion that tribal court convictions are “constitutionally infirm” because they lack Sixth Amendment counsel is therefore legally untenable. The Constitution does not require tribal courts to provide counsel in the same manner as federal or state courts. *See* *United States v. Ant*, 882 F.2d 1389, 1394 (9th Cir. 1989) (citing *United States v. Wheeler*, 435 U.S. at 323), but the court’s reliance on *Ant* is misplaced, as that case predates the full development of federal Indian law and misapplies constitutional standards to tribal proceedings. The Supreme Court has consistently held that the Bill of Rights does not apply to tribal governments. *Plains Commerce Bank v. Long Family Land & Cattle Co.*, 554 U.S. 316, 337 (2008) (citing *United States v. Wheeler*, 435 U.S. at 323); *Santa Clara Pueblo v. Martinez*, 436 U.S. 49, 71 (1978) (noting that “the Bill of Rights does not apply to Indian tribes”).

**II. The Use of Valid Tribal Court Convictions as Predicate Offenses Is Consistent with the Principles of Comity and Respect for Tribal Sovereignty**

Federal courts have long recognized the legitimacy of tribal court judgments, even when they differ from federal or state procedures. *Wilson v. Marchington*, 127 F.3d 805, 810 (9th Cir. 1997), emphasized that “comity does not require that a tribe utilize judicial procedures identical to those used in the United States Courts.” This principle was established in *Hilton v. Guyot*, 159 U.S. 113, 205 (1895), where the Court held that foreign judgments are entitled to full faith and credit so long as they are “not contrary to public policy” and “obtained in a court of competent jurisdiction.” The same principle applies to tribal courts. *See also* *Iowa Mutual Ins. Co. v. LaPlante*, 480 U.S. 9, 15 (1987) (extending comity to tribal courts).

To require that tribal court convictions be invalidated for lack of Sixth Amendment counsel would constitute “judicial paternalism in derogation of tribal self-governance,” *Santa Clara Pueblo*, 436 U.S. at 71, and would undermine the very purpose of Congress’s recognition of tribal sovereignty. The Indian Civil Rights Act of 1968, 25 U.S.C. § 1301 et seq., explicitly recognizes tribal court authority and provides a framework for due process in tribal proceedings—without mandating Sixth Amendment counsel. *See* *United States v. Sandoval*, 231 U.S. at 40. Congress has chosen to regulate tribal justice through the ICRA, not through the Sixth Amendment. To impose the Sixth Amendment as a condition for the admissibility of tribal convictions is to override Congress’s judgment and to impose a federal constitutional standard where Congress has not required it.

**III. The Use of Prior Convictions in Recidivist Statutes Does Not Violate Due Process or the Right to a Fair Trial**

The use of prior convictions as an element of a criminal offense is a long-standing feature of American criminal law. *In re Winship*, 397 U.S. 358, 364 (1970), is not applicable here because it concerns the burden of proof at trial, not the admissibility of prior convictions. The Court in *Winship* held that the government must prove every element of an offense beyond a reasonable doubt, but it did not hold that prior convictions must be proven under the same standard when used as elements of a recidivist statute. *See also* *United States v. Brown*, 333 U.S. 18, 20 (1948) (upholding use of prior convictions in sentencing under the National Motor Vehicle Theft Act).
