**Argument**

I. The Board’s “good-faith reasonable doubt” polling standard is rational and consistent with the National Labor Relations Act.

The National Labor Relations Act does not specifically regulate employer polling of employees concerning an incumbent union’s support. See **Allentown Mack Sales & Serv., Inc. v. NLRB, 522 U.S. 359, 373 (1998)**. In the absence of a statutory command, the Board may adopt a rule that balances the Act’s competing policies so long as the rule is rational and consistent with the statute. **NLRB v. Curtin Matheson Scientific, Inc., 494 U.S. 775, 786-87 (1990)**. That is precisely what the Board did here.

An incumbent union enjoys a presumption of majority support. **Fall River Dyeing & Finishing Corp. v. NLRB, 482 U.S. 27, 37-41 (1987)**. Because unilateral employer testing of union support can destabilize bargaining relationships and invite coercion, the Board reasonably concluded that polling should be permitted only when an employer has objective indications sufficient to raise a reasonable doubt about the union’s majority status. **Allentown Mack, 522 U.S. at 373-77**; **Texas Petrochemicals Corp., 296 N.L.R.B. 1057, 1061-63 (1989), remanded sub nom. Texas Petrochemicals Corp. v. NLRB, 923 F.2d 398 (5th Cir. 1991)**. The Board was entitled to favor Board-supervised elections over unsupervised polling, because RM elections include procedural safeguards that polls lack. **Allentown Mack, 522 U.S. at 375-76**; **NLRB v. A.J. Tower Co., 329 U.S. 324, 330-31 (1946)**.

The courts of appeals that adopted a lower polling standard did so on the premise that polling should be more useful to employers. See **A.W. Thompson, Inc. v. NLRB, 651 F.2d 1141, 1144-45 (5th Cir. 1981)**; **Thomas Indus., Inc. v. NLRB, 687 F.2d 863, 866-67 (6th Cir. 1982)**; **Mingtree Rest., Inc. v. NLRB, 736 F.2d 1295, 1298-99 (9th Cir. 1984)**. But the Supreme Court held that the Board was not required to adopt that policy choice. **Allentown Mack, 522 U.S. at 377-78**. Because the Act leaves polling to the Board’s expert judgment, the Board’s rule is valid if it reasonably protects stability in bargaining. **Curtin Matheson, 494 U.S. at 786-87**; **Beth Israel Hosp. v. NLRB, 437 U.S. 483, 500-01 (1978)**.

Petitioner’s contrary position would invert the Act’s structure. If polling were easier to justify than withdrawal of recognition, employers could pressure unions with informal, unsupervised tests even when they lacked the showing necessary to justify a more formal challenge. The Board reasonably concluded that such a system would be “potentially disruptive and unsettling.” **Texas Petrochemicals, 296 N.L.R.B. at 1061-62**; **Allentown Mack, 522 U.S. at 375-77**. The Board’s rule is therefore rational, consistent with the Act, and entitled to deference. **Chevron U.S.A. Inc. v. Natural Resources Defense Council, Inc., 467 U.S. 837, 843-44 (1984)**; **Curtin Matheson, 494 U.S. at 786-87**.

II. The Board’s determination that Allentown Mack lacked a reasonable doubt was supported by substantial evidence.

A reviewing court must uphold the Board’s factual findings if supported by substantial evidence on the record considered as a whole. **Universal Camera Corp. v. NLRB, 340 U.S. 474, 488 (1951)**; **29 U.S.C. § 160(e)**. That standard is deferential, even where the record permits competing inferences. **Universal Camera, 340 U.S. at 488**.

Here, the Board reasonably concluded that petitioner did not show objective evidence sufficient to raise a reasonable doubt about Local 724’s majority status. Only a minority of the 32-unit employees made statements fairly read as opposing representation, and several of the statements on which petitioner relied were properly discounted. **Allentown Mack, 522 U.S. at 379-84**.

First, statements by employees outside the bargaining unit on the critical date could not support a reasonable doubt as to the unit then existing. **Allentown Mack, 522 U.S. at 379-80**.

Second, the Board permissibly gave little weight to equivocal remarks or expressions of dissatisfaction with representation quality. An employee’s criticism of a union’s performance does not necessarily show opposition to representation itself. **Destileria Serralles, Inc., 289 N.L.R.B. 51 (1988), enforced, 882 F.2d 19 (1st Cir. 1989)**; **Wagon Wheel Bowl, Inc. v. NLRB, 47 F.3d 332, 335-36 (9th Cir. 1995)**.

Third, the Board could discount secondhand reports about what other employees supposedly thought, especially where the declarant did not explain the basis for the assertion. The Board has long treated such evidence skeptically. **Westbrook Bowl, 293 N.L.R.B. 1000 (1989)**; **Allentown Mack, 522 U.S. at 382-84**.

The Court of Appeals erred by reweighing the evidence and substituting its own assessment for the Board’s. The question is not whether petitioner presented some evidence of disaffection, but whether the Board lacked substantial evidence to reject petitioner’s claimed doubt. **Universal Camera, 340 U.S. at 488**. Because the Board permissibly found that the evidence fell short of demonstrating a reasonable doubt, its order should be enforced. **NLRB v. Curtin Matheson Scientific, Inc., 494 U.S. 775, 787-88 (1990)**; **Allentown Mack, 522 U.S. at 379-84**.
