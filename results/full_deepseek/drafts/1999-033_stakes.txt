**ARGUMENT**

The Equal Protection Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment does not provide a cause of action for a “class of one” where the plaintiff has not alleged membership in a cognizable class or group. The Seventh Circuit’s holding—that a plaintiff may state an equal protection claim by alleging that state action was motivated solely by a “spiteful effort to ‘get’ him”—is unprecedented and inconsistent with this Court’s precedents.

**I. The Equal Protection Clause Requires Discrimination Against a Cognizable Class, Not Merely Arbitrary or Spiteful Treatment**

This Court has consistently held that the Equal Protection Clause is directed at “discrimination that is intentional and that is based on an impermissible classification.” *Washington v. Davis*, 426 U.S. 229, 239 (1976). The Clause does not guarantee that all persons will be treated identically; it requires only that “there be a reasonable basis for classification.” *Snowden v. Hughes*, 321 U.S. 1, 8 (1944). A plaintiff must show that the state actor “selected or reaffirmed a particular course of action at least in part ‘because of,’ not merely ‘in spite of,’ its adverse effects upon an identifiable group.” *Personnel Administrator v. Feeney*, 442 U.S. 256, 279 (1979).

The Seventh Circuit’s “spiteful effort” standard eliminates the requirement of class-based discrimination. Under that standard, any individual who receives unfavorable treatment from a government actor—and who can plausibly allege that the actor acted out of personal animus—may bring an equal protection claim. This Court has never recognized such a theory. In *Snowden*, the plaintiff alleged that state officials arbitrarily refused to certify his election, but this Court held that “the equal protection clause does not require that all persons be dealt with identically.” 321 U.S. at 8. The Clause does not “assure uniformity of judicial decisions or immunity from merely erroneous action.” *Id.* at 9.

**II. The “Class of One” Theory Would Transform Routine Government Decisions into Constitutional Claims**

The Village’s decision to require a 33-foot easement from Olech, while requiring only a 15-foot easement from others, was a routine administrative determination. Such decisions are subject to rational-basis review, which requires only that the action be “rationally related to a legitimate state interest.” *New Orleans v. Dukes*, 427 U.S. 297, 303 (1976). The Village’s action easily satisfies that standard: the larger easement may have been necessary due to the specific topography of Olech’s property, drainage concerns, or other legitimate engineering considerations. Olech did not allege that the Village’s action was based on any impermissible classification such as race, gender, or religion.

This Court has repeatedly warned against expanding the Equal Protection Clause into a “generalized grievance” against government action. *See, e.g., Williamson v. Lee Optical*, 348 U.S. 483, 489 (1955) (holding that “the law need not be in every respect logically consistent with its aims to be constitutional”). If every differential treatment by a government actor could give rise to an equal protection claim, federal courts would be flooded with challenges to routine zoning, permitting, and licensing decisions. The Seventh Circuit’s standard would “make the federal courts a court of appeals for every disappointed applicant for a building permit.” *See id.* at 487.

**III. The Seventh Circuit’s “Spiteful Effort” Standard Is Unworkable and Inconsistent with This Court’s Precedents**

The Seventh Circuit held that a plaintiff may state an equal protection claim by alleging that the state actor was motivated by a “spiteful effort to ‘get’ him for reasons wholly unrelated to any legitimate state objective.” This standard is both vague and overbroad. It invites litigation based on subjective allegations of ill will, without requiring any objective evidence of discriminatory purpose. This Court has required a “clear showing” of discriminatory intent in equal protection cases. *Arlington Heights v. Metropolitan Housing Corp.*, 429 U.S. 252, 265 (1977). The “spiteful effort” standard would allow a plaintiff to survive a motion to dismiss merely by alleging that a government official acted out of personal animus, even where the official’s action was rationally related to a legitimate state interest.

Moreover, this Court has recognized that government actors often have broad discretion in making individualized decisions. *See Bordenkircher v. Hayes*, 434 U.S. 357, 364 (1978) (prosecutorial discretion). The Equal Protection Clause does not “subject an otherwise valid law to constitutional attack simply because the law might be applied in an arbitrary or discriminatory manner.” *United States v. Batchelder*, 442 U.S. 114, 125 n.9 (1979). The Seventh Circuit’s standard would effectively impose a constitutional requirement of perfect consistency in government decision-making, a requirement that this Court has never imposed.

**CONCLUSION**

The Equal Protection Clause does not give rise to a cause of action for a “class of one” where the plaintiff has not alleged membership in a protected class. The judgment of the Court of Appeals should be reversed.
