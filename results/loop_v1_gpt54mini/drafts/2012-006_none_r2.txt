The judgment below should be reversed. In a conspiracy prosecution, the Government must prove beyond a reasonable doubt that the defendant remained a member of the conspiracy during the limitations period. Where the defendant has introduced evidence of withdrawal before that period, the Government does not get a presumption of continued membership; it must carry the burden of persuasion on timeliness as part of proving guilt. That rule follows from the Due Process Clause and this Court’s long-settled conspiracy precedents. See In re Winship, 397 U.S. 358, 364 (1970); Hyde v. United States, 225 U.S. 347, 369-70 (1912); Kissel v. United States, 218 U.S. 601, 607-08 (1910).

Conspiracy is a continuing offense, but continuing offense doctrine cuts in the Government’s favor only so long as the defendant remains a conspirator. Hyde held that a conspirator’s liability persists while he remains part of the unlawful agreement, and that passive nonparticipation does not itself terminate membership. Hyde, 225 U.S. at 369-70. Kissel likewise explained that a conspiracy continues as long as the unlawful combination remains operative. Kissel, 218 U.S. at 607-08. Brown v. Elliott, 225 U.S. 392, 401-02 (1912), confirms that conspiracy liability depends on continued participation in the agreement. These cases establish a basic proposition: if membership ended before the limitations period, the defendant is not guilty of the charged conspiracy during the relevant time.

That principle matters because limitations is a substantive constraint on criminal liability, not a technicality the defendant must negate. Fiswick v. United States, 329 U.S. 211, 216-17 (1946), held that conspiracy continues until accomplishment, abandonment, or withdrawal, and that the statute of limitations runs from the point at which the defendant’s participation ends. Grunewald v. United States, 353 U.S. 391, 396-97 (1957), similarly emphasized that limitations is designed to prevent prosecution after the legally prescribed period has passed. Pendergast v. United States, 317 U.S. 412, 418-19 (1943), likewise treated timeliness as an essential boundary on criminal prosecution. These decisions show that once withdrawal is in issue, the Government must prove continued membership to keep the prosecution within the allowable period.

The Constitution requires that result. Winship made clear that due process requires proof beyond a reasonable doubt of “every fact necessary to constitute the crime.” In re Winship, 397 U.S. at 364. Timely membership in the conspiracy is such a fact, because without it the Government cannot lawfully punish the defendant for the charged conspiracy. Morissette v. United States, 342 U.S. 246, 274-75 (1952), rejected any approach that relieves the prosecution of proving the facts that establish criminal liability. Tot v. United States, 319 U.S. 463, 469-70 (1943), similarly rejected presumptions that shift the burden of persuasion on an essential fact. And United States v. Cook, 84 U.S. (17 Wall.) 168, 179-80 (1872), recognized that when timeliness is placed in issue, the prosecution must prove it rather than require the accused to disprove it.

The Government may argue that withdrawal is an affirmative defense and therefore the defendant should bear the burden of persuasion. But that framing cannot control where the effect of withdrawal is to negate the Government’s proof that the conspiracy continued into the limitations period. Yates v. United States, 354 U.S. 298, 334-35 (1957), recognized that a conspirator who withdraws ceases to be a party to the conspiracy. United States v. Bayer, 331 U.S. 532, 542-43 (1947), likewise reflects that conspiracy liability does not continue indefinitely after participation ends. Once the defendant has introduced evidence of withdrawal, the Government must prove the contrary if it wishes to establish criminal liability within the limitations period.

That allocation is also consistent with the logic of Fiswick and Grunewald. If the conspiracy is continuing, then the Government may prosecute only so long as it proves the defendant remained a participant while the conspiracy continued. Fiswick, 329 U.S. at 216-17; Grunewald, 353 U.S. at 396-97. Permitting the Government to rest on the historical fact of earlier membership would erase the statute of limitations whenever a defendant once joined a conspiracy, no matter how long before indictment he withdrew. That is not the law. See Hyde, 225 U.S. at 369-70; Cook, 84 U.S. (17 Wall.) at 179-80.

Because the Government bears the burden of proving continued conspiratorial membership during the limitations period beyond a reasonable doubt, the court below erred in shifting that burden to the defendant. The judgment should be reversed.
