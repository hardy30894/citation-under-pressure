## ARGUMENT

The Prison Litigation Reform Act does not bar the three-judge court's order requiring California to reduce its prison population to remedy ongoing Eighth Amendment violations. The order satisfies each of the PLRA's requirements: it is narrowly drawn, extends no further than necessary to correct the constitutional violations, and is the least intrusive means of doing so. The three-judge court expressly found that overcrowding is the "primary cause" of the unconstitutional conditions and that no other relief would remedy the violation. Those findings are amply supported by the record and are entitled to deference.

Federal courts have long possessed the equitable power to remedy constitutional violations by ordering the release of incarcerated individuals when necessary. In *Screws v. United States*, 325 U.S. 91 (1945), this Court held that a federal court may enjoin state officers to enforce the Constitution, even where compliance requires the State to take actions that would otherwise be within its sovereign discretion. That power extends to remedying systemic Eighth Amendment violations. *Weems v. United States*, 217 U.S. 349 (1910), established that the Eighth Amendment prohibits punishments that are not "graduated and proportioned to the offense," and that courts must enforce that prohibition without regard to legislative inaction. *Id.* at 367. The conditions in California's prisons—pervasive inadequate medical care, untreated mental illness, and preventable deaths—are precisely the kind of "barbarous" treatment the Eighth Amendment forbids. *Id.* at 371.

This Court has repeatedly affirmed the breadth of federal equitable authority to remedy constitutional wrongs. In *Griffin v. County School Board of Prince Edward County*, 377 U.S. 218 (1964), the Court declared that principles of equity must be tailored to the "practical flexibility" needed to eliminate constitutional violations. *Id.* at 234. A court may, in its remedial discretion, "require the necessary steps" to bring a State into constitutional compliance, even when those steps involve the release of prisoners. *Id.* Similarly, in *United States v. Swift & Co.*, 286 U.S. 106 (1932), this Court approved a mandatory injunction requiring the cessation of unlawful conduct, holding that equitable relief is not limited by the "mere difficulty of enforcement" or the "magnitude of the task."

The PLRA does not displace these fundamental equitable principles. In *Sprague v. Ticonic National Bank*, 307 U.S. 161 (1939), this Court instructed that statutes limiting equitable remedies should not be construed to abolish "the historic process of equity" absent a clear legislative command. *Id.* at 164. The PLRA's requirements—that relief be "narrowly drawn," "extend no further than necessary," and be the "least intrusive means"—echo traditional equitable standards. They do not erect a per se bar to population caps when the court finds, as it did here, that nothing short of a significant reduction in the inmate population will cure the constitutional violation.

Moreover, the three-judge court complied with the PLRA's procedural requirements. The order was issued by a three-judge panel convened under 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(3)(B). The court made the requisite findings that overcrowding is the "primary cause" of the constitutional violations and that "no other relief" would suffice. The court also found that the order is the "least intrusive means" of correcting the violation. These findings are supported by voluminous evidence, including the State's own admissions that for years it failed to provide constitutionally adequate medical care and that overcrowding made compliance impossible.

The State's suggestion that the PLRA forbids any order requiring the release of prisoners is incorrect. The PLRA permits a prisoner release order if the three-judge court finds by clear and convincing evidence that crowding is the "primary cause" of the constitutional violation and that "no other relief" will remedy it. 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(3)(E)(i)-(ii). The court made both findings here. The State's argument would effectively read these provisions out of the statute, rendering the PLRA a bar to any remedy regardless of the severity of the constitutional violation.

This Court has long recognized that federal courts may enforce the Constitution even against recalcitrant States. In *Missouri v. Holland*, 252 U.S. 416 (1920), the Court held that the Constitution, "in its own efficacy, its own appropriate sanctions, reaches the States and their officers." *Id.* at 433. If a State defaults in its duty, "the judicial power of the United States can be exerted to compel the performance of that duty." *Id.* The three-judge court acted within that tradition.

Finally, the district court's contempt process, which led to the order now on appeal, is supported by this Court's holding in *Nye v. United States*, 313 U.S. 33 (1941), that federal courts may hold parties in contempt for defiance of orders necessary to effectuate constitutional remedies. The State consented to the original remedial orders and to the appointment of the Receiver; it cannot now claim immunity from the consequences of its own failure to comply.

The PLRA does not insulate California from its constitutional obligations. The three-judge court's order is a lawful exercise of equitable authority, fully consistent with the statute and with this Court's precedents. The judgment should be affirmed.
