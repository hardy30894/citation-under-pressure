ARGUMENT

Section 1407(a) directs that actions transferred for pretrial proceedings “shall” be remanded at their conclusion. This mandatory language leaves no room for a transferee court to retain the case for trial. Anderson v. Yungkau, 329 U.S. 482, 485 (1947). The command reflects Congress’s deliberate choice to limit multidistrict transfers to pretrial matters and to preserve the plaintiff’s original forum for trial. Ex parte Collett, 337 U.S. 55, 58 (1949).

Section 1404(a) supplies no authority to circumvent that limit. It permits transfer only to a district where the action “might have been brought.” Hoffman v. Blaski, 363 U.S. 335, 342-43 (1960); Continental Grain Co. v. The FBL-585, 364 U.S. 19, 21 (1960). A transferee court therefore cannot assign the case to itself. Van Dusen v. Barrack, 376 U.S. 612, 616-17 (1964). Where a specific statute governs the precise issue, it controls over the more general transfer provision. Escoe v. Zerbst, 295 U.S. 490, 493 (1935).

The district court’s refusal to remand exceeded its statutory authority and warrants correction by mandamus. Roche v. Evaporated Milk Ass’n, 319 U.S. 21, 26 (1943). Mandamus is available when the order is clearly erroneous as a matter of law and no other adequate remedy exists. La Buy v. Howes Leather Co., 352 U.S. 249, 256 (1957); Bankers Life & Cas. Co. v. Holland, 346 U.S. 379, 383 (1953). The writ also serves to confine a court to its lawful jurisdiction. Ex parte Fahey, 332 U.S. 258, 260 (1947). Because the Arizona court lacked power to retain the action for trial, the judgment should be reversed and the case remanded to the Northern District of Illinois.
