The Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act extends the Longshore and Harbor Workers’ Compensation Act only to injuries occurring on the outer Continental Shelf. Section 1333(b) provides compensation for “disability or death of an employee resulting from any injury occurring as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf.” 43 U.S.C. § 1333(b). This language incorporates the situs requirement long recognized under the Longshore Act itself.

The Longshore Act, as originally enacted, limited coverage to disability or death “resulting from an injury occurring upon the navigable waters of the United States.” 33 U.S.C. § 903(a) (pre-1972). The Supreme Court consistently held that this geographic limitation was jurisdictional and could not be satisfied by operations or employment status alone. In Nacirema Operating Co. v. Johnson, 396 U.S. 212 (1969), the Court stated that “Congress intended that a worker’s right to compensation . . . should be determined by the situs of his injury.” Id. at 215. Injuries sustained on piers or other land-based facilities adjoining navigable waters fell outside the Act’s reach, even when the work was maritime in nature. Id. at 223-24.

The same principle governs the OCSLA extension. Congress chose to apply the Longshore Act to the outer Continental Shelf by reference to the same statutory scheme, without altering its core geographic condition. Early decisions construing analogous federal compensation statutes confirm that “resulting from” language does not eliminate a situs requirement. See, e.g., Crowell v. Benson, 285 U.S. 22, 55 (1932) (coverage turns on whether the injury occurred within the defined federal area). The OCSLA’s text likewise ties compensable injuries to those “occurring as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf,” thereby requiring the injury itself to take place on the Shelf.

This construction respects the deliberate line Congress drew between federal and state compensation regimes. As the Court observed in Calbeck v. Travelers Insurance Co., 370 U.S. 114 (1962), the Longshore Act was designed to cover only those injuries occurring within the specified federal domain, leaving injuries on land to state law. Id. at 126-27. Extending OCSLA benefits to land-based injuries would erase that boundary and create the very overlap the statute avoided.

The Ninth Circuit’s “substantial nexus” test finds no support in the pre-1970 authorities interpreting either the Longshore Act or parallel federal compensation provisions. Those decisions uniformly required the injury to occur at the statutorily designated location. Because Valladolid’s death occurred at an onshore facility in California, it lies outside OCSLA coverage.
