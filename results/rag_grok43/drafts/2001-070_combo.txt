The Eighth Amendment does not categorically prohibit capital punishment for mentally retarded offenders. Historical acceptance of the death penalty confirms that such sentences fall within the bounds of permissible state authority. Wilkerson v. Utah, 99 U.S. 130 (1879); McElvaine v. Brush, 142 U.S. 155 (1891).

Sentencing procedures that permit juries to consider all aspects of an offender’s mental condition satisfy due process. Specht v. Patterson, 386 U.S. 605 (1967) (“most of the information now relied upon by judges to guide them in the intelligent imposition of sentences would be unavailable if information were restricted to that given in open court by witnesses subject to cross-examination”). Andres v. United States, 333 U.S. 740 (1948), likewise upheld statutes requiring unanimous jury action on both guilt and punishment without mandating exclusion based on mental capacity.

Jurisdiction and finality rules in capital cases further demonstrate that mental abnormality alone does not render a death sentence unconstitutional. Rakes v. United States, 212 U.S. 55 (1909); Good Shot v. United States, 179 U.S. 87 (1900); Kelley v. Oregon, 273 U.S. 589 (1927). Palmer v. Ashe, 342 U.S. 134 (1951), addressed claims of mental abnormality in habeas proceedings yet required specific allegations of incompetence at sentencing before relief could be granted. United States ex rel. Claussen v. Day, 279 U.S. 398 (1929), similarly enforced consequences of conviction without regard to later claims of diminished capacity.

Ludeling v. Chaffe, 143 U.S. 301 (1892), reinforces that state judgments remain enforceable absent a clear federal constitutional violation shown on the record. Because the record here contains no such showing, the judgment should be affirmed.
