**ARGUMENT**

**I. A Violation of Rule 32(a)(2) Does Not Automatically Entitle a Defendant to Collateral Relief Under 28 U.S.C. § 2255.**

Collateral relief under § 2255 is not a substitute for direct appeal and is reserved for errors of constitutional or jurisdictional magnitude, or for "a fundamental defect which inherently results in a complete miscarriage of justice." *Hill v. United States*, 368 U.S. 424, 428 (1962). This Court has consistently refused to treat every departure from the Federal Rules of Criminal Procedure as grounds for vacating a sentence on collateral review. In *United States v. Timmreck*, 441 U.S. 780 (1979), the Court held that a "formal violation" of Rule 11—there, the failure to advise a defendant of a mandatory special parole term—did not justify relief under § 2255 because the defendant "does not argue that he was actually unaware of the special parole term . . . or that, if he had been properly advised . . . he would not have pleaded guilty." *Id.* at 784. The Court explained that "collateral relief is not available when all that is shown is a failure to comply with the formal requirements of the Rule." *Id.* at 783–84.

The same logic controls here. Rule 32(a)(2)'s advice requirement, like Rule 11's, is a procedural safeguard designed to ensure that a defendant is aware of a right he already possesses under 18 U.S.C. § 3742. The Rule does not create the right to appeal; it merely provides a mechanism for communicating that right. Where the defendant already possesses the knowledge the Rule is designed to impart, the district court's failure to recite the advice is not the kind of error that "inherently result[s] in a complete miscarriage of justice." *Hill*, 368 U.S. at 428.

**II. Because Peguero Had Actual Knowledge of His Right to Appeal, the District Court's Omission Did Not Affect His Substantial Rights.**

This Court's collateral-review jurisprudence requires a petitioner to demonstrate "actual prejudice" resulting from the claimed error. *United States v. Frady*, 456 U.S. 152, 168 (1982) (petitioner seeking collateral relief must show that an error "worked to his actual and substantial disadvantage, infecting his entire trial with error of constitutional dimensions"). The purpose of Rule 32(a)(2)'s advice requirement is to ensure that a defendant "know[s] of his right to appeal and be given an opportunity to exercise that right." Where, as here, the district court expressly found after an evidentiary hearing that Peguero was aware of his right to appeal at the time of sentencing, the failure to give the formal advice could not have caused him any prejudice, because the very information the Rule requires to be conveyed was already in his possession.

Petitioner cannot show that the district court's omission caused him to forgo an appeal he otherwise would have taken. To the contrary, the factual record establishes that Peguero's decision not to appeal was made with full knowledge of the option available to him. In these circumstances, the Rule 32(a)(2) violation is precisely the sort of harmless, technical noncompliance that this Court held insufficient to support relief in *Timmreck*. See 441 U.S. at 784 (rejecting relief because the defendant did not allege that he was actually unaware of the omitted information or that it would have changed his conduct).

The Third Circuit correctly applied harmless-error principles in reaching this same conclusion, holding that the Rule's purpose had been served because Peguero already possessed the knowledge the Rule was meant to supply. That approach accords with the general principle, reflected in Rule 52(a) of the Federal Rules of Criminal Procedure, that errors which do not affect substantial rights "shall be disregarded." Fed. R. Crim. P. 52(a). It would elevate form over substance to hold that a district court's failure to recite an advisement automatically entitles a defendant to relief when the defendant independently knew, and thus was not deprived of, the very right the advisement was designed to protect.

**III. Petitioner's Proposed Automatic-Vacatur Rule Would Undermine the Finality of Criminal Judgments Without Advancing Rule 32(a)(2)'s Purpose.**

This Court has long recognized the strong interest in the finality of criminal judgments, particularly on collateral review. See *Bousley v. United States*, 523 U.S. 614, 621 (1998) (habeas review "is an extraordinary remedy and will not be allowed to do service for an appeal"). Petitioner's rule—that any Rule 32(a)(2) omission requires vacatur regardless of the defendant's actual knowledge—would convert a rule of procedural guidance into a strict-liability trap, permitting defendants who had full knowledge of their appellate rights, deliberately chose not to appeal, and later became dissatisfied with that choice, to obtain a new appeal years after judgment became final. Nothing in Rule 32(a)(2)'s text or purpose commands such a result. The Rule exists to inform defendants of a right they might not otherwise know; it is not a freestanding entitlement whose breach mandates relief irrespective of prejudice.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Court of Appeals should be affirmed.
