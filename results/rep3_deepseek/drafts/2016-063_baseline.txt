ARGUMENT

I. Kloeckner Requires District Court Review of All Mixed Cases, Whatever the Board’s Disposition.

The Civil Service Reform Act defines a “mixed case” by what the employee alleges: she “has been affected by an action which [she] may appeal to the Merit Systems Protection Board, and . . . alleges that a basis for the action was discrimination prohibited by” enumerated federal statutes. 5 U.S.C. § 7702(a)(1). For such cases, judicial review “shall” be filed in district court, not the Federal Circuit. 5 U.S.C. § 7703(b)(2). The statute does not condition the forum on the Board’s ultimate ruling about its own jurisdiction.

In *Kloeckner v. Solis*, 568 U.S. 41, 133 S. Ct. 596, 184 L. Ed. 2d 433 (2012), the Supreme Court rejected a similar attempt to narrow the district court’s role. There, the Board had dismissed a mixed case as untimely, without reaching the merits of the discrimination claim. The Court held that the case “belongs in district court, not the Federal Circuit,” because the employee “was affected by an action (i.e., removal) appealable to the MSPB and . . . alleged discrimination.” *Id.* at 44, 604. The Board’s procedural dismissal “does not make [the] case any less a mixed case.” *Id.* at 55. The Court emphasized that “the statutory text is plain” and that a mixed case “shall be filed in district court” regardless of how the Board disposes of it. *Id.* at 55, 604.

*Kloeckner’s* reasoning is not limited to dismissals on grounds of untimeliness. The Court repeatedly framed the question as one involving the proper forum “when the MSPB has dismissed her mixed case on procedural grounds,” *id.* at 603, but the dispositive statutory fact was that the employee had alleged discrimination in connection with an appealable action. The Court observed that “[n]o one here contests that Kloeckner brought a mixed case,” *id.* at 604, and then held that the district court’s jurisdiction “extends to all mixed cases presented to the Board.” *Id.* at 52. There is no textual basis for distinguishing a dismissal for lack of jurisdiction from a dismissal for untimeliness. In both situations, the Board has declined to reach the merits of the employee’s discrimination claim.

The D.C. Circuit’s contrary holding in *Powell v. Department of Defense*, 158 F.3d 597 (D.C. Cir. 1998), was effectively overruled by *Kloeckner*. *Powell* reasoned that a jurisdictional dismissal means the employee’s action was never “appealable to the MSPB,” and thus the case cannot satisfy the statutory definition of a mixed case. *Id.* at 599-600. But *Kloeckner* rejected precisely that logic with respect to procedural dismissals, holding that a dismissal on a non-merits ground does not retroactively strip the case of its mixed-case character. 568 U.S. at 55. The distinction the D.C. Circuit drew between procedural and jurisdictional dismissals is unworkable. In both settings, the Board has refused to adjudicate the discrimination claim, and in both settings the employee has alleged an action appealable to the Board at the time she filed her appeal. *Kloeckner* requires district court review in all such cases.

II. The Board’s Voluntariness Determination Is Not a True Jurisdictional Ruling; It Is Intertwined with the Merits of Perry’s Discrimination Claim.

Perry’s appeal to the Board alleged that his settlement agreement, suspension, and retirement were the product of discrimination and coercion. The Board’s jurisdiction turned on whether the settlement was voluntary, and the Board dismissed for lack of jurisdiction only after resolving that issue against Perry. That voluntariness determination is inseparable from the merits of his discrimination claim: the same evidence of coercion would establish both involuntariness and discrimination.

*Kloeckner* recognized that district courts, not the Federal Circuit, are the appropriate forum for resolving such intertwined questions. The Court noted that “the mixed case scheme” gives district courts jurisdiction over “the entire case,” including “the discrimination claim,” so that an employee is not forced to split her case between two tribunals. 568 U.S. at 49-51. Under *Powell*, by contrast, an employee who alleges discrimination but whose case is dismissed for lack of jurisdiction must pursue review in the Federal Circuit, which has no authority to resolve the discrimination claim on the merits. That result undermines the statutory design and is inconsistent with *Kloeckner’s* holding that the district court’s jurisdiction is determined at the outset by the employee’s allegations, not by the Board’s later decision.

The Federal Circuit’s decision in *Conforto v. Merit Systems Protection Board*, 713 F.3d 1118 (Fed. Cir. 2013), does not compel a different result. *Conforto* held that *Kloeckner* applies only to procedural dismissals, not jurisdictional dismissals. *Id.* at 1118 n.1. But *Conforto* is based on the same flawed distinction that the D.C. Circuit adopted here, and it was decided without the benefit of *Kloeckner’s* command that the statutory text controls. As *Kloeckner* explained, “the fact that the Board dismissed the case on procedural grounds” is irrelevant to the forum inquiry. 568 U.S. at 55. So too, the Board’s dismissal for lack of jurisdiction does not transform the case into a non-mixed case.

III. The D.C. Circuit’s Distinction Would Create an Arbitrary and Unworkable Forum-Splitting Scheme.

Perry filed his appeal believing it was a mixed case. The Board, after extensive proceedings, determined that it lacked jurisdiction. Under the D.C. Circuit’s rule, the forum for judicial review depends entirely on the Board’s eventual characterization of its own dismissal. That approach requires an employee to guess, at the risk of filing in the wrong court, whether the Board will dispose of her case on jurisdictional or procedural grounds. *Kloeckner* rejected such forum uncertainty, holding that “the district court is the only court with jurisdiction to review a mixed case.” 568 U.S. at 51. The statute aims to give employees with discrimination claims a district court forum, and that aim is frustrated when the Board can override the employee’s choice of forum by framing its dismissal in jurisdictional terms.

The judgment of the D.C. Circuit should be reversed, and the case should be transferred to the district court for review of Perry’s mixed-case appeal.
