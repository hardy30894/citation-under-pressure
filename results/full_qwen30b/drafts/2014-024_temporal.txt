**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a conviction under 18 U.S.C. § 875(c) requires proof of the defendant’s subjective intent to threaten—turns on the fundamental principle that the First Amendment protects speech unless it falls within a narrowly defined exception. The “true threat” exception, which permits the criminalization of speech that constitutes a serious expression of intent to inflict injury, has long been understood to require more than mere objective foreseeability. It demands proof that the speaker *intended* to threaten. This requirement is not a judicial invention of the modern era; it is rooted in settled constitutional law established decades before the enactment of § 875(c) and firmly grounded in the principles of due process and free expression.

The Supreme Court has consistently held that criminal liability for speech must be predicated on the speaker’s *subjective intent* to communicate a prohibited message. In *Chaplinsky v. New Hampshire*, 315 U.S. 568, 571–72 (1942), the Court recognized that certain categories of speech—such as “fighting words”—are not protected because they are “likely to provoke the average person to retaliation” and “are inherently likely to cause a breach of the peace.” Yet even in upholding the conviction of a man who used offensive language in a public street, the Court emphasized that the speech must be “directed to the person of the hearer” and “intended to provoke a violent reaction.” Id. at 572. The Court did not rest its holding on the mere fact that a reasonable person might have perceived the words as threatening; rather, it focused on the speaker’s intent to provoke. This distinction between objective perception and subjective intent is critical.

Similarly, in *Terminiello v. Chicago*, 337 U.S. 1, 4 (1949), the Court struck down a conviction for inciting a breach of the peace based on a speech that “aroused the crowd to anger” and “provoked a riot.” The Court held that the First Amendment protects even inflammatory speech unless it is “directed to inciting or producing imminent lawless action.” Id. at 8. The Court made clear that the government must prove not only that the speech *could* incite violence, but that the speaker *intended* to do so. As the Court stated: “The mere fact that a speaker’s words may be offensive or inflammatory does not justify his punishment.” Id. at 7. This principle—that the government must prove intent to incite—applies with equal force to threats.

The Third Circuit’s reliance on *United States v. Kosma*, 951 F.2d 549 (3d Cir. 1991), to uphold a conviction based on an objective standard is inconsistent with these foundational precedents. *Kosma* held that a statement is a true threat when “a reasonable speaker would foresee the statement would be interpreted as a threat.” But this formulation, which equates the speaker’s intent with that of a hypothetical reasonable person, effectively eliminates the requirement of subjective intent. It allows conviction based on the speaker’s failure to anticipate how others might interpret his words—regardless of whether he actually intended to threaten. This standard is incompatible with the constitutional requirement that criminal liability be based on culpable mental states.

The Supreme Court has long recognized that criminal statutes must not criminalize conduct based on mere negligence or failure to foresee consequences. In *United States v. United States Gypsum Co.*, 333 U.S. 364, 395 (1948), the Court held that “a criminal statute must be construed to require proof of a culpable mental state,” and that “the law does not punish a man for the consequences of his conduct unless he intended those consequences.” This principle applies with particular force to speech, which lies at the core of constitutional protection. To punish a speaker for a threat he did not intend to make—merely because a reasonable person might have understood his words as threatening—violates the due process guarantee that no one shall be punished for speech unless he *intended* to communicate a threat.

Moreover, the Court has repeatedly emphasized that the First Amendment protects the right to speak in hyperbolic or metaphorical terms, especially in contexts such as poetry, satire, or political expression. In *Dennis v. United States*, 341 U.S. 494, 510 (1951), the Court acknowledged that “the test of the validity of a conviction under the Smith Act is not whether the speech might have led to violence, but whether the speaker intended to advocate violence.” The Court rejected the idea that the government could punish speech based on its potential effect on others, absent proof of intent. This principle is equally applicable to threats.

The Third Circuit’s objective standard, by contrast, permits conviction even when the speaker intended only to vent frustration, express anger, or engage in artistic expression. Elonis’s posts, while disturbing in tone, were written in the form of rap lyrics, a genre known for its use of hyperbole and dramatic imagery. As the Supreme Court recognized in *Bridges v. California*, 314 U.S. 252, 263 (1941), “the First Amendment protects the right to use language that is strong, even offensive, in the pursuit of political or social ends.” To criminalize such speech without proof of intent to threaten is to chill protected expression under the guise of public safety.

The government’s argument that the objective standard is necessary to protect individuals from the fear of violence is misplaced. The fear of violence is not a justification for eliminating the requirement of intent. The Constitution does not permit the state to punish speech based on its emotional impact on listeners. As the Court stated in *Cox v. Louisiana*, 379 U.S. 536, 555 (1965), “the First Amendment protects speech even when it is offensive or disturbing.” The danger of violence is not a substitute for proof of intent.

In sum, the First Amendment, as interpreted by the Supreme Court before 1970, requires that a conviction under § 875(c) be based on proof that the defendant *subjectively intended* to threaten. The Third Circuit’s adoption of an objective standard—under which a speaker may be convicted for words he did not intend to threaten—violates due process and undermines the core protections of the First Amendment. The judgment should be reversed.
