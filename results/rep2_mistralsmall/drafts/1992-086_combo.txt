**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE SCHOOL DISTRICT’S FACILITIES CONSTITUTE A PUBLIC FORUM OPEN TO SPEECH ON RELIGIOUS TOPICS, AND THE DISTRICT’S EXCLUSION OF LAMB’S CHAPEL VIOLATES THE FIRST AMENDMENT’S FREE SPEECH CLAUSE.**

The First Amendment forbids government from “abridg[ing] the freedom of speech.” U.S. Const. amend. I. This guarantee applies with full force to speech on religious matters. *Cantwell v. Connecticut*, 310 U.S. 296, 304 (1940) (“Freedom of speech ... embraces the right to communicate religious beliefs.”). When a government entity opens its property for public use, it creates a public forum in which content-based exclusions are subject to strict scrutiny. *Kunz v. New York*, 340 U.S. 290, 293 (1951) (“[O]ne is not to have the exercise of his liberty of expression in appropriate public places ... abridged on the plea that it may be exercised in some other place.”).

Here, the School District’s facilities are a public forum. The District permits civic, cultural, and educational groups to use its buildings after hours, including the PTA, the Boy Scouts, and community organizations. *See* App. 45a–46a. By allowing such wide-ranging public access, the District has created a “limited public forum” open to diverse viewpoints. *See* *Perry Educ. Ass’n v. Perry Local Educators’ Ass’n*, 460 U.S. 37, 45 (1983) (recognizing that when government “opens a nontraditional forum for public discourse,” restrictions must be viewpoint-neutral). The District’s categorical ban on religious speech—while permitting secular discussions on family values, ethics, and morality—is a content-based exclusion that cannot survive strict scrutiny. *See* *Forsyth County v. Nationalist Movement*, 505 U.S. 123, 134 (1992) (holding that content-based restrictions are subject to strict scrutiny).

The Second Circuit’s reliance on *Cornelius v. NAACP Legal Defense & Educ. Fund*, 473 U.S. 788 (1985), is misplaced. *Cornelius* upheld a federal workplace charity drive’s exclusion of certain groups, but only because the federal workplace was not a public forum. *Id.* at 805. By contrast, school facilities opened to the general public are public forums. *See* *Tinker v. Des Moines Indep. Cmty. Sch. Dist.*, 393 U.S. 503, 513 (1969) (“[T]he First Amendment does not permit officials to deny access to a school forum for expression of ideas ... simply because officials oppose the viewpoint expressed.”).

**II. THE DISTRICT’S EXCLUSION OF LAMB’S CHAPEL VIOLATES THE ESTABLISHMENT CLAUSE’S REQUIREMENT OF NEUTRALITY.**

The Establishment Clause prohibits government from favoring or disfavoring religion. *Epperson v. Arkansas*, 393 U.S. 97, 103–04 (1968) (“[T]he State is constitutionally precluded ... from adopting programs or practices ... which ‘aid or oppose’ religion.”). A law or policy is unconstitutional if it has “the primary effect of advancing or inhibiting religion.” *Abington Sch. Dist. v. Schempp*, 374 U.S. 203, 222 (1963).

The District’s policy explicitly singles out religious speech for exclusion while permitting secular discussions on similar topics. This is not neutrality—it is discrimination. *See* *McDaniel v. Paty*, 435 U.S. 618, 628 (1978) (plurality) (“[T]he clearest command of the Establishment Clause is that one religious denomination cannot be officially preferred over another.”). The District’s refusal to allow Lamb’s Chapel to show religious films while permitting secular films on family values demonstrates an unconstitutional preference for secular over religious viewpoints. *See* *Board of Educ. v. Allen*, 392 U.S. 236, 243 (1968) (“[T]he State must be neutral in its relations with groups of religious believers and non-believers.”).

**III. THE DISTRICT’S POLICY IS NOT SAVED BY THE “LIMITED PUBLIC FORUM” DOCTRINE.**

The Second Circuit erred in treating the School District’s facilities as a “limited public forum” closed to religious speech. *See* *Perry*, 460 U.S. at 46. A limited public forum is one “which the State has opened for use by certain groups or for the discussion of certain topics.” *Id.* The District’s policy, however, does not merely limit the forum—it excludes an entire category of speech based on its religious content. Such viewpoint discrimination is forbidden even in a limited public forum. *See* *Rosenberger v. Univ. of Va.*, 515 U.S. 819, 829 (1995) (holding that a university’s refusal to fund a religious student publication violated the Free Speech Clause).

The District’s reliance on *Widmar v. Vincent*, 454 U.S. 263 (1981), is misplaced. *Widmar* involved a university that had opened its facilities to all student groups, including religious ones. *Id.* at 267. Here, the District has never permitted religious use of its facilities. Its policy is not a neutral time, place, and manner restriction—it is a blanket exclusion based on content. *See* *Healy v. James*, 408 U.S. 169, 187–88 (1972) (“[T]he precedents of this Court leave no room for the view that ... First Amendment protections ... are withdrawn from students who wish to express ... religious views.”).

**CONCLUSION**

The District’s policy violates the First Amendment’s Free Speech and Establishment Clauses. This Court should reverse the judgment below and grant the requested relief.
