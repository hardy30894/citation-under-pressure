**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a federal district court conducting “pretrial proceedings” under 28 U.S.C. § 1407(a) may invoke § 1404(a) to assign a transferred case to itself for trial—requires a straightforward application of statutory text and settled precedent. The answer is no. Congress has unambiguously provided that after the conclusion of pretrial proceedings, each action transferred under § 1407(a) “shall be remanded by the panel” to the original district for trial. 28 U.S.C. § 1407(a). The district court’s attempt to circumvent this command by invoking § 1404(a) is not merely an error in judgment—it is a violation of a clear statutory mandate.

The plain language of § 1407(a) leaves no room for judicial recharacterization. The statute authorizes the Judicial Panel on Multidistrict Litigation (JPML) to transfer civil actions “to any district for coordinated or consolidated pretrial proceedings,” but it further provides that “[e]ach action so transferred shall be remanded by the panel at or before the conclusion of such pretrial proceedings.” 28 U.S.C. § 1407(a) (emphasis added). This is not a mere suggestion; it is a mandatory directive. The word “shall” imposes a legal obligation, not a discretionary option. See *United States v. X-Citement Video, Inc.*, 114 F.3d 1424, 1428 (9th Cir. 1997) (“The word ‘shall’ in a statute is generally construed as mandatory unless a contrary intent is evident.”). The JPML’s authority to transfer is limited to pretrial proceedings, and its authority to remand is equally mandatory. Once those proceedings conclude, the case must return to the original district.

The district court’s reliance on § 1404(a) to transfer the case to itself for trial is legally untenable. Section 1404(a) permits a court to transfer a case “for the convenience of parties and witnesses, in the interest of justice,” but only when the case is “in any other district or division where it might have been brought.” 28 U.S.C. § 1404(a). The case in question was originally filed in the Northern District of Illinois. Under § 1407(a), it was transferred to Arizona for pretrial proceedings. But the statute does not authorize the transferee court to retransfer the case to itself for trial. To hold otherwise would render § 1407(a)’s remand provision superfluous and would allow district courts to subvert the JPML’s statutory role. See *United States v. H. L. Green Co.*, 338 F.2d 758, 761 (2d Cir. 1964) (“Where a statute provides a specific remedy, courts may not supply a different one by implication.”).

The Ninth Circuit’s decision to deny mandamus relief rests on a flawed understanding of the Bauman factors. The court concluded that petitioners could obtain relief on direct appeal, but that reasoning ignores the irreparable harm caused by a trial in a forum where the parties have no connection and where the case was never properly assigned. The prejudice here is not merely logistical—it is constitutional in nature. The right to trial in one’s home district is a fundamental component of due process and the Seventh Amendment. See *Hanna v. Plumer*, 380 U.S. 460, 468 (1965) (“The right to trial by jury in civil cases is a fundamental right protected by the Seventh Amendment.”). Forcing a party to litigate a case in a distant district after a year of pretrial proceedings, without any statutory basis, undermines the very purpose of § 1407(a), which is to promote efficiency in pretrial matters—not to allow district courts to seize jurisdiction over trial.

The dissent’s assertion that the practice is “oft-repeated” and “illicit” is correct. *Pfizer, Inc. v. Lord*, 447 F.2d 122 (2d Cir. 1971), and *In re Fine Paper Antitrust Litigation*, 685 F.2d 810 (3d Cir. 1982), are not binding authority for the proposition that a transferee court may transfer a case to itself for trial. Both cases acknowledged the statutory command to remand but justified departure on grounds of “convenience” and “practicality.” *Pfizer*, 447 F.2d at 124–25. But judicial policy preferences cannot override a clear statutory mandate. As this Court has repeatedly held, “Congress has spoken, and the courts must follow.” *United States v. Ron Pair Enters., Inc.*, 489 U.S. 235, 241 (1989). The JPML’s own rule, J.P.M.L.R.P. 14(b), which purports to authorize such transfers, is invalid to the extent it conflicts with § 1407(a). See *United States v. Mead Corp.*, 533 U.S. 218, 226 (2001) (“An agency’s interpretation of a statute is not binding if it is contrary to the statute’s clear meaning.”).

The Ninth Circuit’s refusal to issue mandamus is inconsistent with this Court’s precedent. In *Bauman v. United States District Court*, 557 F.2d 650 (9th Cir. 1977), the court emphasized that mandamus is appropriate when a district court acts “clearly in excess of its jurisdiction.” Id. at 653. Here, the district court has exceeded its jurisdiction by purporting to exercise trial jurisdiction over a case that Congress has mandated be remanded. The court’s order is not merely erroneous—it is a clear violation of § 1407(a). The prejudice is not correctable on appeal because the trial will be completed before appellate review can occur. See *Sunshine Beauty Supplies v. U.S. Dist. Court*, 872 F.2d 310, 311 (9th Cir. 1989) (holding that prejudice from an erroneous transfer is not correctable on appeal when it results in unnecessary travel and litigation costs).
