# ARGUMENT

## I. Due Process Requires the Government to Prove Beyond a Reasonable Doubt That the Conspiracy Persisted Into the Limitations Period

The Due Process Clause "protects the accused against conviction except upon proof beyond a reasonable doubt of every fact necessary to constitute the crime with which he is charged." *In re Winship*, 397 U.S. 358, 364 (1970). This Court has never permitted the prosecution to satisfy that burden by resting on inference alone once a defendant has come forward with evidence that negates an essential element of the charge. *Mullaney v. Wilbur*, 421 U.S. 684, 704 (1975) (state may not require defendant to bear "the burden of proving by a preponderance of the evidence" a fact bearing on the degree of criminal culpability). Continued membership in the charged conspiracy during the limitations period is not a peripheral matter; it is the fact that makes the prosecution timely and, indeed, makes petitioner a conspirator subject to conviction at all. Where petitioner adduced evidence that the conspiracy's architects, Rodney Moore and Kevin Gray, had split up before the statute-of-limitations window began, that evidence went directly to whether the single conspiracy charged in the indictment existed during the only period in which the government could lawfully prosecute it.

The government cannot escape *Winship* by relabeling this element an "affirmative defense." This Court has recognized that legislatures possess "much flexibility" in allocating burdens of proof for genuine affirmative defenses that "excuse conduct that would otherwise be punishable," such as insanity or duress. *Patterson v. New York*, 432 U.S. 197, 206–07 (1977); *Dixon v. United States*, 548 U.S. 1, 8 (2006) (duress does not negate an element of a firearms offense but instead excuses otherwise unlawful conduct, so Congress could place the burden on the defendant). Withdrawal from a conspiracy before the limitations period, by contrast, does not excuse continued participation—it disproves it. A defendant who withdraws is not asking to be excused for remaining a conspirator; he is asserting that he was not a conspirator during the time that matters. That is a direct negation of an element, not a plea for leniency despite guilt, and it therefore falls on the *Winship*/*Mullaney* side of the line rather than the *Patterson*/*Dixon* side.

## II. The Structure of Conspiracy Law Confirms That the Government Bears the Burden Once Withdrawal Evidence Is Presented

This Court has long recognized that a conspiracy, once formed, is presumed to continue until its purposes are abandoned or accomplished, and that the burden of showing termination or withdrawal traditionally rests on the party asserting it only in the sense of production, not persuasion. *Hyde v. United States*, 225 U.S. 347, 369 (1912) (conspiracy "continues up to the time of its abandonment or success"). But once a defendant has produced evidence sufficient to raise a genuine question whether the conspiracy of which he was a part terminated—through the splitting of its leadership, the cessation of its object, or his own affirmative acts of disassociation—the burden of persuasion must shift back to the government to prove continuation, just as it must prove every other disputed element. *United States v. Gaudin*, 515 U.S. 506, 522–23 (1995) (jury must find every element, including those that are "quintessentially" fact-bound, beyond a reasonable doubt, and the judge may not resolve such questions for the jury).

This allocation follows naturally from the statute-of-limitations analysis in *Toussie v. United States*, 397 U.S. 112, 122 (1970), which held that "the principle that statutes of limitations should be liberally interpreted in favor of repose" requires courts to resolve doubts about when an offense is complete against the government, not the accused. If ambiguity concerning the temporal scope of an offense is to be resolved against the government for purposes of interpreting when the limitations period runs, it would be anomalous to resolve the underlying factual question—whether the conspiracy in fact continued into that period—by relaxing the government's burden of proof. The same considerations of repose and fair notice that animate *Toussie* counsel that the prosecution, not the defendant, must eliminate reasonable doubt about continued membership.

## III. Practical and Fairness Considerations Support Placing the Burden on the Government

The government is far better positioned than the defendant to marshal proof of a conspiracy's continued operation—wiretaps, seized narcotics, testimony from cooperating co-conspirators, and financial records documenting ongoing enterprise activity. Petitioner, by contrast, must prove a negative: that nothing tied him to an organization from a point in the past forward. Requiring the accused to disprove ongoing participation, rather than requiring the government to prove it, inverts the "bedrock 'axiomatic and elementary' principle" that guilt must be proved, not innocence disproved. *Winship*, 397 U.S. at 363 (quoting *Coffin v. United States*, 156 U.S. 432, 453 (1895)). That principle applies with special force in RICO and drug-conspiracy prosecutions spanning many years and multiple co-defendants, where the risk of merging distinct criminal episodes into a single continuing conspiracy—and thereby circumventing the limitations period—is at its highest.

## CONCLUSION

For these reasons, this Court should hold that once a defendant produces evidence of withdrawal from a conspiracy prior to the statute-of-limitations period, the burden of persuasion shifts to the government to prove beyond a reasonable doubt that the defendant remained a member of the conspiracy during the relevant period. The judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
