ARGUMENT

The district court erred in refusing to resubmit the verdict forms to the jury for clarification. Where a verdict is ambiguous on its face, the trial court must take steps to ascertain the jury’s true intent before entering judgment. Rogers v. United States, 340 U.S. 367, 372 (1951) (verdict must “be certain, and free from ambiguity”). The forms returned here awarded Unit $70,768.02 on its contract claim while simultaneously awarding Enron a set-off of $86,494.30. The court’s decision to subtract one figure from the other without further inquiry produced a zero recovery that neither party’s evidence supported and that the instructions themselves did not authorize.

The same ambiguity required the court to grant Unit’s timely request that the jury be questioned before discharge. Andres v. United States, 333 U.S. 740, 748 (1948) (court has both the power and the duty to resolve uncertainty in the verdict). The instructions permitted the jury to reduce Unit’s recovery for defective performance and, separately, to decide Enron’s set-off claim. Nothing told the jury that it could not do both. When the verdicts revealed that possibility, the only safe course was to seek clarification rather than to impose a judicial construction that nullified the award on Form 1.

The court’s interpretation also rested on an impermissible assumption that the jury had performed the very subtraction the instructions left open. Baltimore & Carolina Line, Inc. v. Redman, 295 U.S. 654, 658 (1935) (verdict may not be molded to conform to a theory the jury was never told to apply). Because the record contains no basis for that assumption, the judgment cannot stand.

The district court’s refusal to clarify therefore requires reversal and remand for a new trial on damages.
