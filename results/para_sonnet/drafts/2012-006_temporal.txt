**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Presumption of Innocence Requires the Government to Prove Every Element of the Charged Conspiracy, Including Its Continuation Into the Limitations Period, Beyond a Reasonable Doubt.**

“The presumption of innocence … lies at the foundation of the administration of our criminal law.” Coffin v. United States, 156 U.S. 432, 453 (1895). That presumption is not a formality; it fixes where the burden of persuasion rests once a criminal charge is contested. In Davis v. United States, 160 U.S. 469, 484 (1895), this Court held that once evidence is introduced raising a reasonable doubt as to an element of the offense — there, sanity — the government must dispel that doubt beyond a reasonable doubt; the burden does not shift to the accused merely because he has come forward with proof. The same logic controls here. Once petitioners introduced evidence that the conspiracy’s architects, Rodney Moore and Kevin Gray, had severed their association before the statute-of-limitations window opened, the government was obligated to prove — not merely permitted to assume — that the conspiracy charged, in the form and membership relevant to the limitations period, persisted beyond that break.

**II. Continued Membership in the Conspiracy During the Limitations Period Is an Element of the Offense, Not an Affirmative Defense.**

This Court has long treated conspiracy as a continuing offense whose temporal boundaries are themselves part of what the government must prove. In United States v. Kissel, 218 U.S. 601, 608 (1910), the Court explained that “a conspiracy is a partnership in criminal purposes” that may continue in time, and that the statute of limitations is measured by the conspiracy’s continuing life, not merely its inception. Hyde v. United States, 225 U.S. 347, 369–370 (1912), reinforced that a conspiracy “continues down to the time of the accomplishment of the purpose” or “abandonment,” and that liability for the ongoing scheme depends on whether the defendant remained a participant during the relevant period. Fiswick v. United States, 329 U.S. 211, 216 (1946), likewise recognized that membership in a conspiracy is presumed to continue only in the absence of “an affirmative act” — but that presumption cannot supplant the government’s obligation to prove, as part of its case, that the defendant was still a member when the limitations clock was running. These cases confirm that the temporal scope of participation is bound up with the very identity of the crime charged; it is not a separate, defendant-carried defense but a fact the government must establish as surely as it must establish the existence of the agreement itself.

**III. Once Petitioners Produced Evidence of Withdrawal, the Government Retained the Ultimate Burden of Persuasion Beyond a Reasonable Doubt on Continued Membership.**

The principle that a legal presumption cannot relieve the prosecution of its constitutional burden is settled. In Morissette v. United States, 342 U.S. 246, 274–275 (1952), this Court held that the government must prove each element of a criminal offense beyond a reasonable doubt, and that a presumption of criminal intent could not be used to dispense with that proof once the defendant contested the element. Leary v. United States, 395 U.S. 6, 32–34 (1969), applied the same reasoning to a statutory inference of knowledge, holding that a permissive inference could not be allowed to stand in place of the government’s obligation to prove the fact beyond a reasonable doubt once the matter was genuinely disputed. Justice Jackson’s concurrence in Krulewitch v. United States, 336 U.S. 440, 445 (1949), sounded the same warning specifically in the conspiracy context, cautioning against loose reliance on a presumed “continuing conspiracy” to relieve the government of its evidentiary burdens. Read together, these decisions establish a single rule: a presumption of continuity — whether of intent, knowledge, or conspiratorial membership — may operate only until contested, and once contested, the burden of persuasion beyond a reasonable doubt reverts, as it always constitutionally rests, on the government.

**IV. The Rule Applied Below Improperly Treats Withdrawal as an Affirmative Defense, Shifting the Burden of Persuasion to the Defendant.**

The approach followed by the courts below, tracing to United States v. Borelli, 336 F.2d 376 (2d Cir. 1964), treated evidence of withdrawal as bearing only on the admissibility of post-withdrawal hearsay and as an affirmative defense that the defendant must establish. That approach cannot be reconciled with Kissel, Hyde, Fiswick, Morissette, and Leary. Those decisions treat the temporal continuity of the conspiracy as inseparable from its existence as a crime — an element, not a defense. To place the burden of disproving continued membership on the defendant, once he has come forward with evidence of separation from the enterprise, is to permit precisely the kind of presumption-driven shortcut to proof that this Court forbade in Davis and Morissette.

**CONCLUSION**

For these reasons, once petitioners introduced evidence that the conspiracy’s leadership had split apart before the limitations period began, the burden of persuasion beyond a reasonable doubt on the question whether petitioners remained members of a single conspiracy during that period remained with the government. The judgment below should be reversed.
