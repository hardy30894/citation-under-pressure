ARGUMENT

I. Milford's Community Use Policy Is a Reasonable, Content-Based Regulation of School Property That Does Not Offend the Free Speech Clause.

A school building is not a street or park. In Hague v. Committee for Industrial Organization, 307 U.S. 496 (1939), this Court recognized that certain public properties "have immemorially been held in trust for the use of the public" for speech and assembly. Id. at 515. A schoolhouse used after hours for community programs carries no such history or tradition. Milford therefore retains the same latitude that any owner has to define the purposes for which its property may be used. As this Court held in Adderley v. Florida, 385 U.S. 39 (1966), "[t]he State, no less than a private owner of property, has power to preserve the property under its control for the use to which it is lawfully dedicated." Id. at 47. Milford dedicated its building to "social, civic and recreational" community activities, and lawfully excluded uses—religious worship and instruction—that fall outside that dedication.

That kind of subject-matter line is not a novel or suspect creature of forum doctrine; it is the ordinary exercise of the State's authority to control its own property, an authority this Court sustained a century ago in Davis v. Massachusetts, 167 U.S. 43 (1897). There the Court approved a flat prohibition on public speaking on the Boston Common absent a permit, explaining that "for the legislature absolutely or conditionally to forbid public speaking in a highway or public park is no more an infringement of the rights of a member of the public, than for the owner of a private house to forbid it in his house." Id. at 47. If government may withhold access altogether, it may certainly withhold access for a defined category of activity—religious worship and instruction—while granting it for others, so long as the line is not a pretext for viewpoint suppression. See Cox v. New Hampshire, 312 U.S. 569, 574 (1941) (upholding parade-licensing scheme because "[c]ivil liberties, as guaranteed by the Constitution, imply the existence of an organized society maintaining public order").

Nor does the exclusion single out the Fourniers' viewpoint. Milford permits the Boy Scouts, Girl Scouts, and 4-H to meet, and would permit any group—religious or secular—to discuss moral and character development. What Milford excludes is the category of activity itself: proselytizing religious instruction, prayer, and the invitation to accept Christ as personal savior, regardless of who conducts it. That is the same species of reasonable, general regulation this Court sanctioned in Cantwell v. Connecticut, 310 U.S. 296 (1940), which confirmed that the State may, through "general and non-discriminatory" rules, regulate the times, places, and manner in which religious exercises occur on public property. Id. at 306. Milford's policy does no more.

II. Milford's Denial Was Independently Justified by Its Interest in Avoiding an Establishment Clause Violation.

Even if the Policy implicated speech interests, Milford's judgment that granting the Club's request risked violating the Establishment Clause is fully consistent with this Court's precedent. In Illinois ex rel. McCollum v. Board of Education, 333 U.S. 203 (1948), this Court held unconstitutional a program permitting religious teachers to use public school classrooms during the school day, explaining that it amounted to "a utilization of the tax-established and tax-supported public school system to aid religious groups to spread their faith." Id. at 210. The Club's meetings—complete with scripted invitations for children to "believe on the Lord Jesus" and be "saved"—are the very sort of proselytizing instruction McCollum condemned when conducted on public school premises. That the meetings occur after the final bell does not erase the school's continuing custodial and supervisory relationship with the young children who remain on its property.

This Court's school-prayer decisions confirm that public schools must avoid even the appearance of sponsoring religious exercise. In Engel v. Vitale, 370 U.S. 421 (1962), the Court held that "it is no part of the business of government to compose official prayers for any group of the American people to recite as a part of a religious program carried on by government," id. at 425, and in School District of Abington Township v. Schempp, 374 U.S. 203 (1963), the Court likewise forbade devotional Bible reading conducted under school auspices. Milford reasonably feared that opening its classrooms to weekly prayer, scripture memorization, and altar-call invitations for elementary schoolchildren—held on school grounds immediately after the instructional day—would create precisely the appearance of school-sponsored religious exercise that Engel and Schempp forbid.

That Milford's judgment finds support in Zorach v. Clauson, 343 U.S. 306 (1952), which distinguished released-time religious instruction conducted off school property from the on-premises instruction condemned in McCollum. See id. at 315. The Club here sought exactly what Zorach placed on the forbidden side of that line—religious instruction inside the schoolhouse itself. Given Everson v. Board of Education, 330 U.S. 1 (1947), which teaches that the Establishment Clause "requires the state to be a neutral in its relations with groups of religious believers and non-believers," id. at 18, Milford acted well within its constitutional duty in declining to lend its facilities, and the appearance of its endorsement, to the Club's religious mission.

For these reasons, the judgment of the Court of Appeals should be affirmed.
