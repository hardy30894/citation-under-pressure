**ARGUMENT**

Section 523(a)(2)(A) excepts from discharge only “any debt … for money, property, services, or an extension, renewal, or refinancing of credit, to the extent obtained by … actual fraud.” 11 U.S.C. § 523(a)(2)(A). The statutory text limits nondischargeability to the value actually obtained by the fraud; it does not reach additional sums imposed as punishment or deterrence. Grogan v. Garner, 498 U.S. 279, 287 (1991) (“a central purpose of the Code is to provide a procedure by which certain insolvent debtors can reorder their affairs … and be given a new opportunity in life … unhampered by the pressure and discouragement of preexisting debt”).

The rent overcharges totaling $31,382.50 were the only sums petitioners obtained. The treble damages awarded under the New Jersey Consumer Fraud Act, N.J.S.A. 56:8-19, constitute a statutory multiple imposed after the fact; they were not “obtained by” the fraudulent conduct. See Field v. Mans, 516 U.S. 59, 64-66 (1995) (the exception requires that the debtor have obtained the debt through the specified misconduct). Because the punitive component was not received by the debtor, it falls outside the plain language of the discharge exception.

Even if the underlying conduct constituted actual fraud, the Code’s “fresh start” policy precludes discharge of only those liabilities that represent the fruits of the fraud. Local Loan Co. v. Hunt, 292 U.S. 234, 244 (1934). Extending nondischargeability to statutorily multiplied awards would enlarge the exception beyond its text and undermine the “honest but unfortunate debtor” principle recognized in Grogan, 498 U.S. at 286-87.

The court below erred in holding the entire judgment nondischargeable. The compensatory portion alone, if any fraud is ultimately established, may be excepted; the treble damages, attorney’s fees, and costs must be discharged.
