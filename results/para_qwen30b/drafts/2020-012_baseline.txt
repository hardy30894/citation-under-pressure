**ARGUMENT**

The Railroad Retirement Board’s decision declining to reopen Petitioner’s 2006 application for a disability annuity is not a “final decision” subject to judicial review under 45 U.S.C. § 355(f). This Court has long held that the jurisdiction of the federal courts of appeals under the Railroad Retirement Act is limited to reviewing final decisions on the *merits* of a claim for benefits after administrative remedies have been exhausted. See *Roberts v. R.R. Retirement Bd.*, 346 F.3d 139, 140 (5th Cir. 2003). The Board’s refusal to reopen a prior claim does not constitute such a final decision, and this Court should affirm the Fifth Circuit’s dismissal of the petition for lack of jurisdiction.

The plain language of § 355(f) provides that “the courts of appeals shall have jurisdiction to review final decisions of the Board on the merits of a claim for benefits.” 45 U.S.C. § 355(f). The term “final decision” is not defined in the statute, but this Court has consistently interpreted it to mean a decision that resolves the *substantive* entitlement to benefits, not an administrative determination regarding procedural or remedial matters such as whether to reopen a prior claim. See *Califano v. Sanders*, 430 U.S. 99, 107 (1977) (holding that a decision not to reopen a claim is not a “final decision” within the meaning of the Social Security Act’s judicial review provision, and by analogy, the same principle applies to the Railroad Retirement Act). The Supreme Court emphasized that “the decision to reopen a claim is not a decision on the merits,” and thus not reviewable under the statute’s jurisdictional grant. *Id.* at 107–08.

This principle is fully consistent with the structure and purpose of the Railroad Retirement Act. The Act establishes a comprehensive administrative scheme for the adjudication of benefits claims, including specific procedures for reconsideration, appeals, and reopening. See 20 C.F.R. § 261.2. The Board’s authority to deny a request to reopen is an *administrative* function, not a determination of entitlement. It is a procedural gatekeeping mechanism, not a substantive adjudication of whether a claimant is entitled to benefits. As the Supreme Court has recognized, “the decision whether to reopen a case is not a decision on the merits, and therefore not a final decision subject to judicial review.” *Califano*, 430 U.S. at 107. The same reasoning applies here: the Board’s refusal to reopen Salinas’s 2006 application did not determine whether he is entitled to a disability annuity; it merely declined to reconsider a prior determination that had already become final.

The Fifth Circuit correctly applied this settled precedent in *Roberts v. R.R. Retirement Bd.*, 346 F.3d 139 (5th Cir. 2003), which held that this Court lacks jurisdiction to review a Board decision declining to reopen a prior benefits claim. *Id.* at 140. The panel in *Roberts* joined the majority of circuits—including the Fourth, Sixth, Seventh, and Tenth Circuits—that have reached the same conclusion, finding the reasoning of *Califano* persuasive. *Id.* at 141. The dissenting views in *Sones v. R.R. Retirement Bd.*, 933 F.2d 636 (8th Cir. 1991), and *Szostak v. R.R. Retirement Bd.*, 370 F.2d 253 (2d Cir. 1966), were rightly rejected as inconsistent with the statutory text and the Supreme Court’s precedent. The D.C. Circuit’s contrary holding in *Stovic v. Railroad Retirement Board*, 826 F.3d 500 (D.C. Cir. 2016), does not alter this analysis. Even if *Stovic* were correct, it is not binding on this Court, and the Fifth Circuit was not free to disregard its own controlling precedent. See *Vaughan v. Anderson Reg’l Med. Ctr.*, 849 F.3d 588, 591 (5th Cir. 2017), cert. denied, 138 S. Ct. 101 (2017).

Salinas’s argument that the Board’s decision is “final” because it is “final” for purposes of reopening under 20 C.F.R. § 261.2 is misplaced. The regulation itself acknowledges that a decision denying a request to reopen is not a decision on the merits. Section 261.2(a) provides that a request to reopen must be filed within four years of the date of the original decision, and that the Board may reopen a case only upon a showing of new and material evidence or administrative error. The regulation does not confer finality for judicial review purposes; it merely establishes a procedural deadline. The fact that a decision is “final” for administrative purposes does not make it reviewable under § 355(f). See *Roberts*, 346 F.3d at 140.

Moreover, allowing judicial review of reopening denials would undermine the administrative efficiency and finality that Congress intended. If every denial of a reopening request were subject to judicial review, the Board would face an endless stream of litigation over procedural decisions, and the entire benefits system would be burdened with appeals that do not resolve the underlying entitlement. This would frustrate the statutory scheme, which contemplates that claims are resolved through a single, final determination on the merits—after all administrative remedies have been exhausted.

For these reasons, the Board’s decision not to reopen Salinas’s 2006 application is not a “final decision” within the meaning of § 355(f). The Fifth Circuit correctly dismissed the petition for lack of jurisdiction. The judgment should be affirmed.
