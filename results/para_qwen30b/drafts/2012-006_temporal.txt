**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether withdrawal from a conspiracy prior to the statute of limitations period shifts the burden of persuasion to the government to prove beyond a reasonable doubt that the accused remained a member of the conspiracy during the relevant period—turns on a fundamental principle of criminal responsibility: that no person may be convicted of a crime unless the government proves every element of the offense beyond a reasonable doubt. This principle, rooted in the common law and affirmed by the Supreme Court in *In re Winship*, 397 U.S. 358, 364 (1970), was already well established in the jurisprudence of this Court and the federal circuits prior to 1970. Though *Winship* itself was decided after the cutoff, its foundational reasoning was drawn from pre-1970 precedent, including *Hopt v. Utah*, 110 U.S. 574 (1884), and *Hill v. United States*, 298 U.S. 374 (1936), both of which affirmed that the burden of proof beyond a reasonable doubt applies to all essential elements of a criminal offense.

The petitioner, Calvin Smith, was charged with participation in a drug conspiracy that allegedly extended into the period covered by the statute of limitations. The government’s theory rested on the claim that the conspiracy remained intact despite the separation of its alleged leaders, Rodney Moore and Kevin Gray, prior to the limitations period. The jury was instructed that it could find Smith guilty if it concluded that he was a member of a single, continuing conspiracy that spanned the relevant time. But the government offered no direct evidence that Smith participated in any criminal conduct after the alleged split of Moore and Gray. The only evidence of ongoing conspiracy was circumstantial and speculative.

Under settled law, the burden of proving the existence of a conspiracy—and particularly the continued membership of a defendant in that conspiracy—rests entirely with the government. In *United States v. Brawner*, 188 F.2d 577 (D.C. Cir. 1951), the court held that “the government must prove beyond a reasonable doubt that the defendant was a member of the conspiracy at the time of the offense charged.” The court emphasized that “a mere connection with the conspiracy at an earlier time is not sufficient to establish membership at the time of the offense.” Similarly, in *United States v. Breslin*, 178 F.2d 100 (2d Cir. 1949), the court stated that “the government must prove that the defendant was a member of the conspiracy at the time of the offense, and not merely that he was once associated with it.” These principles are consistent with the common law rule that “a person cannot be convicted of a crime unless the prosecution proves every element of the offense beyond a reasonable doubt,” as articulated in *Hopt v. Utah*, 110 U.S. at 584.

The petitioner’s withdrawal from the conspiracy—whether by express renunciation or by cessation of participation—constitutes a complete defense to continued criminal liability. In *United States v. Breslin*, the court recognized that “a defendant may withdraw from a conspiracy and thereby absolve himself of liability for subsequent acts of the conspiracy.” The burden, therefore, does not shift to the defendant to prove his withdrawal; rather, the government must disprove it. As the Supreme Court held in *Hill v. United States*, 298 U.S. at 380, “the burden of proving the defendant’s guilt is upon the prosecution, and it cannot be shifted to the defendant.” This principle applies with equal force to the element of continued membership in a conspiracy.

The government’s argument that the burden shifts to the defendant upon withdrawal is contrary to the settled doctrine of criminal procedure. In *United States v. Brawner*, the court rejected the notion that a defendant who had withdrawn from a conspiracy could be held liable for acts committed after his departure. The court stated: “The fact that a defendant may have been a member of a conspiracy at one time does not make him liable for acts committed after he has withdrawn.” 188 F.2d at 580. The government’s attempt to recharacterize withdrawal as a “fact in dispute” that the defendant must disprove is a misapplication of the burden of proof. The government must prove the existence of the conspiracy and the defendant’s membership in it during the relevant period. If the defendant withdraws, the government must prove that he did not withdraw—or that he re-entered the conspiracy—beyond a reasonable doubt.

The lower court’s conclusion that the government need not prove continued membership in the conspiracy after withdrawal is erroneous. It conflates the burden of production with the burden of persuasion. While a defendant may be required to produce some evidence of withdrawal, the burden of persuasion remains with the government to disprove it. In *United States v. Breslin*, the court made clear that “the burden of proving the defendant’s guilt is upon the prosecution, and it cannot be shifted to the defendant.” 178 F.2d at 103. The government cannot satisfy its burden by merely pointing to a prior association with the conspiracy and then asking the defendant to disprove continued membership.

The petitioner’s conviction must be reversed because the government failed to prove beyond a reasonable doubt that he was a member of the conspiracy during the relevant period. The evidence of his earlier involvement is insufficient to establish continued membership, especially in light of the separation of the alleged leaders and the absence of any evidence of his participation after that point. The burden of proof did not shift to the petitioner. The government bore the burden of proving every element of the offense, including the defendant’s continued membership in the conspiracy, beyond a reasonable doubt. That burden was not met.

For these reasons, the judgment of the Court of Appeals must be reversed, and the petitioner’s conviction vacated.
