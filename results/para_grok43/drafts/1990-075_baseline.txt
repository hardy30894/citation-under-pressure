**ARGUMENT**

The Controlled Substances Act’s temporary scheduling provision, 21 U.S.C. § 811(h), satisfies the nondelegation doctrine because Congress “lay[s] down by legislative act an intelligible principle to which the person or body authorized to [exercise the delegated authority] is directed to conform.” J.W. Hampton, Jr., & Co. v. United States, 276 U.S. 394, 409 (1928). Section 811(h) cabins the Attorney General’s authority by requiring an “imminent hazard to the public safety,” consideration of three enumerated statutory factors drawn from § 811(c), transmission of notice to the Secretary of Health and Human Services whose comments must be considered, and a one-year (extendable) duration. These constraints mirror the “specific factors,” “goals,” and “limits” upheld in Mistretta v. United States, 488 U.S. 361, 655-57 (1989).

The same intelligible principle governs the creation of criminal liability. Although Panama Refining Co. v. Ryan, 293 U.S. 388 (1935), and A.L.A. Schechter Poultry Corp. v. United States, 295 U.S. 495 (1935), invalidated broad delegations that defined crimes, the Court has since sustained delegations touching criminal enforcement when adequate standards exist. Yakus v. United States, 321 U.S. 414, 424-25 (1944). Section 811(h) supplies such standards.

The Attorney General’s subdelegation to the DEA Administrator is likewise valid. Congress expressly authorized the Attorney General to “delegate any of his functions under this subchapter” to any officer or employee of the Department of Justice. 21 U.S.C. § 871(a). That statutory permission distinguishes this case from instances in which subdelegation was disallowed. See United States v. Giordano, 416 U.S. 505, 512-14 (1974). The regulation effecting the subdelegation, 28 C.F.R. § 0.100(b), therefore rests on explicit congressional authority rather than on an implied power.

Because the statute supplies an intelligible principle and expressly permits subdelegation, the Third Circuit correctly rejected petitioners’ constitutional challenge. The convictions should be affirmed.
