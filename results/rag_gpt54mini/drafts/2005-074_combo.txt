**ARGUMENT**

I. **Section 241(a)(5) does not apply to aliens whose illegal reentry preceded its 1997 effective date.**

Congress knows how to make immigration consequences apply to pre-enactment conduct, and when it does so, it speaks expressly. In *Lehmann v. United States ex rel. Carson*, 353 U.S. 685 (1957), the Court upheld deportation provisions that by their own terms applied “notwithstanding” that the alien entered before enactment or that the disqualifying facts predated the statute. 353 U.S. at 688-89. Likewise, in *Mulcahey v. Catalanotte*, 353 U.S. 692 (1957), the Court sustained deportation for a narcotics conviction that occurred before the statute made that conviction a deportable offense, because Congress had enacted a provision reaching an alien “who at any time has been convicted” of the offense. 353 U.S. at 693-94. The same logic appears in *Mrvica v. Esperdy*, 376 U.S. 560 (1964), where the Court enforced a residency condition against an alien whose 1942 departure defeated eligibility for later discretionary relief. These cases show that retroactive coverage depends on clear congressional direction. See also *Immigration & Naturalization Service v. Errico*, 385 U.S. 214, 216-18 (1966); *United States ex rel. Hintopoulos v. Shaughnessy*, 353 U.S. 72, 76-78 (1957).

Section 241(a)(5) contains no comparable express command that it applies to aliens who reentered before its effective date. The provision states that a prior order “is reinstated” if the alien “has reentered” illegally, but that language is naturally read to govern reinstatement once the statute is in force, not to attach new legal consequences to completed reentries that occurred years earlier. See *Lehmann*, 353 U.S. at 688-89. Congress’s silence matters especially because it elsewhere has shown it knows how to write clear temporal reach. Id.; *Mulcahey*, 353 U.S. at 693-94. Under basic principles of statutory interpretation, the Court should not infer retroactive application from ambiguous text. See *Shaughnessy v. Pedreiro*, 349 U.S. 48, 51-52 (1955); *Foti v. Immigration & Naturalization Service*, 375 U.S. 217, 222-26 (1963); *Cheng Fan Kwok v. Immigration & Naturalization Service*, 392 U.S. 206, 212-15 (1968).

II. **Even if Section 241(a)(5) reaches pre-enactment reentries, applying it here is impermissibly retroactive.**

A statute has retroactive effect when it “attaches new legal consequences to events completed before its enactment.” *Lehmann*, 353 U.S. at 688-89; *Mulcahey*, 353 U.S. at 694. Fernandez-Vargas completed the critical conduct—his illegal reentry—in 1982, long before the 1997 statute. He then lived for nearly two decades in the United States, and only after marrying a citizen did he seek to adjust his status. Applying Section 241(a)(5) to him would not merely regulate future conduct; it would extinguish the possibility of relief based on a past reentry that was completed before the statute existed. That is the type of retroactive consequence the Court has repeatedly scrutinized. See *Mrvica*, 376 U.S. at 562-64; *Hintopoulos*, 353 U.S. at 78-81.

The Government will rely on the idea that immigration laws may be applied to existing conditions, but the pre-1970 cases do not support that proposition in the absence of clear statutory language. In *Lehmann*, the statute expressly covered aliens who entered before enactment; in *Mulcahey*, Congress expressly covered convictions occurring “at any time”; and in *Errico*, the Court emphasized the breadth of the enacted exception’s text. 353 U.S. at 688-89; 353 U.S. at 693-94; 385 U.S. at 216-18. Here, by contrast, Congress did not clearly say that prior illegal reentries would be swept into the new reinstatement regime. Where Congress has not spoken clearly, the Court should not presume it intended to deprive aliens of avenues to seek discretionary relief based on conduct that predated the law. See *Hintopoulos*, 353 U.S. at 77-81; *Foti*, 375 U.S. at 222-26.

Finally, the Government cannot characterize this case as merely involving a continuing immigration status. Fernandez-Vargas’s reentry was a completed historical event, not an ongoing offense. The statute’s practical effect is to foreclose relief that was available when he reentered and for years afterward. That is retroactive in the sense condemned by the Court’s deportation decisions. See *Low Wah Suey v. Backus*, 225 U.S. 460, 468-69 (1912) (recognizing Congress’s power to regulate deportation, but within the statutory limits Congress sets); *Shaughnessy v. Pedreiro*, 349 U.S. at 51-52. Because the statute neither clearly commands this result nor can be applied without imposing new disabilities on completed conduct, the petition should be granted.
