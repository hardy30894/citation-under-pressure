**ARGUMENT**

The decision of the court of appeals should be affirmed because the Internal Revenue Service cannot collect a partnership’s tax deficiency from individual partners without first making individualized assessments against those partners. The three-year statute of limitations under 26 U.S.C. § 6501(a) has long since expired, and the assessment against the partnership did not extend the limitations period as to the individual partners.

**I. The Assessment Against the Partnership Did Not Constitute an Assessment Against the Individual Partners**

Section 6203 of the Internal Revenue Code requires that an “assessment shall be made by recording the liability of the taxpayer.” 26 U.S.C. § 6203. The term “taxpayer” is defined as “any person subject to any internal revenue tax,” and “person” includes both “an individual” and a “partnership.” 26 U.S.C. § 7701(a)(14), (a)(1). Because the statute treats individuals and partnerships as distinct taxpayers, an assessment against the partnership cannot, by itself, constitute an assessment against the individual partners.

The implementing regulation reinforces this conclusion, providing that a valid assessment “shall provide identification of the taxpayer.” 26 C.F.R. § 301.6203-1. Where, as here, the assessment identifies only the partnership and not the individual partners, the IRS has failed to satisfy the statutory requirement of identifying the taxpayer against whom the assessment is made.

This Court’s decision in *United States v. Coson*, 286 F.2d 453 (9th Cir. 1961), supports this interpretation. In *Coson*, the IRS assessed unpaid taxes against a partnership and later claimed a lien against the property of an alleged general partner. *Id.* at 454. The district court held that “such a lien does not exist against a particular individual’s property pursuant to §§ 6321 and 6322 unless the underlying tax obligation has been assessed against him under § 6203.” *Coson v. United States*, 169 F. Supp. 671, 676 (S.D. Cal. 1958). This Court affirmed, albeit on other grounds, but did not disturb the district court’s reasoning that an assessment against the partnership alone is insufficient to support collection from individual partners. *Coson*, 286 F.2d at 458, 462-63.

**II. The Statute of Limitations Bars Collection from the Individual Partners**

Under 26 U.S.C. § 6501(a), the IRS must assess any tax within three years after the return is filed. The IRS concedes that it did not assess the individual partners within that period. The assessment against the partnership extended the statute of limitations for collection to ten years under 26 U.S.C. § 6502(a), but only as to the partnership. Because the partners are separate taxpayers, the assessment against the partnership had no effect on the limitations period applicable to them.

The IRS’s reliance on *Young v. Riddell*, 283 F.2d 909 (9th Cir. 1960), is misplaced. In *Young*, the partner had voluntarily paid the tax and sought a refund. *Id.* at 910. This Court held only that the partner could not recover a refund because he was liable for the partnership’s debts under state law. *Id.* The Court did not hold that the IRS could collect from an unwilling partner without an individual assessment. To the contrary, the district court in *Young* expressly acknowledged that “to enable collection of the tax without resorting to court action,” the IRS would need to name the individual partners in the assessment. *Young v. Riddell*, 60-1 U.S. Tax Cas. (CCH) ¶ 9831, at 76,050 (S.D. Cal. 1959). Because the partner in *Young* had already paid, the court had no occasion to address whether the IRS could compel payment from an unassessed partner. *Id.* at 76,054.

**III. State Law Does Not Provide an Independent Basis for Collection**

The IRS argues that California partnership law, which makes partners jointly and severally liable for partnership debts, allows it to collect from the partners without an individual assessment. But California law requires a creditor to obtain a judgment against the partner before satisfying a partnership debt from the partner’s assets. Cal. Corp. Code § 16307(c). The IRS obtained no such judgment against the partners, and the statute of limitations for doing so has expired. 26 U.S.C. § 6501(a).

**CONCLUSION**

The judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
