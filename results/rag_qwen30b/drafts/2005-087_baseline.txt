**ARGUMENT**

The Fourteenth Amendment does not require a state to permit a defendant to use expert evidence of mental illness to rebut the prosecution’s burden of proving criminal intent, where the state law properly confines such evidence to the affirmative defense of insanity. The Arizona Supreme Court correctly held that the trial court did not violate Clark’s due process rights by limiting the use of his expert testimony to the insanity defense. This conclusion is compelled by precedent, including *Leland v. Oregon*, 343 U.S. 790 (1952), and *Rivera v. Delaware*, 429 U.S. 877 (1976), which affirm that a state may structure its criminal procedure so that evidence relevant to insanity is not automatically admissible to negate mens rea, so long as the prosecution retains the burden of proving all elements of the crime beyond a reasonable doubt.

In *Leland*, the Court upheld a state’s procedure under which a jury could find a defendant guilty of first-degree murder even if it believed the defendant lacked the capacity to premeditate or form intent—so long as it did not find him legally insane. The Court emphasized that “the prosecution was required to prove beyond a reasonable doubt every element of the crime charged, including, in the case of first degree murder, premeditation, deliberation, malice and intent.” 343 U.S. at 799. The Court further noted that “the evidence adduced during this trial to prove defendant’s insanity shall be considered and weighed by you, with all other evidence, whether or not you find defendant insane, in regard to the ability of the defendant to premeditate, form a purpose, to deliberate, act wilfully, and act maliciously.” Id. This language confirms that *Leland* did not bar the state from limiting the *purpose* of expert testimony to the insanity defense, even as the jury was instructed to consider that same evidence in assessing the presence of criminal intent. The Court recognized that the jury could find a defendant incapable of forming intent *without* finding him legally insane—thus affirming the legitimacy of a bifurcated inquiry.

Similarly, in *Rivera v. Delaware*, the Court rejected the argument that due process required a state to allow a defendant to use mental illness evidence to negate mens rea independently of the insanity defense. The Court observed that “the existence or nonexistence of legal insanity bears no necessary relationship to the existence or nonexistence of the required mental elements of the crime.” 429 U.S. at 877. This distinction is critical: a defendant may lack the capacity to form criminal intent yet still be legally sane under state law. The Court in *Rivera* reaffirmed that “a State may characterize the insanity defense as it chooses,” and that “the requirement of Winship that the State prove all elements of the crime was one of substance, not limited to ‘a State’s definition of the elements of a crime.’” Id. (quoting *Mullaney v. Wilbur*, 421 U.S. 684, 705 (1975)). Thus, Arizona’s decision to treat the insanity defense as a separate, affirmative defense—requiring the defendant to prove it by a preponderance of the evidence—does not violate due process, even if it restricts the use of expert testimony to that defense.

Clark’s argument that he was denied due process because he could not use his expert evidence to rebut the prosecution’s proof of intent misapprehends the nature of the burden of proof. The prosecution bore the burden of proving criminal intent beyond a reasonable doubt. The trial court did not prevent Clark from introducing evidence of his mental state; it merely ruled that such evidence could not be used to undermine the prosecution’s burden unless it was offered in support of the insanity defense. That is a permissible procedural rule. As the Court stated in *Moore v. Duckworth*, 443 U.S. 713, 717 (1979), “the prosecution failed to meet its burden because it relied upon lay witnesses to prove sanity without providing any expert testimony to rebut his expert opinion testimony.” But the Court did not hold that the defendant must be allowed to use expert testimony to attack mens rea; rather, it held that the prosecution must meet its burden. Here, the prosecution met that burden, and Clark was not deprived of a fair opportunity to present his case.

Moreover, Clark’s claim is foreclosed by *Massey v. Moore*, 348 U.S. 105 (1954), which held that a defendant may not collaterally attack a conviction based on a claim of insanity that was properly resolved at trial. The Court emphasized that “petitioner has not had such a hearing” on the issue of his competence to stand trial, but in this case, Clark *did* have a full hearing on his insanity defense. The Arizona courts properly evaluated the evidence, and the jury was instructed on the burden of proof. The trial court’s ruling did not deprive Clark of a meaningful opportunity to present his defense.

Finally, Clark’s reliance on *Davis v. United States*, 160 U.S. 469 (1895), is misplaced. That case involved a jury instruction that the defendant must be found insane “beyond a doubt” to be acquitted—a standard that the Court in *Winship* later condemned. But Arizona did not impose such a standard. The jury was instructed that the prosecution must prove all elements of the crime beyond a reasonable doubt, and that Clark bore the burden of proving insanity by a preponderance of the evidence. This is consistent with due process.

For these reasons, the Arizona Supreme Court correctly concluded that Clark’s due process rights were not violated. The state law did not prevent him from presenting expert evidence of his mental state; it merely governed the permissible use of that evidence. That is a valid exercise of state authority under the Fourteenth Amendment.

**WHEREFORE**, the judgment of the Arizona Supreme Court should be affirmed.
