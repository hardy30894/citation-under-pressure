The government’s position should be rejected because the Internal Revenue Code ties the ten-year collection period to a valid assessment against the taxpayer whose liability the government seeks to enforce. See 26 U.S.C. § 6502(a); 26 U.S.C. § 6203. A partnership and its individual partners are separate “taxpayer[s]” under the Code. 26 U.S.C. § 7701(a)(1), (14). Accordingly, an assessment against the partnership does not, without more, extend the limitations period for collecting from individual partners. United States v. Galletti, 541 U.S. 114, 119-21 (2004) (explaining that assessment against a partnership extends the period only as to the assessed taxpayer); see also 26 C.F.R. § 301.6203-1.

The text confirms that result. Section 6203 provides that an “assessment shall be made by recording the liability of the taxpayer.” 26 U.S.C. § 6203. Section 6502(a) then allows collection by levy or suit only within ten years after “the assessment of the tax.” 26 U.S.C. § 6502(a). Those provisions operate together: the assessment must identify the taxpayer whose liability is being recorded, and the resulting ten-year period runs from that assessment. Galletti, 541 U.S. at 121-22. Because the IRS assessed only the partnership here, it did not secure a ten-year extension for separate collection actions against Abel Galletti, Sarah Galletti, Francesco Briguglio, or Angela Briguglio. Id.; see also United States v. Coson, 286 F.2d 453, 458-63 (9th Cir. 1961) (discussing the necessity of a proper assessment tied to the person from whom the government seeks to collect).

The government’s contrary theory improperly collapses the distinction between substantive liability and procedural enforceability. State law may make partners jointly and severally liable for partnership debts, but that does not eliminate federal procedural requirements for assessment and collection. Cal. Corp. Code § 16307(c); United States v. Galletti, 541 U.S. at 123-24; United States v. Young, 283 F.2d 909, 910 (9th Cir. 1960). As the Ninth Circuit explained below, a partnership debt may be owed by the partners, yet the IRS still must either assess the partners individually or obtain a judgment against them before reaching their assets. United States v. Galletti, 302 F.3d 1136, 1141-44 (9th Cir. 2002), rev’d, 541 U.S. 114 (2004). That is especially so in bankruptcy, where a claim is disallowed if it is unenforceable against the debtor under applicable law. 11 U.S.C. § 502(b)(1); Travelers Cas. & Sur. Co. of Am. v. Pacific Gas & Elec. Co., 549 U.S. 443, 450-51 (2007).

This Court’s precedents support respondent’s position. In Young v. Riddell, the Court held that a partner’s liability for partnership taxes arose under state law, but the decision did not hold that the IRS could dispense with an assessment or judgment against the partner when collecting from him personally. 283 F.2d at 910. Likewise, in Coson, the Court recognized that an assessment that failed to identify the taxpayer was defective as a basis for collection against that person’s property. 286 F.2d at 458, 462-63. Those cases are consistent with the rule that the government must assess the taxpayer from whom it seeks collection. Galletti, 541 U.S. at 122-24.

The government relies on United States v. Wright, 57 F.3d 561 (7th Cir. 1995), but that case is inapposite. As the Ninth Circuit correctly noted, Wright involved assessments against both the partnership and the individual partners, so the only issue was tolling. Galletti, 302 F.3d at 1155-56. Wright therefore does not answer the question here: whether an assessment against the partnership alone extends the limitations period for direct collection from unassessed partners. Id.; see also United States v. Galletti, 541 U.S. at 124.

Because the IRS never assessed these individual debtors within the three-year period in 26 U.S.C. § 6501(a), and because no timely judgment was obtained against them, the government’s claim is time-barred. The judgment below should be affirmed.
