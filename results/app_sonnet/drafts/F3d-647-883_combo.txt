ARGUMENT

I. BUCKLES'S RELIANCE ON THE CLERK'S OFFICE STATEMENT ENTITLES HIM TO EQUITABLE TOLLING UNDER THE "UNIQUE CIRCUMSTANCES" DOCTRINE

This Court need not decide whether its June 12, 2007 order actually restarted the ninety-day certiorari clock, because even if the petition was formally untimely under Supreme Court Rule 13, Buckles is entitled to equitable relief. The Supreme Court has long recognized that a litigant who acts in reliance on a court's own representation about the time for seeking review should not be penalized when that representation later proves erroneous. In Harris Truck Lines, Inc. v. Cherry Meat Packers, Inc., 371 U.S. 215 (1962), the Court held that a party who filed within a period the district court itself had authorized, even though the authorization did not strictly comply with the governing rules, was entitled to have its appeal treated as timely because of the "unique circumstances" of the case. The Court reaffirmed that doctrine in Thompson v. Immigration & Naturalization Service, 375 U.S. 384 (1964), again excusing an untimely filing where the petitioner had reasonably relied on the trial court's own conduct in fixing the time to act. The logic of Harris Truck Lines and Thompson controls here. Buckles did not sit on his rights or guess at the deadline; he received an affirmative representation, through his sister's telephone call to this Court's Clerk, that "the timeline for filing the Writ of Certiorari would be stayed until a ruling was made on the Petition for Appointment of Counsel and a new mandate date would be issued." A pro se prisoner who reasonably relies on such a statement from the very court whose mandate governs his deadline stands in the same position as the litigants in Harris Truck Lines and Thompson.

II. THIS COURT HAS TRADITIONALLY EXTENDED SPECIAL SOLICITUDE TO PRO SE PRISONERS SEEKING POST-CONVICTION REVIEW

The unique-circumstances doctrine dovetails with the settled principle that pro se prisoners' filings in post-conviction proceedings are not to be measured by the same exacting standards applied to pleadings drafted by counsel. In Holiday v. Johnston, 313 U.S. 342 (1941), the Court instructed that habeas applications prepared by laymen should be read practically, not technically. That teaching was reaffirmed in Price v. Johnston, 334 U.S. 266 (1948), where the Court cautioned against holding prisoners to the same procedural precision expected of trained advocates. This solicitude reflects the broader principle articulated in Sanders v. United States, 373 U.S. 1 (1963), that "[c]onventional notions of finality of litigation have no place where life or liberty is at stake." 373 U.S. at 8. And in Fay v. Noia, 372 U.S. 391 (1963), the Court recognized that a prisoner's procedural missteps, particularly those attributable to circumstances beyond his own considered choice, should not automatically foreclose the Great Writ's protections. Buckles's situation—an incarcerated, indigent, formerly-represented litigant attempting to navigate a jurisdictional deadline without the benefit of counsel—is precisely the kind of circumstance these cases contemplated.

III. DENYING RELIEF WOULD OFFEND THE PRINCIPLE THAT ACCESS TO APPELLATE REVIEW MAY NOT TURN ON A PRISONER'S INDIGENCE OR LACK OF COUNSEL

Buckles's predicament arose directly from his indigence: he could no longer afford retained counsel and was forced to seek court-appointed assistance under Circuit Rule 4-1(e), precisely the situation Griffin v. Illinois, 351 U.S. 12 (1956), sought to prevent. As the Court declared, "[t]here can be no equal justice where the kind of trial a man gets depends on the amount of money he has." 351 U.S. at 19. The Court extended this principle to appellate proceedings in Coppedge v. United States, 369 U.S. 438 (1962), holding that indigency may not be permitted to erect barriers to appellate review that a defendant of means would not face. Buckles's attempt to secure appointed certiorari counsel—an attempt frustrated by his retained counsel's unresponsiveness and, he alleges, by inaccurate guidance from this Court's own Clerk—is the modern analogue of the financial barrier condemned in Griffin and Coppedge.

Finally, the Court has recognized that where circumstances beyond a prisoner's control interfere with timely filing, rigid enforcement of a deadline can work an injustice inconsistent with the interests that filing deadlines are meant to serve. Fallen v. United States, 378 U.S. 139 (1964). Buckles's delay was not the product of neglect; it was the product of reliance on representations from officers of this very Court.

IV. AT MINIMUM, THE CASE SHOULD BE REMANDED FOR AN EVIDENTIARY HEARING

Because the record does not conclusively establish what the Clerk's office told Buckles's sister, this Court should, consistent with the solicitude shown pro se litigants in Holiday and Price, remand for an evidentiary hearing to determine whether the Clerk's office in fact made the representation Buckles alleges. If it did, the unique-circumstances doctrine of Harris Truck Lines and Thompson, informed by the equitable principles of Sanders, Griffin, and Coppedge, compels equitable tolling of both the certiorari and § 2255 deadlines.
