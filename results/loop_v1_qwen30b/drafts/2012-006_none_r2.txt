The question presented—whether the government bears the burden of proving beyond a reasonable doubt that a defendant remained a member of a conspiracy during the relevant statute of limitations period after withdrawing from the conspiracy—turns on a foundational principle of criminal law: the prosecution bears the burden of proving every element of the offense, including membership in a conspiracy, beyond a reasonable doubt. This burden does not shift to the defendant merely because he asserts that he withdrew from the conspiracy. The government’s obligation to prove guilt remains intact, and the defendant’s withdrawal is not a defense that extinguishes the prosecution’s duty to establish the essential elements of the crime.

The Supreme Court has long held that the prosecution bears the burden of proving guilt beyond a reasonable doubt. In *Coffin v. United States*, 156 U.S. 432, 453 (1895), the Court declared that “the burden of proof is upon the prosecution to establish the guilt of the accused beyond a reasonable doubt.” This principle was reaffirmed in *Hopt v. Utah*, 110 U.S. 574, 581 (1884), where the Court stated that “the burden of proof is upon the prosecution to establish the guilt of the accused beyond a reasonable doubt.” These decisions, grounded in the due process protections of the Fifth and Fourteenth Amendments, establish that the burden of proof never shifts to the defendant, regardless of the nature of the defense raised.
