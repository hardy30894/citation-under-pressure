**ARGUMENT**

**I. Federal courts do not possess exclusive jurisdiction over Minton's state-law legal-malpractice claim merely because it requires resolution of a hypothetical patent-law question.**

**A. Section 1338(a) reaches only claims in which patent law creates the cause of action or in which a substantial patent question is necessarily presented.**

Congress extended federal jurisdiction under 28 U.S.C. § 1338(a) to any case "in which a well-pleaded complaint establishes either that federal patent law creates the cause of action or that the plaintiff's right to relief necessarily depends on resolution of a substantial question of federal patent law, in that patent law is a necessary element of one of the well-pleaded claims." *Christianson v. Colt Indus. Operating Corp.*, 486 U.S. 800, 808–09 (1988). Minton's cause of action is Texas legal malpractice; patent law enters only as an element of proof in the "case within a case" that any malpractice plaintiff must establish. Placing this dispute in the narrower *Christianson* category—claims that "necessarily depend" on patent law—requires the closer substantiality analysis that this Court later supplied in *Grable & Sons Metal Products, Inc. v. Darue Engineering & Manufacturing*, 545 U.S. 308 (2005).

**B. The embedded patent issue is not "substantial" within the meaning of *Grable*.**

*Grable* asks "does a state-law claim necessarily raise a stated federal issue, actually disputed and substantial, which a federal forum may entertain without disturbing any congressionally approved balance of federal and state judicial responsibilities." 545 U.S. at 314. Substantiality is measured by the issue's importance to the federal system generally, not merely to the parties. In *Grable*, the question—the adequacy of IRS notice under the tax-sale statute—was substantial because it was an issue of federal tax administration that would recur in future controversies and implicated the government's capacity to enforce its claims nationwide. *Id.* at 315. The on-sale-bar and experimental-use questions embedded in Minton's malpractice suit share none of those characteristics. They are backward-looking and case-specific: they ask only what a hypothetical court would have decided in already-concluded federal litigation. No patent will issue, survive, or fall depending on the answer; the '643 patent was declared invalid by final federal judgment years before this malpractice suit began. A dispute of this kind is precisely the sort that must be laid aside under the "selective process which picks the substantial causes out of the web and lays the other ones aside." *Gully v. First Nat'l Bank in Meridian*, 299 U.S. 109, 117–18 (1936). It likewise fails the older formulation that a claim does not arise under federal law unless it "really and substantially involves a dispute or controversy respecting the validity, construction, or effect of such a law, upon the determination of which the result depends." *Shulthis v. McDougal*, 225 U.S. 561, 569 (1912).

**C. Exclusive federal jurisdiction here would disturb, not preserve, the balance of judicial responsibility Congress has struck.**

Legal malpractice is a quintessential subject of state concern, governed by state-defined standards of duty, breach, causation, and damages. Texas courts are fully competent to resolve the hypothetical patent question necessary to decide the "case within a case," just as state courts routinely resolve embedded federal issues arising in malpractice, contract, and professional-negligence suits against lawyers who practiced in federally regulated fields. Unlike *Grable*, where a federal forum was warranted because of the government's "direct interest in the availability of a federal forum to vindicate its own administrative action," 545 U.S. at 315, no federal executive or regulatory interest is implicated by a state court's backward-looking assessment of what a competent patent lawyer should have argued in already-terminated litigation. Treating every malpractice suit that happens to involve a patent, copyright, trademark, or securities matter as one arising exclusively under federal law would sweep far beyond anything Congress contemplated when it entrusted the "bulk of malpractice litigation," however factually complex, to the states—precisely the sort of disturbance of the "congressionally approved balance of federal and state judicial responsibilities" that *Grable* forbids. 545 U.S. at 314.

Nor does the interest in patent-law uniformity transform this case into one warranting exclusive federal jurisdiction. The uniformity concern reflected in this Court's patent jurisprudence, including its construction of the on-sale bar in *Pfaff v. Wells Electronics, Inc.*, 525 U.S. 55 (1998), concerns the consistent application of patent law to live patent rights—not the resolution, for the limited purpose of calculating malpractice damages, of a hypothetical defense in patent litigation that has already ended in a final and unappealable judgment of invalidity. A state court's answer to that hypothetical question binds no one, creates no patent right, and destroys none; it therefore poses no threat to the uniformity interests that animate the Federal Circuit's exclusive appellate jurisdiction over actual patent judgments.

Because the patent issue embedded in Minton's malpractice claim is not "substantial" as *Grable* requires, and because treating it as a basis for exclusive federal jurisdiction would disturb rather than respect the balance of state and federal judicial authority that Congress struck in §§ 1331 and 1338(a), this Court should hold that the Texas courts possessed, and properly exercised, jurisdiction over Minton's malpractice suit, and should reverse the judgment of the Supreme Court of Texas.
