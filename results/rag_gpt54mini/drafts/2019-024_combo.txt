Intel’s position rewrites ERISA’s text. Section 1113(2) bars suit only after “the earliest date on which the plaintiff had actual knowledge of the breach or violation,” 29 U.S.C. § 1113(2), and “actual knowledge” is not satisfied by mere disclosure of facts to a plan participant who never read them or cannot recall reading them. This Court has long distinguished actual knowledge from constructive notice. See Curtis v. Connly, 257 U.S. 260, 266 (1921); United States v. Wurts, 303 U.S. 414, 418 (1938); United States v. A. S. Kreider Co., 313 U.S. 443, 446 (1941); Unexcelled Chemical Corp. v. United States, 345 U.S. 59, 66-67 (1953); Jaben v. United States, 381 U.S. 214, 221-22 (1965).

The statute’s plain language controls. Congress did not say “disclosed to,” “available to,” or “reasonably ascertainable by” the plaintiff. It said “actual knowledge.” When Congress uses a knowledge-based trigger, courts do not expand it to cover what a party could have learned but did not. In Curtis, this Court held that a limitations period is tolled only where fraud “conceal[s] from him the existence of the cause of such action,” 257 U.S. at 266, showing that limitations turn on awareness, not mere exposure to information. Likewise, in American Surety Co. v. Pauly, 170 U.S. 160 (1898), the Court treated discovery of the wrongful acts as the operative event. And in Exploration Co. v. United States, 247 U.S. 435, 449-50 (1918), the Court rejected a limitations defense where frauds were “concealed until after six years had elapsed,” emphasizing actual discovery, not theoretical availability.

Intel’s rule would collapse “actual knowledge” into constructive notice. That is precisely what Congress rejected. The pre-1987 ERISA scheme included a constructive-knowledge trigger based on reports from which a plaintiff “could reasonably be expected to have obtained knowledge.” 29 U.S.C. § 1113(a)(2)(B) (1976). Congress later deleted that language, leaving only “actual knowledge.” See Omnibus Budget Reconciliation Act of 1987, Pub. L. No. 100-203, § 9342(b), 101 Stat. 1330. Whatever latitude courts may have had before the amendment, they may not restore a constructive-notice standard under another name. See United States v. Goldman, 277 U.S. 229, 237 (1928); Unexcelled Chemical Corp., 345 U.S. at 67.

Intel relies on disclosure of investment documents. But disclosure is not knowledge. In Wurts, the Court explained that the Government’s right to sue did not depend on formal notice alone but on the wrongful payment itself; the key point was that a limitations rule must be applied according to its terms, not enlarged by implication. 303 U.S. at 418. In Jaben, 381 U.S. at 220-22, the Court treated the statutory trigger as the institution of a proper complaint, again refusing to add extra-textual requirements. The same approach applies here: Congress required actual knowledge by the plaintiff, not merely the existence of documents in an employer’s file system.

Intel’s argument also conflicts with the structure of ERISA fiduciary litigation. A fiduciary breach may be subtle, complex, and undiscoverable absent actual awareness of the challenged conduct. That is why this Court has repeatedly focused on when the claimant knew of the operative facts. See Baker v. Cummings, 181 U.S. 117, 121 (1901); Curtis, 257 U.S. at 266; American Surety, 170 U.S. at 165-66. If Intel were correct, fiduciaries could defeat claims simply by posting dense materials online, even if participants never read them. Nothing in § 1113(2) authorizes that result.

Nor does the fact that Sulyma “could have” read the disclosures suffice. This Court has never equated mere opportunity with knowledge. To the contrary, the cases recognizing constructive or imputed knowledge did so only where the statute expressly allowed it. See Unexcelled Chemical Corp., 345 U.S. at 65-67. Congress eliminated that mode of triggering limitations in ERISA. Intel cannot resurrect it through judicial interpretation.

The Ninth Circuit was therefore correct to hold that a plaintiff must actually know the facts constituting the breach, not merely have information disclosed to him. See Curley v. City of North Las Vegas, 772 F.3d 629, 631 (9th Cir. 2014) (summary-judgment standard), and its analysis accords with ERISA’s text and this Court’s precedents. Because Intel showed only that Sulyma had access to disclosures—not that he actually knew of the challenged investments and their alleged imprudence before October 29, 2012—the action is not time-barred under 29 U.S.C. § 1113(2).
