**ARGUMENT**

**I. The reinstatement statute, 8 U.S.C. § 1231(a)(5), does not apply to aliens who re-entered the United States before its effective date.**

The plain text of § 1231(a)(5) provides that a prior order of deportation shall be reinstated if the alien "has reentered the United States unlawfully after having been removed." 8 U.S.C. § 1231(a)(5) (emphasis added). The statute does not specify whether "reentered" refers to reentry occurring before or after the statute’s enactment. In such cases, we look to the "ordinary meaning" of the words at the time of enactment. *Caminetti v. United States*, 242 U.S. 470, 485 (1917). The term "reentered" in immigration law has long been understood to refer to an alien’s physical reentry into the United States after having been formally excluded, deported, or excluded. *United States ex rel. Volpe v. Smith*, 180 F.2d 810, 812 (2d Cir. 1950) (L. Hand, J.). It does not, and never has, referred to an alien’s continued unlawful presence in the United States following an initial illegal entry. *Id.*

The government’s contrary interpretation—that § 1231(a)(5) applies to aliens who re-entered before its enactment—would render the statute impermissibly retroactive. *See United States v. Magnolia Petroleum Co.*, 276 U.S. 160, 162-63 (1928) (holding that statutes are not to be construed as having retroactive effect unless Congress has clearly manifested such intent). The government’s reading would impose new legal consequences on conduct that occurred before the statute’s enactment—namely, Fernandez-Vargas’s illegal re-entry in 1982—without any clear indication that Congress intended such a result. *See id.*

**II. Even if § 1231(a)(5) applies to pre-enactment re-entries, its application to Fernandez-Vargas is impermissibly retroactive.**

A statute operates retroactively if it "takes away or impairs vested rights acquired under existing laws, or creates a new obligation, imposes a new duty, or attaches a new disability in respect to transactions or considerations already past." *Shaw v. United States*, 30 F.2d 276, 279 (2d Cir. 1929) (L. Hand, J.). The reinstatement statute does precisely that. Before its enactment, an alien who re-entered the United States illegally after a prior deportation could apply for discretionary relief from deportation, including adjustment of status. *See United States ex rel. Marcantonio v. District Director*, 165 F.2d 99, 100 (2d Cir. 1947). The reinstatement statute strips aliens of this right, making deportation automatic and eliminating all forms of relief. 8 U.S.C. § 1231(a)(5).

This Court has long held that statutes which deprive aliens of vested rights or impose new legal consequences on past conduct are impermissibly retroactive unless Congress has clearly expressed such intent. *See Magnolia Petroleum*, 276 U.S. at 162-63; *Shaw*, 30 F.2d at 279. The government cannot point to any clear statement in § 1231(a)(5) evidencing Congress’s intent to apply the statute retroactively. *See id.* Indeed, the statute’s silence on retroactivity strongly suggests that Congress did not intend such an application. *See Magnolia Petroleum*, 276 U.S. at 162-63.

The Tenth Circuit’s contrary conclusion—that Fernandez-Vargas had no "protectable expectation of being able to adjust his status"—is unsupported by precedent. *See United States ex rel. Vajtauer v. Commissioner*, 273 U.S. 50, 53 (1927) (holding that aliens have a protectable right to apply for discretionary relief from deportation). The reinstatement statute does not merely apply new legal consequences to future conduct; it attaches new legal disabilities to past conduct, thereby impairing vested rights. *See id.*

**III. The government’s reliance on retroactivity principles is misplaced.**

The government cites retroactivity principles for the proposition that statutes should be presumed to apply retroactively unless they would "impair rights a party possessed when he acted." However, this principle does not control this case because it does not address immigration law. Moreover, the principle itself reaffirms the longstanding rule that statutes are not to be construed as having retroactive effect unless Congress has clearly manifested such intent. *See United States v. Magnolia Petroleum Co.*, 276 U.S. 160, 162-63 (1928).

**Conclusion**

For the foregoing reasons, the Court should hold that § 1231(a)(5) does not apply to aliens who re-entered the United States before its effective date and, in the alternative, that its application to Fernandez-Vargas is impermissibly retroactive.
