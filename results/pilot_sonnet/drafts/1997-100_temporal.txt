# ARGUMENT

**THE DOUBLE JEOPARDY CLAUSE BARS RETRIAL OF THE PRIOR-CONVICTION ALLEGATION**

The Double Jeopardy Clause "protects against a second prosecution for the same offense after acquittal ... [a]nd it protects against multiple punishments for the same offense." *North Carolina v. Pearce*, 395 U.S. 711, 717 (1969). That guarantee, made binding on the States because it is "fundamental to the American scheme of justice," *Benton v. Maryland*, 395 U.S. 784, 796 (1969), forecloses California from giving the prosecution a second opportunity to prove the very allegation a court has already found unproven.

**A. The Court of Appeal's Reversal for Insufficient Evidence Is Functionally an Acquittal, and the State May Not Retry What It Failed to Prove**

This Court held long ago that the Constitution "was designed as much to prevent the criminal from being twice punished for the same offence as from being twice tried for it." *Ex parte Lange*, 85 U.S. (18 Wall.) 163, 173 (1874). Once a tribunal determines, as the Court of Appeal did here, that the evidence was legally insufficient to establish the enhancement allegation beyond a reasonable doubt, the proceeding has resulted in the functional equivalent of an acquittal on that allegation. To permit the State a second attempt to marshal proof it lacked the first time is precisely the evil this Court condemned in *Green v. United States*, 355 U.S. 184 (1957). There the Court explained that "the State with all its resources and power should not be allowed to make repeated attempts to convict an individual for an alleged offense, thereby subjecting him to embarrassment, expense and ordeal and compelling him to live in a continuing state of anxiety and insecurity, as well as enhancing the possibility that even though innocent he may be found guilty." *Id.* at 187-88. Once Monge's jeopardy on the truth of the prior-conviction allegation "came to an end" through the appellate finding of insufficiency, the State could not be given a second chance to prove what it failed to prove the first time. *Id.* at 191. The same rule that bars the government from retrying a defendant on a charge it failed to establish at trial bars the government from retrying an enhancement allegation it failed to establish at a proceeding functioning as a trial.

**B. California's Prior-Conviction Proceeding Bears Every Hallmark of a Criminal Trial and Therefore Falls Within the Protection of the Double Jeopardy Clause**

California did not merely make an informal sentencing determination of the kind this Court sanctioned in *Williams v. New York*, 337 U.S. 241 (1949), where the sentencing judge could rely on out-of-court information without affording "confrontation, cross-examination, and representation by counsel." *Id.* at 245-46. Instead, California constructed a formal adversarial proceeding indistinguishable in substance from a trial on guilt. The State was required to prove the prior conviction allegation beyond a reasonable doubt; Monge retained the privilege against self-incrimination; the ordinary rules of evidence applied; and, as this Court itself has recognized a defendant is entitled in an analogous sentencing-type hearing, Monge possessed the right "to be confronted with witnesses against him [and] to cross-examine" those witnesses. *Specht v. Patterson*, 386 U.S. 605, 610 (1967).

Because California voluntarily clothed the prior-conviction proceeding with the full panoply of trial-type procedural protections, the proceeding possesses the "hallmarks of the trial on guilt or innocence" that trigger double jeopardy protection. Having chosen to prove the enhancement through an adversarial, evidence-based proceeding governed by the reasonable-doubt standard, California cannot now disclaim the constitutional consequences that attach to such a proceeding. A State may not simultaneously afford a defendant the procedural apparatus of a trial and deny him the substantive protection against being forced to endure that trial twice.

**C. Denying Double Jeopardy Protection Would Permit the State an Unfair Second Opportunity Never Sanctioned by This Court's Precedent**

Nothing in *Stroud v. United States*, 251 U.S. 15 (1919), supports a contrary result. *Stroud* addressed only the permissibility of an increased sentence following retrial of the underlying substantive offense after a defendant's own successful appeal; it did not sanction repeated attempts by the State to prove a discrete factual allegation it had already failed to establish. Here, by contrast, Monge did not seek retrial of his substantive convictions — those stand affirmed. He seeks only to prevent the State from getting a second opportunity to prove an enhancement allegation as to which the courts below already found the evidence wanting. Allowing such a second opportunity subjects Monge to precisely the "embarrassment, expense and ordeal" and "continuing state of anxiety and insecurity" that *Green* forbids. 355 U.S. at 187-88.

**CONCLUSION**

For these reasons, the judgment of the California Supreme Court should be reversed insofar as it permits retrial of the prior-conviction allegation.
