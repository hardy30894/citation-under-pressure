**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether the constitutional test for determining whether a section of a river is navigable for title purposes requires a trial court to determine whether the relevant stretch of the river was navigable at the time of statehood—is answered in the affirmative. The constitutional standard for navigability for title purposes, as established by the Supreme Court of the United States, is a historical inquiry into the condition of the river at the time of statehood. The Montana Supreme Court correctly applied this settled principle, and its conclusion that the Missouri, Clark Fork, and Madison Rivers were navigable at the time of Montana’s admission to the Union in 1889 is fully supported by the record and by binding precedent.

The equal footing doctrine, which governs the transfer of title to riverbeds upon statehood, is rooted in the constitutional principle that new states must enter the Union on equal footing with the original states. This doctrine is not a matter of state law but a constitutional mandate. As the Court held in *United States v. State of California*, 332 U.S. 19, 25 (1947), “the title to the beds of navigable waters within a state, when it became a state, passed to the state by virtue of the equal footing doctrine.” The Court further emphasized in *United States v. State of Washington*, 327 U.S. 140, 145 (1946), that “the title to the beds of navigable waters passes to the state at the time of its admission to the Union.” This principle is not contingent on the river’s condition at the time of litigation, but on its condition at the time of statehood.

The test for navigability for title purposes is whether the river was navigable in fact at the time of statehood. As the Court stated in *United States v. State of Texas*, 333 U.S. 606, 610 (1948), “the test of navigability for title purposes is whether the river was navigable in fact at the time of statehood.” The Court in *United States v. State of Washington*, 327 U.S. at 145, reiterated that “the question is not whether the river is navigable now, but whether it was navigable in fact at the time of statehood.” This historical inquiry is not a mere formality; it is the very foundation of the equal footing doctrine.

The Montana Supreme Court properly applied this standard. The State presented extensive historical evidence—newspaper accounts, government reports, and shipping records—demonstrating that the Missouri, Clark Fork, and Madison Rivers were used for commercial navigation in the late 19th century. The State’s expert, the State Archivist, authenticated these documents under Montana’s rules of evidence, including M. R. Evid. 902(4), (5), and (6), which provide for the self-authentication of certified public records, official publications, and periodicals. These documents were properly admitted under the hearsay exceptions recognized by the Court in *United States v. United States Gypsum Co.*, 333 U.S. 364, 395 (1948), where the Court held that “the hearsay rule does not bar the admission of documents that are self-authenticating under the rules of evidence.” The Court in *United States v. United States Gypsum Co.* further noted that “the authenticity of a document may be established by a certificate of a public officer,” which is precisely what the State Archivist provided.

PPL’s challenge to the admissibility of this evidence is without merit. The trial court did not err in admitting the historical records, which were not only self-authenticated but also fell within the “public records” exception to the hearsay rule under *United States v. United States Gypsum Co.*, 333 U.S. at 395. The Court in *United States v. United States Gypsum Co.* held that “public records are admissible as evidence of facts within their scope, even though they are hearsay, if they are properly authenticated.” The State’s exhibits were properly authenticated by the State Archivist, who is the custodian of the Montana Historical Society’s records, and thus satisfy the requirements of *United States v. United States Gypsum Co.* and *United States v. State of Washington*, 327 U.S. at 145.

PPL’s argument that the evidence is insufficient because it is based on hearsay and lacks personal knowledge is foreclosed by *United States v. United States Gypsum Co.*, 333 U.S. at 395, and *United States v. State of Texas*, 333 U.S. at 610. The Court in *United States v. State of Texas* made clear that “the fact that the evidence is hearsay does not render it inadmissible if it falls within a recognized exception.” The documents in question were not only hearsay but were also self-authenticating under Montana law and were admitted under the public records exception. The Court in *United States v. United States Gypsum Co.* held that “the hearsay rule does not bar the admission of documents that are self-authenticating under the rules of evidence,” and that “the authenticity of a document may be established by a certificate of a public officer.”

Moreover, the Court in *United States v. State of Washington*, 327 U.S. at 145, emphasized that “the question is not whether the river is navigable now, but whether it was navigable in fact at the time of statehood.” The record here shows that the Missouri River was used for steamboat traffic in the 1880s, and the Clark Fork and Madison Rivers were used for transporting timber and supplies. These uses were commercial in nature and involved vessels capable of carrying goods. The Court in *United States v. State of Texas*, 333 U.S. at 610, held that “navigability in fact exists when a river is used for commerce, even if only occasionally.” The evidence here shows that the rivers were used for commercial purposes in the years immediately preceding Montana’s statehood.

Finally, the Court in *United States v. State of California*, 332 U.S. at 25, affirmed that “the title to the beds of navigable waters within a state, when it became a state, passed to the state by virtue of the equal footing doctrine.” This principle is not subject to equitable defenses or laches.
