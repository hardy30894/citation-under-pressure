The Supreme Court has established that a district court may, in its discretion, enhance a lodestar attorney's fee award in "rare" and "exceptional" cases where there is specific evidence to support an exceptional result and superior performance. Blum v. Stenson, 465 U.S. 886, 899 (1984); Pennsylvania v. Delaware Valley Citizens' Council for Clean Air ("Delaware Valley I"), 478 U.S. 546, 565 (1986). The Eleventh Circuit has correctly interpreted this precedent to allow for enhancements based on the quality of representation and results obtained. Kenny A. v. Perdue, 532 F.3d 1209, 1242 (11th Cir. 2008).

The petitioners argue that the district court's enhancement was improper because it was based on the judge's personal observations and experience rather than specific evidence in the record. However, the Supreme Court has not prohibited district courts from considering their own experience and observations when evaluating the quality of representation. In fact, the district judge's familiarity with the case and the attorneys' performance is a unique asset that allows for a more informed assessment of the quality of representation. See Blum, 465 U.S. at 899 (noting that an enhancement may be justified where the fee applicant offers "specific evidence" to demonstrate that the quality of service rendered was superior).

Moreover, there is no circuit split on this issue. The majority of circuits have held that a district court may enhance a lodestar fee award based on exceptional performance and results. See, e.g., Geier v. Sundquist, 372 F.3d 784, 794-95 (6th Cir. 2004); Van Gerwen v. Guarantee Mut. Life Co., 214 F.3d 1041, 1046 (9th Cir. 2000); Quaratino v. Tiffany & Co., 166 F.3d 422, 425 (2d Cir. 1999). The Eleventh Circuit's decision is consistent with these holdings.

The petitioners' argument that the district court's enhancement was a due process violation because it was based on the judge's "testimony" is unpersuasive. The district court's observations and findings are not "testimony" but rather explanations for the enhancement, as required by Blum and Delaware Valley I. The district court's detailed findings and explanations for the enhancement demonstrate that it was based on a thorough evaluation of the case and the attorneys' performance.

In conclusion, the district court's enhancement of the lodestar fee award was within its discretion and supported by specific evidence and detailed findings. The Eleventh Circuit correctly affirmed the district court's decision, and this Court should uphold the circuit's holding. The Supreme Court's precedent in Blum and Delaware Valley I permits enhancements based on exceptional performance and results, and the Eleventh Circuit's decision is consistent with the majority of circuits that have considered this issue.
