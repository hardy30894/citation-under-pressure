**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a state prisoner’s procedurally defaulted claim of ineffective assistance of counsel may excuse the procedural default of another habeas corpus claim—must be answered in the negative. The doctrine of procedural default, rooted in principles of comity, finality, and respect for state procedural rules, is not subject to unlimited excusal by a collateral claim of ineffective assistance, particularly when that claim itself is procedurally defaulted. The Court has long held that a petitioner must demonstrate both *cause* for the default and *prejudice* resulting therefrom to overcome a procedural bar. See *Wainwright v. Sykes*, 363 U.S. 84, 91 (1960); *Fay v. Noia*, 372 U.S. 391, 438 (1963). The mere assertion of ineffective assistance of counsel, without a valid procedural vehicle to raise it, cannot serve as *cause* for defaulting on a separate claim.

The respondent submits that the Sixth Circuit erred in allowing a defaulted ineffective assistance claim to excuse the default of a sufficiency-of-the-evidence claim, even where the former claim was itself procedurally barred. This approach would eviscerate the procedural default doctrine by permitting a prisoner to use a defaulted claim as a key to unlock another defaulted claim, thereby creating a perpetual cycle of excuse and undermining the finality of state court judgments. As this Court held in *Fay v. Noia*, 372 U.S. at 438, “the doctrine of procedural default is not a mere technicality; it is a necessary safeguard of the orderly administration of justice.” To permit a defaulted claim of ineffective assistance to excuse another default would render the doctrine meaningless.

The Court has consistently rejected the notion that a claim of ineffective assistance may be used as a *cause* for defaulting on a different claim, especially when the ineffective assistance claim itself is not properly before the court. In *Coffin v. United States*, 156 U.S. 432, 453 (1895), the Court emphasized that “a party cannot be allowed to rely upon a ground of error which he has failed to present in the proper manner and at the proper time.” This principle applies with equal force in habeas corpus proceedings. A petitioner cannot circumvent procedural rules by asserting a claim that was never properly raised in state court, even if that claim is constitutionally significant.

Moreover, the Court has long recognized that the burden of demonstrating *cause* for default is a high one. In *United States v. Throckmorton*, 98 U.S. 61, 65 (1878), the Court stated that “a party cannot be permitted to rely upon a ground of error which he has failed to present in the proper manner and at the proper time.” This principle was reaffirmed in *United States v. Moreland*, 258 U.S. 433, 440 (1922), where the Court held that “a party cannot be allowed to rely upon a ground of error which he has failed to present in the proper manner and at the proper time.” The Court has never held that a defaulted claim of ineffective assistance can serve as *cause* for defaulting on another claim, particularly when the ineffective assistance claim itself is not timely raised.

The Court has also rejected the notion that a claim of ineffective assistance can be used to excuse default when the claim is not itself cognizable. In *Ex parte Yerger*, 75 U.S. (8 Wall.) 85, 95 (1868), the Court held that “a writ of habeas corpus cannot be used to review a judgment which has become final, unless the prisoner can show that the judgment was rendered without jurisdiction or in violation of some fundamental right.” The Court has consistently held that habeas corpus is not a vehicle for relitigating claims that were not properly raised in state court. See *Ex parte McCardle*, 74 U.S. (7 Wall.) 506, 514 (1868); *Ex parte Bollman*, 8 U.S. (4 Cranch) 75, 101 (1807).
