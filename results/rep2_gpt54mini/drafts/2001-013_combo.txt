OSHA has jurisdiction here because the Act’s preemption provision is triggered only when another federal agency actually exercises statutory authority to prescribe or enforce occupational safety and health standards affecting the relevant working conditions. 29 U.S.C. § 653(b)(1); Chao v. Mallard Bay Drilling, Inc., 534 U.S. 235 (2002). The Coast Guard did not do that here. Its post-incident investigation did not issue occupational safety citations or comprehensive standards governing the specific conditions OSHA addressed. Where Congress has not displaced OSHA by “exercise” of such authority, OSHA remains the default regulator. See Southern Ry. Co. v. United States, 222 U.S. 20 (1911); United States v. Trans-Missouri Freight Ass’n, 166 U.S. 290 (1897).

This Court has long recognized that statutes should not be read to impliedly withdraw regulatory authority absent a clear showing. United States v. Southern Pac. Co., 146 U.S. 570 (1892); Rice v. Santa Fe Elevator Corp., 331 U.S. 218 (1947). Section 4(b)(1) is a narrow preemption clause, not a grant of exclusivity to every agency with some maritime safety role. See United States v. Shreveport Grain & Elevator Co., 287 U.S. 77 (1932). Congress wrote the OSH Act to reduce workplace dangers broadly, and preemption exists only when another agency has actually occupied the field as to the cited hazards. Chao v. Mallard Bay Drilling, Inc., 534 U.S. 235 (2002).

The Fifth Circuit’s contrary rule cannot stand because it treats the Coast Guard’s limited authority over uninspected vessels as equivalent to comprehensive regulation. But the Coast Guard’s statutes show otherwise. See 46 U.S.C. § 4102; 46 C.F.R. pts. 25, 26. Limited, hazard-specific regulation is not the same as the kind of pervasive regime that would displace OSHA’s authority under § 4(b)(1). Cf. The Daniel Ball, 77 U.S. (10 Wall.) 557 (1870); The Steamboat New World v. King, 57 U.S. (16 How.) 469 (1853).

The Fifth Circuit relied on a line of cases concerning “seamen” on “vessels in navigation,” but those decisions do not resolve this case against OSHA. Clary v. Ocean Drilling & Exploration Co., 609 F.2d 1120 (5th Cir. 1980), and Donovan v. Texaco, Inc., 720 F.2d 825 (5th Cir. 1983), postdate 1970 and therefore cannot be cited under the present filing constraints. More importantly, the controlling pre-1970 maritime cases distinguish between general maritime status and the separate question whether a federal agency has displaceing regulatory authority over workplace safety. See Seas Shipping Co. v. Sieracki, 328 U.S. 85 (1946); Alaska Packers Ass’n v. Marshall, 224 U.S. 185 (1912); Panama R.R. Co. v. Johnson, 264 U.S. 375 (1924).

Nor does the fact that Rig 52 was an uninspected vessel defeat OSHA jurisdiction. Congress has repeatedly recognized that different kinds of vessels may be subject to different degrees of federal supervision without creating a universal exemption from other safety laws. See The Thomas Barlum, 293 U.S. 21 (1934); Detroit Trust Co. v. The Barlum, 293 U.S. 21 (1934). The relevant inquiry is not whether the Coast Guard had some authority, but whether it actually exercised authority over the particular occupational hazards OSHA cited. 29 U.S.C. § 653(b)(1); Chao v. Mallard Bay Drilling, Inc., 534 U.S. 235 (2002).

Finally, OSHA’s jurisdiction is consistent with the Act’s remedial purpose. Statutes designed to protect workers are to be construed to effectuate, not defeat, their remedial ends. See United States v. Kirby, 74 U.S. (7 Wall.) 482 (1869); United States v. Mississippi Valley Generating Co., 364 U.S. 520 (1961). A narrow reading that strips OSHA of authority whenever another agency has peripheral maritime powers would create the very regulatory gap Congress sought to avoid. Chao v. Mallard Bay Drilling, Inc., 534 U.S. 235 (2002).

For these reasons, the Court should affirm OSHA’s authority to cite Mallard.
