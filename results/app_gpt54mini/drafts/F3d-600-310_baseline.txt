**ARGUMENT**

The bankruptcy court had no jurisdiction to adjudicate post-petition interest and collection costs after Kirkland’s Chapter 13 case was closed and discharge entered. Federal bankruptcy jurisdiction extends to cases “arising under” title 11, “arising in” title 11 cases, and proceedings “related to” such cases. 28 U.S.C. §§ 1334(b), 157(a); *Celotex Corp. v. Edwards*, 514 U.S. 300, 307-08 (1995). But that grant is not boundless. Once a bankruptcy estate has been fully administered and the case closed, the bankruptcy court’s authority is at its narrowest, and a court may not use ancillary jurisdiction to decide a new post-closure dispute about the amount now owed on a nondischargeable student loan. See *In re A.H. Robins Co., Inc.*, 880 F.2d 694, 700 (4th Cir. 1989) (bankruptcy jurisdiction requires a sufficient nexus to the estate); *New Horizon of N.Y. LLC v. Jacobs*, 231 F.3d 143, 151-52 (4th Cir. 2000) (jurisdiction exists only where a matter could conceivably affect the estate).

Here, the loan principal was not paid during the Chapter 13 case, and the case was closed before ECMC sought any determination of post-petition interest or costs. That timing matters. A bankruptcy court may determine whether a debt is dischargeable, but it does not obtain perpetual jurisdiction to liquidate every component of a creditor’s post-closure collection rights. See *Travelers Indem. Co. v. Bailey*, 557 U.S. 137, 151 n.4 (2009) (bankruptcy jurisdiction is tied to matters that fall within the statutory grant); *Celotex Corp.*, 514 U.S. at 308. Kirkland’s complaint expressly acknowledged that post-petition interest would remain nondischargeable. Once the bankruptcy court determined that the principal had not been paid in full and therefore was not discharged, nothing in title 11 required it to go further and calculate an amount of interest or decide collection costs that had never been placed at issue in the bankruptcy plan, proof of claim process, or dischargeability litigation.

Nor could the bankruptcy court rely on “core” jurisdiction under 28 U.S.C. § 157(b)(2)(A). A matter is not core merely because it arises in the aftermath of a bankruptcy case. Core jurisdiction covers proceedings that arise under title 11 or arise in a case under title 11; it does not create authority over every post-confirmation or post-closure dispute between a debtor and a creditor. *Stern v. Marshall*, 564 U.S. 462, 473-74 (2011); *In re Resorts Int’l, Inc.*, 372 F.3d 154, 161-67 (3d Cir. 2004). The question whether ECMC was entitled to an unstated amount of post-petition interest and collection costs on a surviving student-loan obligation is, at most, a state-law or contract-law collection issue. Such a dispute is not the kind of bankruptcy administration question that affects the estate’s adjustment or distribution. See *Valley Historic Ltd. P’ship v. Bank of N.Y.*, 486 F.3d 831, 836-38 (4th Cir. 2007) (post-confirmation jurisdiction requires a close nexus to plan administration); *Binder v. Price Waterhouse & Co. (In re Resorts Int’l, Inc.)*, 372 F.3d at 165-67.

The bankruptcy court’s reliance on § 1334(a) and § 157(a) was therefore misplaced. A court cannot bootstrap jurisdiction from the fact that a debtor once filed bankruptcy. Once discharge entered, the estate was closed, and no administration issue remained concerning claim no. 9. The only live dispute was the amount ECMC could pursue outside bankruptcy on a debt that was nondischargeable absent undue hardship. See 11 U.S.C. § 523(a)(8); *Tennessee Student Assistance Corp. v. Hood*, 541 U.S. 440, 447-48 (2004). That question belongs, if anywhere, in a nonbankruptcy forum unless a proper bankruptcy issue is actually pending.

The court also exceeded the scope of the adversary complaint. Kirkland sought a declaration that principal had been paid and discharged except for “any interest” that accrued during bankruptcy. ECMC did not ask the bankruptcy court to determine the amount of post-petition interest or collection costs, and the court’s sua sponte award of $184.40 in interest and denial of costs was not necessary to resolve dischargeability. Federal courts do not issue advisory opinions or decide issues not properly before them. See *Flast v. Cohen*, 392 U.S. 83, 96 (1968); *Steel Co. v. Citizens for a Better Env’t*, 523 U.S. 83, 94-95 (1998). The bankruptcy court was obliged to decide only whether the student-loan principal was discharged; having found it was not, it lacked a jurisdictional basis to adjudicate additional post-closure collection components.

Finally, the bankruptcy court’s observation that the trustee “cannot unilaterally reduce the amount of an allowed claim,” J.A. 86, underscores why the court’s post-closure monetary award was improper. The allowed claim process was already complete, and no party sought claim modification under any Bankruptcy Code provision authorizing such relief after case closure. See *United Student Aid Funds, Inc. v. Espinosa*, 559 U.S. 260, 269-70 (2010) (bankruptcy court must act within the Code’s statutory framework). Because no pending bankruptcy administration issue remained, and because post-petition interest and collection costs were never properly placed before the court, the award should be vacated for lack of jurisdiction.
