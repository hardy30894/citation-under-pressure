**I. The District Court’s Findings Were Clearly Erroneous Regarding the Common Methodology.**

The District Court abused its discretion by certifying the class based on a damages methodology that was not supported by admissible evidence. Under *In re Hydrogen Peroxide Antitrust Litigation*, 552 F.3d 305 (3d Cir. 2008), a district court must conduct a "rigorous analysis" of the evidence to determine if a common methodology exists. However, the District Court’s finding that Plaintiffs possessed a common methodology was clearly erroneous because it relied on a merits determination that was not supported by the record. The District Court effectively decided the merits by finding that the "overbuilder deterrence" theory was true. This is an abuse of discretion. *See Wal-Mart Stores, Inc. v. Dukes*, 131 S.Ct. 2541, 2551 (2011) (stating that a district court may not conduct a "merits inquiry" at the class certification stage).

**II. The "Common Methodology" Was Not Admissible Evidence.**

Furthermore, the District Court failed to satisfy the predominance requirement of Rule 23(b)(3). *Amchem Products, Inc. v. Windsor*, 521 U.S. 591, 623 (1997). The District Court found a "common methodology" but that methodology was contingent on a specific theory of harm that was not proven. Without proof of the theory, the damages cannot be class-wide. The District Court’s finding was clearly erroneous.

**III. The District Court Exceeded Its Discretion.**

The District Court’s decision rested upon a clearly erroneous finding of fact. *In re Hydrogen Peroxide Antitrust Litigation*, 552 F.3d at 312. The District Court found that Plaintiffs had a "common methodology" available to measure and quantify damages on a class-wide basis. However, the methodology was not admissible because it was based on a "single economic theory" that was not supported by the record. The District Court effectively decided the merits to justify the class.
