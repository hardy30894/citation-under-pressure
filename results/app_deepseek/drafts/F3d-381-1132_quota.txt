**ARGUMENT**

The Court of Federal Claims erred by concluding that the scope of the government’s easement is irrelevant to whether a physical taking occurred. Because the government’s installation of fiber-optic cables with excess capacity, combined with its leasing of that capacity to third parties, exceeded the easement’s plain terms, the government effected a permanent physical occupation of Tuthill’s property that constitutes a *per se* taking under the Fifth Amendment.

**I. The Scope of the Easement Defines the Property Rights Retained by Tuthill**

A property owner retains all rights not expressly granted by an easement. *Ladd v. United States*, 630 F.3d 1015, 1023 (Fed. Cir. 2010). “[T]he scope of the easement determines the nature of the property interest retained by the landowner.” *Boise Cascade Corp. v. United States*, 296 F.3d 1339, 1350 (Fed. Cir. 2002). Here, the 1953 easement granted only a right to “erect, operate, maintain, repair, rebuild, and patrol” power lines and “appurtenant signal lines … necessary in connection therewith.” The government’s fiber-optic cables, containing 36 or 72 fibers when only a fraction are needed for BPA’s own operations, are not “necessary” within the meaning of the easement. The lease of excess capacity to a telecommunications company is a commercial use wholly unrelated to BPA’s transmission of electrical power. Such use exceeds the easement’s scope and strips Tuthill of its right to exclude, a fundamental stick in the bundle of property rights. *Kaiser Aetna v. United States*, 444 U.S. 164, 176 (1979).

**II. A Government Action That Exceeds the Scope of an Easement May Constitute a Physical Taking**

The lower court’s holding that misuse of an easement “cannot give rise to a physical taking” misstates the law. In *Preseault v. United States*, 100 F.3d 1525, 1538 (Fed. Cir. 1996) (en banc), this court recognized that a government action that exceeds the scope of a previously granted easement “may constitute a taking for which just compensation is required.” The inquiry is whether the government’s use “amounts to a physical occupation of the plaintiff’s property.” *Hendler v. United States*, 952 F.2d 1364, 1374 (Fed. Cir. 1991). The fiber-optic cables are physically present on Tuthill’s land. Their presence is permanent, and the government’s lease to a third party is an ongoing commercial invasion. This is a classic *per se* physical taking. *Loretto v. Teleprompter Manhattan CATV Corp.*, 458 U.S. 419, 434–35 (1982) (permanent physical occupation authorized by government is a taking regardless of the public interest served).

**III. The Government’s Overbuilding and Commercial Leasing Constitute a New and Independent Taking**

The government installed more fibers than needed for its own signal-line purposes, and then leased the surplus to a for-profit telecommunications company. Even if the original installation within the easement’s scope were non-compensable, the subsequent lease of excess capacity to a third party is a “new use” that imposes an additional burden on the servient estate. *United States v. 1.58 Acres of Land*, 523 F.3d 98, 105 (1st Cir. 2008) (lease of fiber-optic space for commercial use constitutes a separate taking when not authorized by original easement). The government’s own projections—that it may need the fibers by 2025—do not justify taking now what it does not currently need. The Fifth Amendment requires compensation for present occupation, not a speculative future need. *Armstrong v. United States*, 364 U.S. 40, 48–49 (1960) (taking of property rights must be compensated when it occurs).

**IV. The Government’s Physical Occupation Is *Per Se* a Taking, and Compensation Is Due Even for a Temporary Occupation**

The installation of fiber-optic cables that are not “necessary” for the easement’s purpose is a permanent physical occupation. Under *Loretto*, 458 U.S. at 441, “a permanent physical occupation authorized by the government is a taking without regard to the public interests that it may serve.” Tuthill is entitled to just compensation for the value of the occupation. Alternatively, if the government ceases its occupation, Tuthill is entitled to compensation for the temporary taking. *Kimball Laundry Co. v. United States*, 338 U.S. 1, 3–4 (1949) (temporary takings require compensation for the period of occupation). The lower court’s reliance on the fact that the physical intrusion is no greater than the original easement ignores that the *use* of the fiber—commercial leasing—is entirely outside the easement’s scope. The property right being taken is Tuthill’s right to exclude commercial telecom operations from its land. *Kaiser Aetna*, 444 U.S. at 176.

**V. Conclusion**

The Court of Federal Claims erred by holding that the scope of the easement is irrelevant and that no physical taking occurred. The undisputed facts show that the government installed fiber-optic cables far exceeding its own needs, and leases excess capacity to third parties. This is a physical occupation beyond the easement’s terms, and it constitutes a *per se* taking under the Fifth Amendment. This court should reverse the grant of summary judgment to the government and remand for determination of just compensation.
