## Argument

### I. A plain-error court may not rely on materials outside the trial record to decide prejudice.

Plain-error review is tightly constrained. A defendant must show (1) an error, (2) that is clear or obvious, and (3) that affected substantial rights; only then may an appellate court exercise discretion to correct the error if it “seriously affect[s] the fairness, integrity or public reputation of judicial proceedings.” *United States v. Olano*, 507 U.S. 725, 732-36 (1993); *Johnson v. United States*, 520 U.S. 461, 466-67 (1997). The burden remains on the defendant throughout. *Olano*, 507 U.S. at 734-35; *Puckett v. United States*, 556 U.S. 129, 135 (2009).

That burden cannot be met by asking an appellate court to look beyond the trial record to locate evidence that the jury never heard. Appellate review exists to assess whether the trial proceedings were infected by reversible error, not to reconstruct the case from extra-record materials. *United States v. Vonn*, 535 U.S. 55, 62-63 (2002), held that a court reviewing for plain error may consult the entire record, but only to determine whether an error affected substantial rights in the context of the proceedings below. *Vonn* did not authorize reliance on materials outside the record. Likewise, *United States v. Dominguez Benitez*, 542 U.S. 74, 80-83 (2004), explained that prejudice under Rule 52(b) is assessed by asking whether the defendant has shown a “reasonable probability” of a different result based on the record of the proceeding. See also *Puckett*, 556 U.S. at 135-36.

This Court’s cases point the same way. The plain-error inquiry is not a license for appellate factfinding untethered from the trial record. In *Henderson v. United States*, 568 U.S. 266, 271-73 (2013), the Court described plain error as an error that is clear at the time of appellate review, but it did not alter the rule that prejudice must be shown from the proceedings under review. And in *Rosales-Mireles v. United States*, 585 U.S. 129, 136-39 (2018), the Court again emphasized the structured nature of Rule 52(b): the defendant must first establish an effect on substantial rights before a court considers whether to exercise discretion to correct the error.

Greer’s position would collapse those settled limits. If an appellate court could search outside the trial record for facts that were never presented to the jury, the government’s failure of proof at trial would be retroactively cured on appeal. That is incompatible with *Olano*’s allocation of burdens and with the basic appellate function of reviewing, not retrying, cases. See *Olano*, 507 U.S. at 737-38; *Dominguez Benitez*, 542 U.S. at 82-83.

### II. Under Rehaif, Greer still had to show prejudice from the trial record; his status and prior convictions do not permit an appellate court to bypass the jury’s missing finding.

In *Rehaif v. United States*, 588 U.S. 176, 181-90 (2019), the Court held that in a prosecution under 18 U.S.C. § 922(g), the government must prove the defendant knew both that he possessed a firearm and that he belonged to the relevant prohibited status category. But *Rehaif* did not alter Rule 52(b)’s demanding prejudice standard. Post-*Rehaif* defendants must still show that the instructional or evidentiary error affected the outcome of their trial. *Greer v. United States*, 593 U.S. ___, 141 S. Ct. 2090, 2096-98 (2021), makes that point explicit.

In *Greer*, the Court held that when a defendant was convicted after a trial with no *Rehaif* instruction, the defendant must make a sufficient showing on appeal that the error affected substantial rights. Id. at 2097-99. The Court approved reviewing the “whole record” to determine whether the defendant could plausibly demonstrate prejudice, including the defendant’s prior admissions and other trial evidence. Id. at 2098-99. But the Court did not approve looking outside the record to supply missing trial proof or to speculate about facts not presented to the factfinder. To the contrary, the Court explained that in most felon-in-possession cases, a defendant will have difficulty showing prejudice because “convicted felons typically know they’re convicted felons.” Id. at 2097.

That makes Greer’s extra-record theory especially untenable. His prior convictions may make prejudice hard to prove, but they do not authorize an appellate court to leave the record behind. Rule 52(b) asks whether the error likely affected the verdict actually rendered, not whether the court can identify post hoc reasons the verdict might still have been correct. *Olano*, 507 U.S. at 734; *Dominguez Benitez*, 542 U.S. at 81-82.

### III. The Eleventh Circuit correctly confined its review to the record and properly denied relief.

The Eleventh Circuit did what plain-error doctrine requires: it examined the trial record, Greer’s admissions, and his undisputed criminal history to decide whether the *Rehaif* error affected substantial rights. That approach accords with *Greer*, 141 S. Ct. at 2097-99, and with *Vonn*, 535 U.S. at 72-74. Because Greer could not show a reasonable probability that the jury would have acquitted him had it been properly instructed, he could not satisfy Rule 52(b). *Dominguez Benitez*, 542 U.S. at 83; *Puckett*, 556 U.S. at 135.

The judgment should therefore be affirmed.
