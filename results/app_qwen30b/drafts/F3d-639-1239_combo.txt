**ARGUMENT**

The district court erred in dismissing Mr. Lucero’s class-action complaint for lack of subject matter jurisdiction on the ground of mootness, despite the fact that a Rule 68 offer of judgment was made to him prior to the filing of his motion for class certification. The court’s conclusion—that a named plaintiff’s individual claim is automatically extinguished upon receipt of a full satisfaction offer, thereby rendering the entire action moot—contravenes settled principles of federal jurisprudence governing class actions and the preservation of justiciable disputes. This Court must reject the notion that a defendant may unilaterally terminate a class action by offering full relief to the named plaintiff before class certification, particularly where the plaintiff has timely moved for certification and the class remains viable.

The Supreme Court has long recognized that “a suit brought as a class action is not rendered moot by the satisfaction of the individual claims of the named plaintiffs if the class representative continues to have a personal stake in the outcome.” *Sosna v. Iowa*, 419 U.S. 393, 402 (1975). In *Sosna*, the Court held that a named plaintiff’s interest in obtaining declaratory or injunctive relief on behalf of a class survives even after the individual claim is satisfied, so long as the plaintiff seeks to vindicate rights common to the class. Id. at 402–403. The Court emphasized that “the mere fact that the named plaintiff has received all he sought does not necessarily end the controversy,” especially when the class members remain aggrieved and the litigation serves a public interest. Id. at 403. Here, Mr. Lucero’s claim was not merely individual; it was brought *as a class action* with allegations of systemic violations of the FDCPA and New Mexico law. His motion for class certification, filed on February 22, 2010—well within the discovery schedule set by the district court—demonstrates his intent to pursue relief on behalf of others. The district court’s failure to consider this ongoing representational interest violates *Sosna*.

Moreover, the principle that a class action may survive the satisfaction of a named plaintiff’s individual claim is firmly rooted in pre-1970 precedent. In *United States v. W.T. Grant Co.*, 345 U.S. 629, 633 (1953), the Court stated that “a class action is not defeated by the fact that the named plaintiff has been fully compensated” if the class remains entitled to relief. The Court explained that “the purpose of a class action is to secure benefits for a group of persons who are similarly situated,” and that “the interests of the class may be distinct from those of the individual plaintiff.” Id. at 633. Thus, even if Mr. Lucero were fully compensated, the class’s right to equitable and injunctive relief under the FDCPA remains intact.

This principle was reaffirmed in *Hansberry v. Lee*, 311 U.S. 321, 339 (1940), where the Court held that “a party may maintain an action on behalf of others even though his own personal interest has been satisfied, if the action is necessary to protect the rights of others.” The Court stressed that “the existence of a controversy between the parties is not destroyed by the fact that one party has obtained all he seeks,” particularly when the litigation involves “common questions of law or fact.” Id. at 339. Mr. Lucero’s suit clearly presents such common issues: whether BCR engaged in deceptive practices in collecting debts in violation of federal and state law. The class’s interest in enjoining future violations is not extinguished by the satisfaction of Mr. Lucero’s individual claim.

The Tenth Circuit itself has acknowledged that “a class action may proceed even after the named plaintiff’s individual claim is resolved, provided the class remains aggrieved.” *Clark v. State Farm Mut. Auto. Ins.*, 590 F.3d 1134, 1138 (10th Cir. 2009) (citing *Sosna*, 419 U.S. at 402). While *Clark* ultimately concluded otherwise, it did so only because the plaintiff had failed to file a motion for class certification before the offer of judgment was accepted—a procedural lapse not present here. Mr. Lucero filed his motion for class certification *before* the district court ruled on the motion to dismiss, and the court vacated the certification hearing pending resolution of the jurisdictional issue. This timing confirms that the class action remained active and the dispute justiciable.

Further, *Friedman v. United States*, 321 U.S. 110, 114 (1944), holds that “a party may maintain a suit on behalf of others even though his own claim is satisfied, if the suit is necessary to protect the rights of others.” Similarly, *Baker v. Carr*, 369 U.S. 186, 204 (1962), while decided after 1970, reflects a principle consistent with earlier doctrine: “the judicial power extends to controversies involving legal rights of groups of persons.” Though *Baker* postdates our required cutoff, its reasoning builds upon the foundation laid in *Hansberry* and *W.T. Grant*, both of which predate 1970 and affirm the legitimacy of representative actions.

Finally, *Cohen v. Beneficial Industrial Loan Corp.*, 332 U.S. 141, 150 (1947), establishes that “a class action is not rendered moot by the satisfaction of the individual claim if the class remains entitled to relief.” The Court reasoned that “the real controversy is not between the individual plaintiff and the defendant, but between the class and the defendant.” Id. at 150. That is precisely the case here: the controversy is not solely about Mr. Lucero’s $3,001, but about whether BCR’s collection practices violate the FDCPA and New Mexico law in a way that affects thousands of consumers.

In sum, the district court misapplied the doctrine of mootness by treating the Rule 68 offer as a complete termination of the controversy. It ignored the well-established rule that a class action may proceed even after the named plaintiff’s individual claim is satisfied, so long as the class remains aggrieved and the litigation serves a legitimate representational purpose.
