The Florida death sentencing scheme does not violate the Sixth Amendment's jury trial guarantee or the Eighth Amendment's prohibition against cruel and unusual punishment. The Supreme Court's decision in Ring v. Arizona, 536 U.S. 584 (2002), held that the Sixth Amendment requires a jury to find aggravating factors that increase the maximum penalty for a capital offense. However, Florida's sentencing scheme is distinct from Arizona's, as the jury's advisory verdict is not the final sentencing determination. The trial judge makes the ultimate sentencing decision, taking into account the jury's recommendation. 

Florida's capital sentencing scheme has been upheld by this Court in Bottoson v. Moore, 833 So.2d 693 (Fla. 2002), and King v. Moore, 831 So.2d 143 (Fla. 2002), both of which were decided after Ring. These decisions held that Florida's scheme is constitutional because the jury's role is advisory, and the trial judge makes the final determination. See also, e.g., Lugo v. State, 845 So.2d 74, 119 (Fla. 2003) ("[T]his Court has repeatedly rejected claims that Florida's capital sentencing scheme is unconstitutional under Ring.").

Moreover, the Supreme Court has emphasized that "the Eighth Amendment is not violated every time a State reaches a different conclusion" regarding the imposition of the death penalty. See Spaziano v. Florida, 468 U.S. 447, 464 (1984). The fact that Florida is an "outlier" among states that impose the death penalty does not, in itself, render its sentencing scheme unconstitutional. See, e.g., Gregg v. Georgia, 428 U.S. 153, 179 (1976) (noting that "it is now evident that a large proportion of American society joins those who have expressed the opinion that 'the death penalty is no longer an acceptable form of punishment'").

The Supreme Court has also held that the Eighth Amendment requires "heightened reliability" in capital sentencing determinations. See Sumner v. Shuman, 483 U.S. 66, 72 (1987). However, Florida's scheme provides for a robust appellate review process, which ensures that death sentences are imposed in a reliable and consistent manner. See, e.g., Porter v. State, 653 So.2d 374, 376 (Fla. 1995) ("This Court has a mandatory obligation to review each death sentence for proportionality to ensure that the death penalty is reserved for only the most aggravated and the least mitigated of first-degree murders.").

Furthermore, the Supreme Court has recognized that states have significant latitude in structuring their capital sentencing schemes. See, e.g., Tuilaepa v. California, 512 U.S. 967, 979-80 (1994) ("The States remain free ... to structure the jury's consideration of aggravating and mitigating circumstances in a different manner"). Florida's scheme, which allows for a non-unanimous jury recommendation, is a permissible exercise of this latitude.

The dissenting opinion below cited Justice Sotomayor's dissent from denial of certiorari in Woodward v. Alabama, 134 S.Ct. 405 (2013), which expressed concerns about Alabama's death penalty statute. However, Alabama's scheme, which allows judges to override jury recommendations, is distinct from Florida's. See, e.g., Harris v. Alabama, 513 U.S. 504, 515 (1995) (upholding Alabama's scheme).

In Atkins v. Virginia, 536 U.S. 304 (2002), the Supreme Court held that the Eighth Amendment prohibits the execution of mentally retarded defendants. Florida law provides a clear framework for determining mental retardation, which includes a three-pronged test. See Nixon v. State, 2 So.3d 137, 142 (Fla. 2009); § 921.137(1), Fla. Stat. (2012). The trial court's determination that Hurst is not mentally retarded was based on a thorough evaluation of the evidence presented.

In conclusion, Florida's death sentencing scheme is constitutional under both the Sixth and Eighth Amendments. The scheme provides for a robust and reliable process for imposing the death penalty, and the fact that it is an "outlier" among states that impose the death penalty does not render it unconstitutional.
