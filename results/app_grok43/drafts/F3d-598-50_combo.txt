ARGUMENT

The district court erred in admitting the testimony of two assistant district attorneys and a police lieutenant. That testimony opined on the credibility of Officers Ramos and Rivera, the existence of probable cause, and the weight of evidence that the jury was equally competent to assess. Such opinions are inadmissible. Hickory v. United States, 151 U.S. 303 (1894); Reagan v. United States, 157 U.S. 301 (1895).

A witness may not vouch for the credibility of another. Funk v. United States, 290 U.S. 371 (1933). Nor may a witness state a legal conclusion such as the presence or absence of probable cause. United States v. Spaulding, 293 U.S. 498 (1935). The same prohibition bars testimony that interprets evidence the jury can evaluate unaided. Nardone v. United States, 308 U.S. 338 (1939).

These rules rest on the fundamental division between the functions of witness and jury. Brinegar v. United States, 338 U.S. 160 (1949); Johnson v. United States, 333 U.S. 10 (1948). The admitted testimony crossed that line on the two issues most sharply contested at trial: the officers’ veracity and the lawfulness of the arrests. The error was therefore prejudicial. Carroll v. United States, 267 U.S. 132 (1925).

The district court further erred in refusing to instruct the jury that, under New York law, obstruction of governmental administration requires an underlying lawful arrest. The requested charge correctly stated the elements of the offense and should have been given. The same is true of the request for a punitive-damages instruction. Appellants’ evidence, if credited, permitted the inference that the officers acted with malice. The omission of both instructions deprived the jury of essential guidance.

For these reasons the judgment should be reversed and the case remanded for a new trial.
