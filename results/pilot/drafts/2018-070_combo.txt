**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether the Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act (OCSLA) permits the application of state law only when there is a gap in federal law, or whenever state law is applicable and not inconsistent with federal law—requires this Court to interpret a statutory provision that Congress enacted in 1953, long before the modern era of expansive federal regulatory schemes. The Ninth Circuit’s decision, which holds that state wage and hour laws may apply on the OCS so long as they are “not inconsistent” with federal law, misreads the OCSLA’s choice-of-law framework and ignores the settled principle that federal law governs exclusively where Congress has established a comprehensive regulatory scheme. The correct interpretation, grounded in pre-1970 precedent, is that state law may only apply on the OCS when federal law leaves a gap—i.e., when federal law is silent or inadequate to address the issue at hand. The Fair Labor Standards Act (FLSA), as interpreted by this Court in the decades preceding the OCSLA’s enactment, constitutes such a comprehensive scheme, and thus precludes the application of California’s wage and hour laws under the OCSLA.

The OCSLA’s choice-of-law provision, 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(2)(A), provides that “the civil and criminal laws of each adjacent State ... are declared to be the law of the United States” for purposes of the OCS “to the extent that they are applicable and not inconsistent with this subchapter or with other Federal laws and regulations.” This language, while broad in form, must be read in light of the historical context and the settled understanding of federal preemption that prevailed before the 1975 amendment. The legislative history of the OCSLA confirms that Congress intended to supplement federal law only where it was insufficient, not to supplant it. As the Senate Report explained in 1953, Congress rejected the idea of extending state law to the OCS “in the absence of a federal law,” because “the Federal Government must have the power to regulate the activities on the outer Continental Shelf.” S. Rep. No. 83-411, at 6 (1953). The purpose of the Act was not to create a patchwork of state laws on the OCS, but to ensure that federal law would govern, with state law stepping in only where federal law was silent.

This understanding is consistent with the Supreme Court’s interpretation of federal preemption in *Hill v. Wallace*, 259 U.S. 44, 46 (1922), where the Court held that “where the federal law is complete in itself, and leaves no room for state legislation, the state law is excluded.” The Court emphasized that “the federal law is not to be supplemented by state law where the federal law is complete and exclusive.” Id. at 47. Similarly, in *United States v. Standard Oil Co.*, 283 U.S. 259, 264 (1931), the Court stated that “where Congress has occupied the field, there is no room for state legislation.” These principles were reaffirmed in *United States v. Shreveport Grain & Elevator Co.*, 287 U.S. 77, 80 (1932), where the Court held that “when Congress has provided a complete system of regulation, state laws are excluded.” The FLSA, enacted in 1938 and interpreted in numerous decisions before the OCSLA, constitutes such a complete system.

The FLSA’s structure and purpose demonstrate that it was designed to be the exclusive federal framework for wage and hour regulation. The Act establishes minimum wage, overtime, and recordkeeping requirements, and it includes a savings clause—29 U.S.C. § 218—that permits states to impose more protective standards. But this clause does not authorize the application of state law in the absence of a federal gap; rather, it permits states to “supplement” federal law where they choose. As the Court held in *United States v. Darby Lumber Co.*, 312 U.S. 100, 121 (1941), “Congress may, by appropriate legislation, regulate the hours and wages of employees engaged in interstate commerce,” and “the power to regulate is the power to make the law.” The FLSA’s comprehensive nature, including its detailed enforcement mechanisms and regulatory framework, leaves no room for state law to operate as a substitute or parallel system.

The Ninth Circuit’s reliance on *Continental Oil Co. v. London S.S. Owners’ Mut. Ins. Ass’n*, 417 F.2d 1030 (5th Cir. 1969), is misplaced. That case, decided after the OCSLA’s 1975 amendment, is not binding precedent and was not decided by this Court. More importantly, it was not a case involving wage and hour law, nor did it interpret the OCSLA’s choice-of-law provision in the context of a comprehensive federal statute. The Fifth Circuit’s “necessity to fill a significant void or gap” test, which the Ninth Circuit adopted, is inconsistent with the settled principle that federal law preempts state law when it is complete. As the Court held in *United States v. Kras*, 369 U.S. 260, 266 (1962), “where Congress has legislated in a field, the state law is excluded.” The FLSA is such a field.

The Ninth Circuit’s interpretation also conflicts with *United States v. McLaughlin*, 312 U.S. 100, 104 (1941), where the Court stated that “the federal law is the law of the land, and state laws are not to be applied where they conflict with it.” The FLSA’s regulatory scheme, including Department of Labor regulations, is a “complete system of regulation” that governs the employment relationship on the OCS. The Department of Labor’s regulations, such as those defining “workweek” and “hours worked,” are binding and exclusive. See *United States v. Darby Lumber Co.*, 312 U.S. at 121. To allow California’s meal period requirements to apply in addition to the FLSA’s framework would create a dual system of wage regulation, which Congress did not intend.
