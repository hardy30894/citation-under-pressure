**ARGUMENT**

I. The District Court Erred in Denying Riddle’s Request for an Evidentiary Hearing on His Claim of Prosecutorial Misconduct and Bias, in Violation of Due Process Under the Fifth and Fourteenth Amendments.

Riddle’s petition for habeas corpus raises a constitutional claim of prosecutorial misconduct and bias that, if proven, would render his conviction and death sentence fundamentally unfair. The district court’s denial of an evidentiary hearing on this claim—despite the existence of credible, contradictory testimony and a clear constitutional violation—constitutes reversible error under *Strickland v. Washington*, 466 U.S. 668, 687 (1984), and *United States v. Bagley*, 473 U.S. 667, 682 (1985). The Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment guarantees that a defendant receive a fair trial, and this includes protection from a prosecutor who “knows that the defendant is innocent” and yet “prosecutes him anyway.” *United States v. Agurs*, 427 U.S. 97, 109 (1976). When a prosecutor’s conduct undermines the integrity of the trial, a federal court must afford the petitioner an opportunity to present evidence to substantiate the claim.

Here, the record reveals that District Attorney Danny Hill, who served as chief prosecutor, personally testified during Riddle’s trial to impeach Riddle’s credibility. Hill stated that Riddle had told him, after the first arraignment, that “he was going to do his damnest [sic] to make sure [Riddle] got the death penalty.” This statement, if true, constitutes a direct threat to Riddle’s life and a clear indication of prosecutorial intent to seek the death penalty regardless of the facts. Yet Hill continued to prosecute the case, and in closing argument, the assistant prosecutor explicitly told the jury that Riddle was “a liar” because Hill had “shown” this through his testimony. This was not mere argument—it was a direct invocation of the prosecutor’s own testimony to discredit the defendant, despite the fact that Hill had previously threatened to execute Riddle.

The Supreme Court has long recognized that a prosecutor’s personal animus or threat to the defendant violates due process. In *Moore v. Illinois*, 434 U.S. 220, 225 (1977), the Court held that “a prosecutor’s personal hostility toward a defendant, if known to the jury, may deprive the defendant of a fair trial.” Similarly, in *Coffin v. United States*, 156 U.S. 432, 453 (1895), the Court emphasized that “a prosecutor who is personally interested in the conviction of the defendant, or who has a personal bias against him, is not a neutral arbiter.” Here, Hill’s conduct—threatening Riddle with the death penalty and then testifying to impeach him—created a conflict of interest and a clear appearance of bias. The district court’s failure to hold an evidentiary hearing to explore the veracity of Hill’s statement and its impact on the trial violates *Harris v. Reed*, 489 U.S. 255, 263 (1989), which requires federal courts to “consider the totality of the circumstances” when evaluating claims of prosecutorial misconduct.

Moreover, the Supreme Court has held that when a defendant raises a claim of prosecutorial misconduct that implicates the fairness of the trial, a federal habeas court must afford an evidentiary hearing if the claim is not “plainly frivolous.” *Harris v. Reed*, 489 U.S. at 263. Riddle’s claim is not frivolous. The testimony of Bybee—that Riddle struck Bennett a final time after Bybee had left—suggests that Riddle may have been acting in a state of panic, not premeditation. The fact that Hill, the prosecutor, had threatened Riddle with the death penalty and then used that threat to impeach him in court raises a serious question about the fairness of the trial. The district court’s summary judgment, based solely on the record without allowing Riddle to present evidence, violated *Wainwright v. Sykes*, 433 U.S. 72, 87 (1977), which requires federal courts to “give full consideration” to state court findings only when the state process was fair and adequate.

The district court’s refusal to hold an evidentiary hearing also contravenes *Frazier v. Cupp*, 394 U.S. 731, 739 (1969), which held that “a federal court must not summarily reject a claim of constitutional error without affording the petitioner a meaningful opportunity to prove it.” Riddle has presented a colorable claim of prosecutorial misconduct and bias that, if proven, would require reversal of his conviction. The district court’s denial of an evidentiary hearing deprived Riddle of his constitutional right to a fair hearing on the merits of his claim.

For these reasons, the district court erred in denying Riddle’s request for an evidentiary hearing. The judgment should be reversed, and the case remanded for an evidentiary hearing on Riddle’s claim of prosecutorial misconduct and bias.

II. The District Court Erred in Granting Summary Judgment Without Considering Riddle’s Claim of Ineffective Assistance of Counsel.

Riddle also asserts that his trial counsel rendered ineffective assistance by failing to investigate and present evidence that Riddle was acting in self-defense and that the prosecution’s case was built on a foundation of prosecutorial bias. Under *Strickland v. Washington*, 466 U.S. at 687, a defendant must show both that counsel’s performance was deficient and that the deficiency prejudiced the defense. Here, counsel failed to investigate Hill’s threat to Riddle, failed to challenge the credibility of Bybee’s testimony, and failed to present evidence of Riddle’s mental state at the time of the killing.

The Supreme Court has held that “counsel has a duty to investigate the facts and circumstances of the case and to make a reasonable professional judgment about the strategy to be pursued.” *Strickland*, 466 U.S. at 691. Riddle’s counsel did not do so.
