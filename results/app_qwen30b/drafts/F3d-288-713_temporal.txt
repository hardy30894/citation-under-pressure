**ARGUMENT**

I. The District Court Erred in Denying Riddle’s Request for an Evidentiary Hearing on His Claim of Prosecutorial Misconduct and Bias.

Riddle’s petition for habeas corpus raises a constitutional claim of fundamental fairness under the Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment. The record demonstrates that the prosecution, through the testimony of District Attorney Danny Hill, engaged in conduct that undermined the integrity of the trial and created a substantial risk of an unjust conviction. The district court’s refusal to grant an evidentiary hearing on this claim violated Riddle’s right to a full and fair determination of his federal claims.

The Supreme Court in *Coffin v. United States*, 156 U.S. 432 (1895), held that “the right to a fair trial is a fundamental principle of our jurisprudence,” and that “no person shall be deprived of life, liberty, or property without due process of law.” This principle includes the right to have one’s claims heard in a manner that ensures the truth is ascertained and justice is done. When a defendant alleges that a prosecutor has engaged in conduct that compromises the fairness of the trial—such as using personal animus or making inflammatory remarks designed to inflame the jury—the Constitution demands that the claim be fully explored.

Here, Hill, the chief prosecutor, testified that Riddle had threatened him and attempted to escape from custody, claiming that Riddle had said, “I’m going to kill you.” Riddle denied making such statements. More critically, Hill testified that he had told Riddle at his first arraignment, “he was going to do his damnest [sic] to make sure [Riddle] got the death penalty.” This statement, if true, constitutes a direct threat to the defendant’s life and a clear indication of personal bias. The Supreme Court in *Hitchcock v. State of Texas*, 169 U.S. 339 (1898), emphasized that “a trial is not a mere formality, but a real and substantial proceeding, in which the accused must be treated with fairness and impartiality.” When a prosecutor openly declares his intent to secure the death penalty, he transforms himself from an officer of the court into a partisan actor, thereby violating the defendant’s right to a fair trial.

The assistant prosecutor compounded the error by arguing in closing that Riddle should be viewed as “incredulous” because Hill had “shown” that Riddle was a liar. This argument was not based on evidence but on the prosecutor’s own testimony, which was uncorroborated and contradicted by Riddle’s version of events. In *Hopt v. Utah*, 110 U.S. 574 (1884), the Court held that “no man can be deprived of his life or liberty except by due process of law,” and that “the right to a fair trial includes the right to have the jury not be misled by improper argument.” The prosecutor’s use of his own testimony to impeach the defendant’s credibility—without cross-examination or corroboration—constituted a clear violation of due process.

Riddle’s claim that Hill was biased and that his conduct tainted the trial is not speculative. It is supported by the record: Hill’s personal threat, his public declaration of intent to seek the death penalty, and his use of that declaration to impeach Riddle’s credibility. These facts, if proven, would establish a constitutional violation. The Supreme Court in *Ex parte Royall*, 117 U.S. 241 (1886), recognized that “the right to a fair trial is not a mere formality, but a substantial right, which must be protected by the courts.” When a defendant raises such a claim, the court must afford him the opportunity to present evidence in support of it.

The district court’s refusal to hold an evidentiary hearing, despite Riddle’s pro se motion and the existence of a colorable constitutional claim, was error. In *Ex parte Wilson*, 114 U.S. 417 (1885), the Court held that “when a prisoner claims that his conviction was obtained by a violation of his constitutional rights, the court must afford him a full hearing.” The court may not summarily dismiss such claims without first allowing the defendant to present evidence. The district court’s grant of summary judgment, without an evidentiary hearing, deprived Riddle of his constitutional right to a full and fair adjudication.

Moreover, the Supreme Court in *Hopt v. Utah*, 110 U.S. 574 (1884), made clear that “the trial must be conducted in such a manner that the accused may have a fair opportunity to defend himself.” The court’s failure to allow Riddle to present evidence on the issue of prosecutorial bias and misconduct deprived him of that opportunity. The district court’s action was not only erroneous but also contrary to settled principles of due process.

For these reasons, the district court erred in denying Riddle’s request for an evidentiary hearing. The claim of prosecutorial misconduct and bias is not frivolous, and the record supports the need for a full hearing. The denial of such a hearing violated Riddle’s constitutional rights under the Fourteenth Amendment.

II. The District Court Erred in Granting Summary Judgment Without Considering the Full Record.

The district court’s grant of summary judgment was premature and contrary to established precedent. In *Ex parte Wilson*, 114 U.S. 417 (1885), the Court held that “a writ of habeas corpus is not to be denied merely because the petitioner has not presented a perfect case, but because the court is satisfied that the facts do not support a constitutional violation.” The court must consider the entire record, including pro se filings and uncontroverted facts.

Here, Riddle presented a detailed account of the events, including the alleged threats by Hill, the inconsistency in the prosecution’s narrative, and the physical evidence suggesting a staged burglary. The district court failed to consider these facts in the context of the constitutional standard. In *Ex parte Royall*, 117 U.S. 241 (1886), the Court emphasized that “the court must examine the entire record to determine whether the conviction was obtained in accordance with the law.” The court’s failure to do so here was a clear error.
