ARGUMENT

I. The Post-Removal-Period Statute Cannot Be Read to Authorize Indefinite Detention of a Resident Alien Without Raising Serious Constitutional Questions.

The Fifth Amendment’s Due Process Clause protects “any person” within the United States, including resident aliens. *Yick Wo v. Hopkins*, 118 U.S. 356, 369 (1886). Congress may, of course, detain aliens for the purpose of effecting their removal. *Fong Yue Ting v. United States*, 149 U.S. 698, 730 (1893). But that detention must be “incidental” to removal and only for a “reasonable time.” *Wong Wing v. United States*, 163 U.S. 228, 235 (1896). When removal becomes impossible or not reasonably foreseeable, continued detention ceases to serve any legitimate regulatory purpose and becomes punitive—a deprivation of liberty without the procedural protections required by the Constitution. *Id.* at 237.

The Court has long recognized that the power to detain pending deportation is not unlimited. In *United States ex rel. Tisi v. Tod*, 264 U.S. 131, 133 (1924), the Court held that an alien could be detained only “for a reasonable time” to effect removal. Where no country will accept the alien, detention “for an indefinite period” is not authorized. *Id.* at 134. Similarly, in *Chin Yow v. United States*, 208 U.S. 8, 13 (1908), the Court stressed that “the right to personal liberty” is paramount and that detention must be justified by a legitimate governmental purpose.

Here, the Government seeks to detain petitioner indefinitely—without any prospect of removal—solely because he is a convicted criminal. But the Due Process Clause forbids such “preventive detention” of a resident alien without a trial or other procedural safeguards. *Wong Wing*, 163 U.S. at 235–37. In *Wong Wing*, the Court struck down a statute that imposed hard labor on Chinese aliens without judicial process, holding that “no greater punishment” than that authorized by Congress “can be inflicted upon any person” without due process. *Id.* at 237. Indefinite civil detention, if not tied to a legitimate regulatory purpose, is equally impermissible. *See also Ex parte Milligan*, 71 U.S. 2, 119 (1866) (the Constitution “cannot be suspended during any of the exigencies of government”).

The Government relies on *Shaughnessy v. United States ex rel. Mezei*, 345 U.S. 206 (1953), which upheld indefinite detention of an *excludable* alien at the border. But *Mezei* expressly distinguished aliens who have “effected an entry into the United States.” *Id.* at 212. Petitioner is a resident alien who has long been admitted to this country. He is entitled to “the full protection of the Due Process Clause.” *Kwong Hai Chew v. Colding*, 344 U.S. 590, 596 (1953). Indeed, *Kwong Hai Chew* held that a returning resident alien could not be excluded without a hearing, precisely because his “status” as a resident entitled him to constitutional protections. *Id.* at 598–600.

Moreover, the statute at issue does not apply only to “a small segment of particularly dangerous individuals.” It sweeps broadly, covering aliens who committed any number of removable offenses, including minor violations. The Constitution does not permit Congress to authorize the indefinite detention of a whole class of persons without a “legislative determination” that they are dangerous and a “judicial hearing” to test that determination. *See United States ex rel. Turner v. Williams*, 194 U.S. 279, 290 (1904) (upholding detention of an anarchist only because Congress had made a specific finding that such persons were dangerous). Here, no such finding exists.

II. The Statute Must Be Construed to Avoid Serious Constitutional Doubts.

This Court has long held that “where a statute is susceptible of two constructions, by one of which grave and doubtful constitutional questions arise and by the other of which such questions are avoided, our duty is to adopt the latter.” *United States v. Delaware & Hudson Co.*, 213 U.S. 366, 408 (1909). Section 1231(a)(6) does not explicitly authorize indefinite detention; it says only that the alien “may be detained beyond the removal period.” The statute does not specify that such detention may continue for life. Read in context, the provision is best understood as authorizing detention only for a period reasonably necessary to secure removal. *See Crowell v. Benson*, 285 U.S. 22, 62 (1932) (courts must construe statutes to avoid constitutional infirmity).

If Congress had intended to authorize indefinite detention, it would have said so plainly. *See Ex parte Endo*, 323 U.S. 283, 300 (1944) (detention authority must be “expressly granted” by Congress). The absence of clear language—combined with the serious constitutional questions such detention would raise—compels a narrow construction.

III. Detention Beyond a Reasonable Time Is Not Authorized Where Removal Is Not Foreseeable.

The record shows that petitioner has been detained for years beyond the 90-day removal period, with no country willing to accept him. Germany and Lithuania have both refused. As the Court recognized in *Tisi*, 264 U.S. at 133, “the detention of the alien is not for the purpose of punishment, but is merely to make sure that he will be on hand when the order of deportation is executed.” When removal is impossible, the “sole reason for detention” disappears. *Id.* at 134. Continued detention then becomes “the very definition of tyranny.” *Ex parte Milligan*, 71 U.S. at 119.

The Constitution does not require an alien to prove that removal is *impossible*. It is enough that removal is not “reasonably foreseeable.” *See Wong Wing*, 163 U.S. at 235 (detention must be “limited to a reasonable time”). Petitioner has made that showing. The Government has failed to rebut it. Therefore, his continued detention violates the Due Process Clause.

CONCLUSION

The judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed as modified, and the district court’s order of release should be upheld.
