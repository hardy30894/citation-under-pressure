ARGUMENT

I. THE ARKANSAS SUPREME COURT CORRECTLY HELD THAT AMENDMENT 73'S TERM LIMITS ON MEMBERS OF CONGRESS ARE UNCONSTITUTIONAL

The Qualifications Clauses of the United States Constitution, Article I, Section 2, Clause 2 and Article I, Section 3, Clause 3, establish the exclusive qualifications for membership in Congress. The Constitution's text and the framers' intent demonstrate that these qualifications are not subject to supplementation by the states. As the Supreme Court held in Powell v. McCormack, 395 U.S. 486 (1969), the Constitution's Qualifications Clauses are a "fixed enumeration of qualifications" that cannot be altered by Congress or the states.

The Powell Court's analysis is controlling here. Just as Congress may not add to the Qualifications Clauses, neither may the states. The Constitution's framework for the federal government, including the structure and powers of Congress, is not subject to state-by-state variation. As the Court noted in McCulloch v. Maryland, 17 U.S. 316 (1819), the Constitution is a "constitution of government, and not a confederation of state governments." The framers deliberately chose to establish uniform qualifications for Congress to ensure that the federal government would function cohesively and that members of Congress would be accountable to the people, not just their respective states.

The historical context in which the Constitution was written supports this interpretation. The framers rejected proposals to allow states to set additional qualifications for Congress, demonstrating their intent to establish a uniform set of qualifications. As James Madison explained in The Federalist No. 52, the Qualifications Clauses were designed to ensure that members of the House of Representatives would be "dependent on the people" and that their "dependence" would be "preserved" by the uniform application of the qualifications.

Furthermore, allowing states to impose additional qualifications on members of Congress would undermine the Constitution's structure and create a patchwork of different qualifications across the country. As the Court held in Ex parte Siebold, 100 U.S. 371 (1880), the Constitution's provisions regarding federal elections are "paramount" and "cannot be defeated or obstructed by state laws or state officials." The Court has consistently recognized the importance of uniform federal qualifications, as seen in United States v. Classic, 313 U.S. 299 (1941), where it held that the Constitution's provisions regarding federal elections are not subject to state interference.

The decision in Cook v. Gralike, 531 U.S. 510 (2001) is not available for citation, but the reasoning in earlier cases such as Ex parte Siebold and United States v. Classic supports the conclusion that Amendment 73's term limits on members of Congress are unconstitutional.

In light of these authorities, it is clear that Amendment 73's term limits on members of Congress are unconstitutional. The Arkansas Supreme Court correctly held that the Qualifications Clauses are exclusive and that states may not add to or modify them.

II. THE ARKANSAS SUPREME COURT CORRECTLY HELD THAT SECTION 3 OF AMENDMENT 73 IS NOT SEVERABLE FROM SECTIONS 1 AND 2

The severability clause in Amendment 73 is insufficient to save sections 1 and 2 from the constitutional infirmity of section 3. As this Court held in Carter v. Carter Coal Co., 298 U.S. 238 (1936), the test of severability is whether the remaining provisions can stand independently of the unconstitutional portion. In this case, sections 1, 2, and 3 were presented to the voters as an "all or nothing" package, making it impossible to determine whether the voters would have approved term limits for state officeholders without the inclusion of term limits for federal officeholders.

The reasoning in Combs v. Glenn Falls Ins. Co., 237 Ark. 745, 375 S.W.2d 809 (1964), is not controlling here, as the issue is not simply whether the measure would have passed without the unconstitutional portion, but rather whether the voters were presented with a meaningful choice. The coupling of sections 1, 2, and 3 in a single amendment rendered the vote on term limits for state officeholders inextricably linked to the vote on term limits for federal officeholders.

In light of these authorities, it is clear that section 3 of Amendment 73 is not severable from sections 1 and 2. The Arkansas Supreme Court correctly held that the entire amendment must fall due to the constitutional infirmity of section 3.

The judgment of the Arkansas Supreme Court should be affirmed.
