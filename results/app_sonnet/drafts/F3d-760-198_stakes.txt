# ARGUMENT

## I. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED BY EXCLUDING FACIALLY SEX-NEUTRAL CONDUCT FROM THE HOSTILE WORK ENVIRONMENT ANALYSIS

### A. Legal Standard

To establish a hostile work environment claim, a plaintiff must show "that the workplace is permeated with discriminatory intimidation, ridicule, and insult that is sufficiently severe or pervasive to alter the conditions of the victim's employment and create an abusive working environment." Harris v. Forklift Systems, Inc., 510 U.S. 17, 21 (1993). Courts examine "all the circumstances," including "the frequency of the discriminatory conduct; its severity; whether it is physically threatening or humiliating, or a mere offensive utterance; and whether it unreasonably interferes with an employee's work performance." Id. at 23.

Critically, this Court has held that a hostile work environment claim need not rest exclusively on conduct that is overtly sexual in nature. In Alfano v. Costello, 294 F.3d 365, 375 (2d Cir. 2002), this Court explained that "[f]acially sex-neutral incidents" may support a Title VII hostile work environment claim so long as "a reasonable fact-finder could conclude" that those incidents "were in fact based on sex." The relevant inquiry is not whether each individual act is sexually explicit, but "whether all the incidents of harassment complained of are part of the same actionable hostile work environment practice." Id. Facially neutral mistreatment can support a finding of discrimination when it is "part of a course of sex-based discrimination" together with more overtly sex-based conduct. Id.

### B. The District Court Improperly Segregated the Sexually Offensive Conduct from the Broader Pattern of Sex-Based Discrimination

The district court's error was fundamental: it artificially bifurcated Moll's factual allegations into two categories—"sexually offensive acts" and everything else—and then dismissed the claim because the only conduct falling into the first category fell outside the limitations period. This approach directly contravenes Alfano's instruction that courts must consider "the totality of the circumstances" when assessing a hostile work environment claim. 294 F.3d at 374.

Moll's Complaint alleges a continuous pattern of conduct spanning from 1998 through her constructive removal from the Buffalo office, including:

- Sexually charged notes and phone calls from her supervisor, Irving;
- Irving's refusal to communicate with Moll except in person, while permitting normal communication with male colleagues;
- Denial of promotion opportunities granted to less-senior male colleagues;
- Disparate treatment regarding work-from-home privileges, extended to male colleagues but denied to Moll;
- Denial of vacation requests granted to male colleagues with less tenure;
- Exclusion of Moll and other female employees from workplace social events.

Under Alfano, each of these facially neutral acts—the promotion denials, the vacation denial, the exclusion from social events, the disparate work-from-home policy—must be considered alongside the sexually charged notes and phone calls to determine whether, in totality, they reflect a "workplace... permeated with discriminatory intimidation." Harris, 510 U.S. at 21. The district court's refusal to consider this evidence in the aggregate, and its insistence that only overtly "sexual" conduct could anchor the claim, contradicts the reasoning of Alfano, which expressly recognizes that sex-based hostile work environments are frequently constructed through a mixture of overtly sexual and facially neutral discriminatory treatment. 294 F.3d at 375.

Because the district court excised the facially neutral conduct from its timeliness and merits analysis, it never asked the correct question: whether the neutral conduct, considered together with the sexual harassment, formed part of the same continuing violation. This Court should vacate the grant of summary judgment and remand for the district court to conduct the totality-of-circumstances analysis Alfano requires.

## II. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN APPLYING THE "SHAM ISSUE OF FACT" DOCTRINE TO DISREGARD NON-PARTY WITNESS TESTIMONY

### A. Legal Standard

It is well established that "a party may not create a material issue of fact by contradicting his own earlier testimony," in an effort to manufacture a factual dispute sufficient to survive summary judgment. This doctrine, however, is narrowly tailored to address self-serving contradictions by a party to the litigation who has a stake in the outcome.

### B. The Doctrine Does Not Extend to Non-Party Witnesses

The rationale underlying the sham issue of fact doctrine—preventing a party from strategically manufacturing factual disputes—simply does not apply when a non-party witness later clarifies, supplements, or even contradicts prior statements. A non-party witness has no personal stake in the litigation's outcome and no motive to fabricate testimony to defeat summary judgment. Accordingly, when a non-party witness's subsequent testimony conflicts with earlier statements, the proper course is not automatic disregard, but rather an assessment of credibility—a function reserved for the jury, not the court on summary judgment. See Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, Inc., 477 U.S. 242, 255 (1986) ("Credibility determinations, the weighing of the evidence, and the drawing of legitimate inferences from the facts are jury functions, not those of a judge.").

The district court's application of the sham issue doctrine to discount a non-party witness's testimony—testimony that corroborated Moll's account of discriminatory treatment—improperly usurped the jury's role. That testimony should have been considered as competent evidence creating a genuine issue of material fact.

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should vacate the judgment of the district court and remand for further proceedings consistent with the totality-of-circumstances standard articulated in Alfano and the proper treatment of non-party witness testimony under the summary judgment standard set forth in Anderson.
