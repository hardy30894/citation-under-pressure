I. The District Court Misapplied the Commercial-Activity Exception By Treating All of Pakistan’s Conduct As Sovereign

The Foreign Sovereign Immunities Act codifies the restrictive theory of sovereign immunity. That theory was not a statutory invention. Long before the Act, the Supreme Court recognized that foreign sovereigns are immune only for true public acts, not for ordinary commercial dealings. In The Schooner Exchange v. McFaddon, 11 U.S. (7 Cranch) 116, 136 (1812), Chief Justice Marshall grounded immunity in the consent of the territorial sovereign and limited the exemption to the narrow case of a public armed vessel. The later merchant-ship case, Berizzi Bros. Co. v. S.S. Pesaro, 271 U.S. 562 (1926), applied a broader absolute immunity, but that doctrine was quickly confined. Compania Espanola de Navegacion Maritima, S.A. v. The Navemar, 303 U.S. 68 (1938), held that a foreign sovereign does not obtain immunity by simply asserting title or purpose; the act and possession must be genuinely public. Republic of Mexico v. Hoffman, 324 U.S. 30 (1945), refused to extend immunity to a commercial vessel where the Executive had not requested it, and Ex parte Republic of Peru, 318 U.S. 578, 588 (1943), described the immunity as a matter of grace and comity, not an absolute right.

By 1955 the Supreme Court understood that the old absolute immunity was giving way to the restrictive doctrine. National City Bank v. Republic of China, 348 U.S. 356, 361–65 (1955), held that a foreign sovereign cannot claim immunity from counterclaims when it invokes the jurisdiction of our courts. The restrictive doctrine was then applied directly to commercial transactions by the Second Circuit. In Victory Transport Inc. v. Comisaria General de Abastecimientos y Transportes, 336 F.2d 354 (2d Cir. 1964), a Spanish state agency was denied immunity for a voyage charter even though the underlying purpose was to procure food for the public. Petrol Shipping Corp. v. Kingdom of Greece, 326 F.2d 117 (2d Cir. 1964), reached the same conclusion for a commercial charter by a foreign ministry. These cases establish the controlling principle: courts must examine the nature of the act, not the governmental purpose behind it.

Here, the district court ignored the commercial nature of Pakistan’s later conduct. Beg’s complaint alleges that, after the initial expropriation, the Government of Punjab sent a representative to the United States who offered Beg an alternative parcel of land. That offer, and the exchange approved by the Lahore High Court, was not the exercise of a sovereign prerogative. A private landowner could have made the identical offer. Sending an emissary to the United States to negotiate an exchange of land is a commercial transaction carried on in this country. Under the nature-of-the-act test applied in Victory Transport and Petrol Shipping, that conduct places Beg’s claim squarely within 28 U.S.C. § 1605(a)(2).

The district court apparently treated the entire dispute as one continuous act of expropriation and therefore as necessarily sovereign. That was error. The complaint does not rest solely on the taking; it also rests on Pakistan’s independent decision to enter the American marketplace and bargain with a resident. The Navemar, 303 U.S. at 74, teaches that a sovereign’s bare assertion of public purpose is insufficient; courts must look to the actual nature of the sovereign’s action. Pakistan’s connection to Beg in this case was not an act of military force or public command; it was the presence of a government representative in the United States offering land. That is classic commercial activity.

Even if the original taking were a sovereign act, the claim is also “based upon” the later commercial transaction. The exchange offer, made on American soil, was an act performed in the United States in connection with commercial activity elsewhere, within the plain language of § 1605(a)(2). A foreign sovereign cannot immunize the whole controversy by characterising the beginning of the chain as expropriation while it simultaneously chooses to engage in commercial dealing with an American resident.

The district court’s additional concern about a direct effect in the United States was misplaced because the act was performed in the United States. A representative’s presence and offer was not merely an effect of activity abroad; it was the activity itself. And the consequence to Beg—loss of the promised exchange and of the ability to recover—flowed directly from that in-forum act. When a foreign government comes into this country to transact with a citizen, it cannot later claim the cloak of sovereignty to escape the consequences. Hoffman, 324 U.S. at 34–35.

The judgment below should be reversed.
