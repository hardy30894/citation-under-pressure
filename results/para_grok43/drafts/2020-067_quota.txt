The Eleventh Circuit correctly held that a reviewing court may consult the full record when assessing the third and fourth prongs of plain-error review. In *United States v. Olano*, 507 U.S. 725, 734 (1993), the Court stated that an appellate court may correct an error only if it is plain and “affects substantial rights.” The Court has repeatedly confirmed that this inquiry is not confined to the evidence presented to the jury. *United States v. Vonn*, 535 U.S. 55, 59 (2002), held that a court conducting plain-error review “may consult the whole record” when determining whether an omission affected substantial rights. *United States v. Dominguez Benitez*, 542 U.S. 74, 83 (2004), likewise directed courts to “consider the entire record” in evaluating whether a defendant has shown a reasonable probability that the error altered the outcome.

The same principle governs the fourth prong. *Johnson v. United States*, 520 U.S. 461, 469–70 (1997), examined the entire record before concluding that the error did not “seriously affect the fairness, integrity or public reputation of judicial proceedings.” *Puckett v. United States*, 556 U.S. 129, 142 (2009), reaffirmed that the fourth-prong inquiry “is meant to be applied on a case-specific and fact-intensive basis,” necessarily requiring review beyond the trial transcript alone. *Henderson v. United States*, 568 U.S. 266, 276 (2013), and *Rosales-Mireles v. United States*, 138 S. Ct. 1897, 1907 (2018), continued this practice, each time looking to matters outside the jury’s view to decide whether the error warranted correction.

*Rehaif v. United States*, 139 S. Ct. 2191 (2019), did not alter these settled rules. The decision addressed only the elements the government must prove; it did not restrict the sources a court may examine when applying the third and fourth prongs of *Olano*. Because the Eleventh Circuit followed the methodology prescribed by *Vonn*, *Dominguez Benitez*, *Johnson*, *Puckett*, *Henderson*, and *Rosales-Mireles*, its judgment should be affirmed.
