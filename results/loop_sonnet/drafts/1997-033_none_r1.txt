# ARGUMENT

## I. The mandatory text of Section 1407(a) forecloses use of Section 1404(a) to convert a temporary pretrial assignment into a permanent trial venue.

Section 1407(a) commands that the Panel "shall" remand each transferred action "at or before the conclusion of" pretrial proceedings. That word choice is not incidental. This Court has long recognized that "shall" is ordinarily "the language of command," *Anderson v. Yungkau*, 329 U.S. 482, 485 (1947), and Congress's use of mandatory phrasing must be honored according to its terms. As this Court explained in *Caminetti v. United States*, "the meaning of a statute must, in the first instance, be sought in the language in which the act is framed, and if that is plain, ... the sole function of the courts is to enforce it according to its terms." 242 U.S. 470, 485 (1917). Section 1407(a) contains no exception for cases in which a transferee judge independently concludes, under the discretionary convenience standard of Section 1404(a), that retention would serve efficiency. The absence of any such qualification is itself telling, for "there is no more persuasive evidence of the purpose of a statute than the words by which the legislature undertook to give expression to its wishes." *United States v. American Trucking Ass'ns*, 310 U.S. 534, 543 (1940).

Respondent's countermotion asked the District of Arizona to do exactly what Section 1407(a) forbids: retain permanent custody of a case assigned to it solely "for coordinated or consolidated pretrial proceedings." Congress did not leave the duration of a multidistrict transfer to the discretion of the transferee court. It fixed that duration by statute. A transferee judge's belief that continued retention is convenient cannot override a command that Congress phrased in mandatory terms.

## II. Section 1407(a)'s specific limitation on the duration of multidistrict transfer controls over the general transfer authority of Section 1404(a).

Sections 1404(a) and 1407(a) address different problems and were drafted with different scope. Section 1404(a) is a "federal judicial housekeeping measure," applicable to ordinary civil litigation and designed "to protect litigants, witnesses, and the public against unnecessary inconvenience and expense." *Van Dusen v. Barrack*, 376 U.S. 612, 636–37 (1964). It permits transfer only to a district in which the action "might have been brought" by the plaintiff at the outset—a limitation this Court has enforced strictly, refusing to relax it even where the parties themselves preferred a different result. *Hoffman v. Blaski*, 363 U.S. 335, 343–44 (1960). Section 1407(a), by contrast, is a specialized mechanism enacted for the narrow purpose of coordinating overlapping pretrial proceedings, subject to the Panel's express and continuing duty to remand once those proceedings conclude.

Where Congress has enacted a specific provision governing a particular subject, that provision is not displaced by a general statute addressing the same broad area. As this Court held in *Fourco Glass Co. v. Transmirra Products Corp.*, "however inclusive may be the general language of a statute, it 'will not be held to apply to a matter specifically dealt with in another part of the same enactment.'" 353 U.S. 222, 228–29 (1957) (quoting *Rodgers v. United States*, 185 U.S. 83, 87–89 (1902)). Section 1407(a) specifically and exclusively governs the temporal scope of a Panel-ordered transfer. That specific command cannot be "controlled or nullified" by resort to the general transfer authority of Section 1404(a)—a statute that says nothing about multidistrict proceedings and was never intended to supply an exception to Section 1407(a)'s remand mandate. *Id.*

Nor does Section 1404(a)'s own purpose support such a use. That statute codifies the doctrine of forum non conveniens for ordinary venue disputes between litigants already free to choose among proper forums, *Ex parte Collett*, 337 U.S. 55, 61 (1949), and directs transfer only "for the convenience of parties and witnesses, in the interest of justice," *Continental Grain Co. v. Barge FBL-585*, 364 U.S. 19, 26 (1960). Nothing in that discretionary calculus authorizes a court that acquired a case solely by virtue of the Panel's pretrial assignment to displace the plaintiff's chosen forum for trial. Lexecon selected the Northern District of Illinois. Section 1404(a) supplies no license for the transferee court to override that choice by transferring the case to itself.

## III. The court of appeals erred in treating the statutory conflict as unsettled.

The Ninth Circuit acknowledged "what appears to be a clear statutory mandate," yet excused noncompliance based on Multidistrict Litigation Rule 14(b) and prior circuit practice. But an internal Panel rule cannot rewrite the words Congress enacted, and no court may relax a mandatory command because compliance proves inconvenient. Under *American Trucking*, 310 U.S. at 543, and *Caminetti*, 242 U.S. at 485, a court's "sole function" when statutory text is plain is to enforce it—not to weigh the competing policy considerations Congress itself already resolved. Because Section 1407(a) required remand of this action to the Northern District of Illinois "at or before" the conclusion of pretrial proceedings, and because Section 1404(a) furnishes no authority to override that specific command, the judgment below should be reversed.
