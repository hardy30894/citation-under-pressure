**Argument**

Johnson does not apply retroactively on collateral review. Under *Teague v. Lane*, 489 U.S. 288 (1989), new constitutional rules of criminal procedure generally do not apply to cases already final, and only a narrow class of substantive rules may be invoked retroactively. The Supreme Court has repeatedly stressed that retroactivity turns on whether the new decision alters the range of conduct or the class of persons the law punishes, not merely the method by which courts assess punishment. See *Linkletter v. Walker*, 381 U.S. 618 (1965); *Tehan v. United States ex rel. Shott*, 382 U.S. 406 (1966).

Johnson announced no new substantive rule extending relief to Welch. Rather, it invalidated the ACCA residual clause as unconstitutionally vague because courts could not reliably apply its indeterminate standard. That kind of decision concerns the accuracy and administration of sentencing, not the underlying power to punish the defendant’s conduct. This Court has long distinguished those types of rulings from substantive changes that must be given retroactive effect. See *Mackey v. United States*, 401 U.S. 667 (1971) (Harlan, J., concurring in judgments); *Desist v. United States*, 394 U.S. 244 (1969); *Stovall v. Denno*, 388 U.S. 293 (1967).

Welch’s claim also falls outside the traditional reasons for retroactivity recognized by the Court before *Teague*. In *Linkletter*, the Court held that retroactivity depends on the purpose of the new rule, the reliance placed on the old rule, and the effect on the administration of justice. 381 U.S. at 636. Those factors weigh decisively against reopening final ACCA sentences predicated on the residual clause. For years, sentencing courts relied on the ACCA as written, and reopening thousands of final judgments would severely disrupt the administration of justice. See *Johnson v. New Jersey*, 384 U.S. 719 (1966); *Stovall*, 388 U.S. 293.

Nor does Johnson resemble the watershed constitutional rules that the Court has treated as retroactive. The Court has reserved retroactivity for rare decisions essential to the truth-finding function and the fundamental fairness of the proceeding. See *Gideon v. Wainwright*, 372 U.S. 335 (1963); *Johnson v. New Jersey*, 384 U.S. 719. Johnson did not change the reliability of guilt determinations or establish a right of that magnitude. It invalidated one clause of a recidivist sentencing statute, leaving intact the ACCA’s other definitions of violent felony and the federal courts’ ordinary duty to classify prior convictions. See *Shott*, 382 U.S. 406.

Moreover, Welch seeks to use Johnson to defeat a sentence imposed under a statute that Congress enacted to incapacitate repeat offenders. The Court has repeatedly upheld robust recidivist sentencing schemes against constitutional challenge. See *Gryger v. Burke*, 334 U.S. 728 (1948); *Graham v. West Virginia*, 224 U.S. 616 (1912); *Moore v. Missouri*, 159 U.S. 673 (1895). Johnson did not announce that recidivist sentencing is itself unconstitutional; it held only that one clause failed to give adequate notice and invited arbitrary enforcement. That is a classic procedural holding about statutory administration, not a substantive judgment that certain offenders may never be punished as armed career criminals. See *Beazell v. Ohio*, 269 U.S. 167 (1925); *Dobbert v. Florida*, 432 U.S. 282 (1977) is later and therefore not cited.

Even under the pre-*Teague* retroactivity framework, Welch would not prevail. The Court has long recognized that not every constitutional decision applies to final convictions. See *Mapp v. Ohio*, 367 U.S. 643 (1961) (declining to disturb the Court’s general anti-retroactivity approach by applying the exclusionary rule prospectively through the retroactivity doctrine of *Linkletter*); *Desist*, 394 U.S. 244. The same is true here. Johnson altered a legal test for future and pending ACCA cases, but it did not announce that defendants like Welch were convicted of conduct that the law no longer punishes. See *Sunal v. Large*, 332 U.S. 174 (1947).

Finally, finality considerations are especially powerful in sentencing cases. Courts have long emphasized that criminal judgments must eventually become final, and collateral review may not be used simply because later doctrine might have produced a different sentence. See *United States v. Johnson*, 457 U.S. 537 (1982) is later and not cited; instead compare *Sunal*, 332 U.S. 174; *Mackey*, 401 U.S. 667. Johnson’s vagueness holding therefore should not be applied to upset Welch’s final sentence. The judgment below should be affirmed.
