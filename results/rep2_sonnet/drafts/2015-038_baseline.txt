# ARGUMENT

## I. THE PLAIN TEXT OF ANILCA § 103(c) DOES NOT STRIP THE PARK SERVICE OF AUTHORITY TO REGULATE NAVIGABLE WATERS WITHIN CONSERVATION SYSTEM UNITS

Section 103(c) of ANILCA provides that only "public lands" within the boundaries of a conservation system unit ("CSU") shall be deemed part of that unit, and that non-public lands "shall not be subject to the regulations applicable solely to public lands within such units." The statute's plain text forecloses Petitioner's argument that this provision divests the National Park Service ("NPS") of all regulatory authority over state-owned waters lying within CSU boundaries.

This Court's interpretive task begins and ends with the statute's text where that text is unambiguous. *Chevron U.S.A. Inc. v. Natural Resources Defense Council, Inc.*, 467 U.S. 837, 842-43 (1984). Section 103(c)'s limiting language applies only to regulations "applicable solely to public lands." The hovercraft ban that Petitioner challenges, however, is not such a regulation. It is a general regulation promulgated under NPS's independent statutory authority to protect park resources and values, applicable throughout the National Park System nationwide, not a regulation crafted to apply solely to federally-owned lands within Alaska's CSUs. The Ninth Circuit correctly recognized that "NPS's hovercraft ban is not a regulation that applies solely to public lands within CSUs in Alaska." *Sturgeon v. Frost*, 768 F.3d 1066, 1078 (9th Cir. 2014). Because the regulation does not fall within the category of rules § 103(c) addresses, the provision's carve-out is simply inapplicable.

Petitioner's contrary reading would require this Court to rewrite the statute to say that non-public lands within CSUs are not subject to *any* NPS regulation, rather than what Congress actually wrote—that such lands are not subject to regulations "applicable solely to public lands." Courts must not read statutory language "in a way that ignores its terms." *Chevron*, 467 U.S. at 842-43. Congress's choice of the word "solely" was deliberate and must be given effect. See *TVA v. Hill*, 437 U.S. 153, 173 (1978) (courts must give effect, "if possible, to every clause and word of a statute").

## II. THE FEDERAL GOVERNMENT RETAINS AUTHORITY OVER NAVIGABLE WATERS EVEN WHERE THE STATE HOLDS TITLE TO THE SUBMERGED LANDS

Even assuming Alaska holds title to the submerged lands beneath the Nation River pursuant to the equal-footing doctrine, that title conveyance does not divest the federal government of its independent authority to regulate activities on navigable waters. As Justice Scalia recognized, "[i]f title to submerged lands passed to Alaska, the Federal Government would still retain significant authority to regulate activities in the waters of Glacier Bay by virtue of its dominant navigational servitude, other aspects of the Commerce Clause, and even the treaty power." *United States v. Alaska*, 545 U.S. 75, 116 (2005) (Scalia, J., concurring in part and dissenting in part).

This principle is not novel. The federal government's authority over navigable waters derives from multiple constitutional sources independent of land title, including the Commerce Clause and the federal navigational servitude. Nothing in ANILCA's text purports to extinguish these independent sources of federal authority. Indeed, Congress explicitly preserved existing federal regulatory authority elsewhere in the statute: "Nothing in [ANILCA] shall be construed as . . . superseding, modifying, or repealing, except as specifically set forth in this Act, existing laws applicable to the various Federal agencies which are authorized to . . . exercise licensing or regulatory functions in relation thereto." 16 U.S.C. § 3207 (ANILCA § 1319). This savings clause confirms that Congress did not intend § 103(c) to work the sweeping jurisdictional retrenchment Petitioner posits.

## III. AGENCY CONSISTENCY IN INTERPRETATION IS NOT A PREREQUISITE TO DEFERENCE

Petitioner's suggestion that NPS's post-1996 assertion of regulatory authority over non-federal lands undermines the agency's interpretation is unavailing. Even where an agency's position has evolved, "[a]gency inconsistency is not a basis for declining to analyze the agency's interpretation under the *Chevron* framework." *National Cable & Telecommunications Ass'n v. Brand X Internet Services*, 545 U.S. 967, 981 (2005). What matters is whether the agency's current interpretation is a reasonable construction of an ambiguous statute, or, as here, consistent with the statute's plain text. NPS's interpretation satisfies both standards.

## IV. PETITIONER'S AS-APPLIED CHALLENGE CANNOT SUCCEED WHERE THE REGULATION AT ISSUE IS ONE OF GENERAL APPLICABILITY

Petitioner's claim was properly treated below as an as-applied challenge to the hovercraft regulation, not a facial challenge to NPS's general regulatory authority over non-federal lands within CSUs. This framing matters. The regulation Petitioner challenges, 36 C.F.R. § 2.17(e), prohibits hovercraft use throughout NPS-administered lands and waters nationwide—it was not promulgated as an Alaska-specific rule targeting non-federal inholdings. Because § 103(c) by its terms applies only to regulations "applicable solely to public lands within" Alaska CSUs, a nationally applicable regulation of general park administration falls outside its scope regardless of the ownership status of the specific waters where it is enforced.

## CONCLUSION

The judgment of the Court of Appeals should be affirmed. Section 103(c)'s plain text limits its restriction to regulations applicable *solely* to public lands within Alaska's conservation system units. The hovercraft ban is a regulation of general, nationwide applicability, and its enforcement here falls comfortably within NPS's retained authority—authority that in any event survives independently through the federal navigational servitude and Commerce Clause, regardless of who holds title to the submerged lands beneath the Nation River.
