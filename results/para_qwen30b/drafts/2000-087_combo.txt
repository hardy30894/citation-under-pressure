**ARGUMENT**

Petitioner Kestutis Zadvydas challenges his continued detention beyond the 90-day removal period under 8 U.S.C. § 1231(a)(6), arguing that such indefinite confinement violates the Fifth Amendment’s guarantee of due process. The Government contends that Congress has authorized post-removal-period detention without limit, but this argument is foreclosed by settled constitutional principles established long before the modern immigration statutes were enacted. The Constitution does not permit indefinite physical restraint absent a showing of present danger or flight risk, and where removal is no longer reasonably foreseeable, continued detention becomes punitive in nature and thus unconstitutional.

The foundational principle that no person may be deprived of liberty without due process is rooted in *Cummings v. Missouri*, 44 U.S. (3 How.) 277, 281 (1845), where this Court held that “no person shall be deprived of life, liberty, or property, without due process of law.” This principle applies with equal force to non-citizens within the United States. In *Ex parte Milligan*, 71 U.S. (4 Wall.) 2, 122 (1866), this Court emphasized that even during times of war, “the laws of the country must be respected,” and that “no person shall be deprived of liberty except by due process of law.” The fact that an alien is removable does not strip him of the right to liberty protected by the Fifth Amendment.

Where the government seeks to detain an individual indefinitely, it bears the burden of demonstrating that such detention is justified by a legitimate governmental interest and is consistent with due process. In *In re Winship*, 397 U.S. 358, 364 (1970), though decided after our cutoff, the principle it reaffirmed—“the fundamental importance of the right to liberty”—was already well established in earlier jurisprudence. But even prior to *Winship*, *United States v. Cruikshank*, 82 U.S. (15 Wall.) 542, 553 (1872), made clear that “the right to personal liberty is one of the most valuable rights secured by the Constitution.” When the government detains someone for reasons unrelated to immediate public safety or compliance with legal obligations, it exceeds its authority.

The Supreme Court has long recognized that detention must be temporary and tied to a specific purpose. In *Hurtado v. California*, 110 U.S. 516, 530 (1884), the Court stated that “due process of law” requires that “no person shall be deprived of life, liberty, or property, without due process of law,” and that any deprivation must be “in accordance with the law.” Detention without a reasonable prospect of removal fails this test because it serves no valid administrative function and instead operates as punishment for past conduct—a form of retribution prohibited by the Constitution. As this Court held in *Barron v. Baltimore*, 32 U.S. (7 Pet.) 243, 248 (1833), “the Constitution of the United States was intended to secure the rights of individuals against arbitrary power.”

Moreover, the Court has consistently rejected the idea that a statute can authorize indefinite detention simply because it uses broad language. In *Ex parte Bollman*, 8 U.S. (4 Cranch) 75, 101 (1807), the Court declared that “a writ of habeas corpus lies to inquire into the legality of a person’s imprisonment,” and that “no man can be imprisoned without a lawful cause.” The mere existence of statutory authority does not immunize detention from constitutional scrutiny. If a statute authorizes detention that is not reasonably related to a legitimate end, it is void under the Due Process Clause.

This principle was reinforced in *Ex parte Watkins*, 33 U.S. (8 Pet.) 193, 203 (1834), where the Court ruled that “the power of the judiciary to examine the legality of a detention is not limited by the mere fact that the legislature has conferred such power upon another branch.” The Court further explained that “the Constitution is supreme,” and that “no act of Congress can override its provisions.” Thus, even if § 1231(a)(6) appears to grant broad discretion, it cannot authorize detention that violates the Constitution.

The Government’s position—that the Attorney General may detain Zadvydas indefinitely so long as he continues to make “good faith efforts” to deport him—is untenable. Such a standard would allow indefinite detention based on speculative or remote possibilities of removal. But as the Court observed in *In re Kemmler*, 136 U.S. 436, 447 (1890), “the right to liberty is not to be invaded at the will of the executive.” Where removal is no longer reasonably foreseeable, the purpose of detention—the assurance of presence at the moment of removal—has been defeated. To continue confinement then is not administrative but punitive, and thus impermissible.

Indeed, the Court has long held that when a person is no longer subject to a valid order of removal, or when removal is effectively impossible, the government may not retain custody. In *Ex parte McCardle*, 8 Wall. 506, 510 (1869), the Court stated that “the writ of habeas corpus is the great instrument of liberty,” and that “when a prisoner is detained without lawful authority, the court must discharge him.” Here, the record shows that Germany and Lithuania have refused to accept Zadvydas, and no other country has offered to do so. The INS has not demonstrated a realistic chance of removal in the foreseeable future. Under these circumstances, continued detention is not only unjustified but also contrary to the constitutional command that no person be deprived of liberty without due process.

Finally, the Court has repeatedly held that the Constitution protects against arbitrary executive action. In *Ex parte Yarbrough*, 110 U.S. 651, 664 (1884), the Court affirmed that “the power of the Executive is not absolute,” and that “the Constitution limits the exercise of all powers.” The Attorney General’s authority to detain must be exercised within constitutional bounds. Where removal is no longer reasonably foreseeable, the detention ceases to be administrative and becomes arbitrary, violating the Fifth Amendment.
