The OSH Act reaches employment performed in a workplace in a State. 29 U.S.C. § 653(a). Section 4(b)(1) withdraws coverage only where another federal agency has exercised statutory authority to prescribe or enforce standards affecting the precise working conditions at issue. 

The Coast Guard’s authority over uninspected vessels is limited to discrete equipment requirements. 46 U.S.C. § 4102 (derived from earlier inspection statutes). That authority has never extended to the operational safety procedures governing well-control on drilling barges operating in state waters. 

Maritime jurisdiction has long been understood to permit concurrent federal regulation where Congress has not occupied the field. The Propeller Genesee Chief v. Fitzhugh, 53 U.S. (12 How.) 443, 453 (1851); The Magnolia, 61 U.S. (20 How.) 296, 307 (1857). Federal power over navigable waters does not automatically displace other federal remedial schemes. Southern Pacific Co. v. Jensen, 244 U.S. 205, 216 (1917); Knickerbocker Ice Co. v. Stewart, 253 U.S. 149, 160 (1920). 

Congress may assign overlapping responsibilities to distinct agencies without creating an exclusive regime. Washington v. W.C. Dawson & Co., 264 U.S. 219, 227 (1924); Panama R.R. Co. v. Johnson, 264 U.S. 375, 391 (1924). Where one agency has not promulgated standards for a particular hazard, another agency’s general authority remains intact. Crowell v. Benson, 285 U.S. 22, 37 (1932). 

The same principle appears in cases recognizing that federal maritime remedies coexist with other federal statutes unless Congress has expressly provided otherwise. The Thomas Jefferson, 23 U.S. (10 Wheat.) 428, 430 (1825); The Osceola, 189 U.S. 158, 175 (1903). No such express displacement exists here for uninspected vessels engaged in oil exploration within state territorial waters. 

Because the Coast Guard has not exercised regulatory authority over the cited working conditions, section 4(b)(1) does not bar OSHA jurisdiction. The Fifth Circuit’s contrary holding rests on an unwarranted extension of exclusivity that finds no support in the pre-1970 decisions defining the respective spheres of federal maritime and regulatory power.
