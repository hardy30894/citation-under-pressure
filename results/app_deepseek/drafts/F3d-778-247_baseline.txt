ARGUMENT

I. The Search Warrant Lacked a Sufficient Nexus to the Place Searched

The Fourth Amendment requires that a warrant “particularly describ[e] the place to be searched” and be supported by probable cause. *Illinois v. Gates*, 462 U.S. 213, 238 (1983). Probable cause demands a “nexus between the contraband to be seized or the suspected criminal activity and the place to be searched.” *United States v. Tiem Trinh*, 665 F.3d 1, 10 (1st Cir. 2011). Here, the affidavit failed to establish that evidence of child exploitation would be found at Joubert’s parents’ home.

The affidavit described complaints of molestation occurring years earlier, but provided no direct evidence that incriminating items were then located at the parents’ home. The only link was SJ’s report that Joubert moved computer components there after destroying a tower. But SJ did not see Joubert discard the components, and the affidavit did not allege that the components contained contraband. The affiant’s conclusory statement that persons “often maintain” such evidence is insufficient. *United States v. Feliz*, 182 F.3d 82, 87 (1st Cir. 1999) (rejecting boilerplate assertions). Without a “substantial basis” for inferring evidence would be found at that address, the warrant was invalid. *Gates*, 462 U.S. at 238-39.

II. The District Court Abused Its Discretion in Admitting Uncharged Molestation Testimony

Federal Rule of Evidence 404(b) prohibits evidence of other acts “to prove a person’s character in order to show that on a particular occasion the person acted in accordance with the character.” Such evidence is admissible only for a non-propensity purpose, and its probative value must not be substantially outweighed by unfair prejudice. *United States v. Varoudakis*, 233 F.3d 113, 118 (1st Cir. 2000). Here, the government introduced testimony from SJ and others about uncharged molestations spanning decades. This evidence served no purpose other than to portray Joubert as a serial pedophile, inviting the jury to convict based on character. *United States v. Rodriguez-Soler*, 773 F.3d 289, 296 (1st Cir. 2014) (reversing where 404(b) evidence was “overwhelmingly prejudicial”). The charged conduct—a single videotaped incident with KC—was straightforward; the uncharged acts were unnecessary to prove intent or plan. The district court’s balancing was an abuse of discretion.

III. The Commerce Clause Does Not Support Application of 18 U.S.C. §§ 2251(a) and 2252A(a)(5)(B) to Joubert’s Intrastate Conduct

Congress may regulate only activities that “substantially affect interstate commerce.” *United States v. Lopez*, 514 U.S. 549, 558-59 (1995). Joubert’s conduct—coercing a child in a private home and possessing a homemade videotape—was purely intrastate and non-economic. Unlike the child pornography statutes upheld in *United States v. Poulin*, 631 F.3d 17, 22 (1st Cir. 2011), which required a nexus to interstate commerce (e.g., use of a computer or camera manufactured outside the state), the government here offered no evidence that Joubert’s activities crossed state lines or substantially affected any market. The mere fact that the videotape was later seized does not retroactively create a commercial nexus. *United States v. Morrison*, 529 U.S. 598, 613 (2000) (rejecting aggregation of non-economic violent crimes). Applying these statutes to wholly local conduct exceeds Congress’s commerce power.

IV. The 480-Month Sentence Is Substantively Unreasonable

A sentence must be “sufficient, but not greater than necessary” to achieve the purposes of sentencing. 18 U.S.C. § 3553(a); *Gall v. United States*, 552 U.S. 38, 50 (2007). Joubert’s 40-year sentence—effectively a life sentence for a man of his age—is greater than necessary. The district court gave insufficient weight to Joubert’s lack of prior convictions, his age, and the non-violent nature of the offense. *United States v. Martin*, 520 F.3d 87, 93 (1st Cir. 2008) (remanding where court failed to consider mitigating factors). The sentence also creates unwarranted disparities with similar offenders. The court abused its discretion by imposing a term that is “plainly unreasonable” in light of the § 3553(a) factors. *Gall*, 552 U.S. at 51.

CONCLUSION

For these reasons, the convictions should be reversed or the sentence vacated and remanded for resentencing.
