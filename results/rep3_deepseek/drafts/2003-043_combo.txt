**ARGUMENT**

The decision of the court of appeals should be affirmed. The Internal Revenue Service cannot collect a partnership’s tax deficiency from individual partners without first making individualized assessments against those partners. The IRS’s timely assessment against the partnership extended the statute of limitations only as to the partnership, not as to the individual partners.

**I. The IRS Must Assess Each Taxpayer Individually Before Collecting from That Taxpayer**

The Internal Revenue Code requires that an “assessment shall be made by recording the liability of the taxpayer.” 26 U.S.C. § 6203. A “taxpayer” is “any person subject to any internal revenue tax,” and a “person” includes both “an individual” and a “partnership.” 26 U.S.C. § 7701(a)(14), (a)(1). The partnership and each individual partner are separate taxpayers. *See United States v. Coson*, 286 F.2d 453, 458 (9th Cir. 1961) (holding that a valid assessment requires identification of the taxpayer, and an assessment naming only the partnership does not constitute an assessment against an individual partner). Because the IRS assessed only the partnership, it never assessed the individual partners within the three-year limitations period. 26 U.S.C. § 6501(a).

The IRS’s argument that an assessment against the partnership suffices to collect from partners conflates two distinct taxpayers. In *United States v. Updike*, 281 U.S. 489 (1930), this Court held that a partnership and its partners are separate entities for tax purposes, and that the government must proceed against each individually to collect partnership tax liabilities. *Id.* at 494-95. Similarly, in *United States v. Garbutt Oil Co.*, 302 U.S. 528 (1938), the Court recognized that the government’s right to collect from individual partners depends on compliance with statutory procedures applicable to each taxpayer. *Id.* at 531-32.

The Ninth Circuit’s decision in *Young v. Riddell*, 283 F.2d 909 (9th Cir. 1960), does not support the IRS. There, the court merely held that a partner who voluntarily paid a partnership tax could not obtain a refund. *Id.* at 910. The court did not hold that the IRS could collect from an unwilling partner without an individual assessment. Indeed, the district court in *Young* expressly acknowledged that individual assessment is necessary to collect without resorting to court action. *Young v. Riddell*, 60-1 U.S. Tax Cas. (CCH) ¶ 9831, at 76,050 (S.D. Cal. 1959). *Coson* confirms this principle: the Ninth Circuit affirmed the invalidity of a lien against a partner where no individual assessment had been made. 286 F.2d at 458.

**II. The Statute of Limitations Bars Collection from the Individual Partners**

Because the IRS failed to assess the individual partners within three years, the statute of limitations has expired. 26 U.S.C. § 6501(a). The assessment against the partnership extended the collection period to ten years only for the partnership. 26 U.S.C. § 6502(a). The partners remain separate taxpayers with their own limitations periods. *See United States v. Lehigh Valley R.R. Co.*, 220 U.S. 257 (1911) (holding that statutory limitations periods are specific to each taxpayer and cannot be extended by actions against other entities). *See also United States v. Wurts*, 303 U.S. 414 (1938) (strictly construing limitations periods in tax collection cases).

The IRS’s reliance on state partnership law does not alter this result. Under California law, a judgment against a partnership is not a judgment against a partner. Cal. Corp. Code § 16307(c). A creditor must obtain a separate judgment against the partner to reach the partner’s assets. *See United States v. Standard Oil Co.*, 155 F.2d 479 (9th Cir. 1946) (applying state law to determine the procedural requirements for collecting partnership debts). The IRS obtained no such judgment against the partners, and the time to do so has expired.

**III. The IRS’s Interpretation Would Evade Congressional Intent**

Congress intended the assessment and limitations provisions to protect taxpayers from stale claims. *See United States v. Garbutt Oil Co.*, 302 U.S. at 531-32. Allowing the IRS to collect from partners without individual assessments would render the three-year limitations period meaningless for partners. *See United States v. Coson*, 286 F.2d at 458 (rejecting the government’s attempt to bypass statutory requirements). The Ninth Circuit correctly held that the IRS must follow the statutory procedures for each taxpayer.

**CONCLUSION**

The judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
