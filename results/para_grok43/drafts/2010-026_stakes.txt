**ARGUMENT**

The Ninth Circuit erred in holding that the ESQD documents are exempt under 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(2). That exemption covers only matters “related solely to the internal personnel rules and practices of an agency.” The court’s “predominantly internal” test, drawn from Crooker v. Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco & Firearms, 670 F.2d 1051 (D.C. Cir. 1981) (en banc), expands the statute beyond its text and the narrow construction required by Dep’t of the Air Force v. Rose, 425 U.S. 352 (1976).

FOIA embodies “a general philosophy of full agency disclosure unless information is exempted under clearly delineated statutory language.” Rose, 425 U.S. at 360-61 (quoting S. Rep. No. 813-89, at 3 (1965)). The nine exemptions are “explicitly exclusive.” U.S. Dep’t of Justice v. Tax Analysts, 492 U.S. 136, 151 (1989) (quoting Adm’r FAA v. Robertson, 422 U.S. 255, 262 (1975)). They “are to be interpreted narrowly.” Lahr v. NTSB, 569 F.3d 964, 973 (9th Cir. 2009).

The statutory phrase “internal personnel rules and practices” refers to employment-related matters such as “parking facilities, lunch hours, and sick leave.” Rose, 425 U.S. at 363. Hardy v. Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco & Firearms, 631 F.2d 653 (9th Cir. 1980), extended Exemption 2 only to “law enforcement materials, the disclosure of which may risk circumvention of agency regulation.” Id. at 656. It did not authorize withholding of operational safety data like the ESQD arcs, which the Navy uses “to design, array, and construct ammunition storage facilities.” The documents are not personnel rules; they are technical specifications governing facility layout.

Maricopa Audubon Society v. United States Forest Service, 108 F.3d 1082 (9th Cir. 1997), confirms the limit. There the court first asked whether the information related “solely,” or even “predominantly,” to “internal personnel rules and practices,” and only then considered the separate “law enforcement” rationale. Id. at 1085-87. The ESQD materials fail both inquiries: they are not personnel rules, and the Navy compiled them for facility design, not law-enforcement purposes. See Church of Scientology v. IRS, 611 F.2d 738, 748 (9th Cir. 1979).

By adopting Crooker’s “predominantly internal” standard, the Ninth Circuit replaced the statutory text with a policy-driven test that “distorts Congress’s careful balance.” The judgment should be reversed.
