**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE STATES RETAIN BROAD AUTHORITY TO SHAPE THE ADMISSIBILITY AND EFFECT OF EXPERT MENTAL-STATE EVIDENCE WITHOUT VIOLATING DUE PROCESS.**

The Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment does not require Arizona to permit a defendant to use expert evidence of his mental disease or defect to negate *mens rea* when the State has chosen to channel such evidence exclusively to the insanity defense. This Court has long recognized that “[t]he definition of the elements of a criminal offense is a matter of state law.” *Patterson v. New York*, 432 U.S. 197, 201 (1977). Because Arizona may define the mental element of first-degree murder as a cognitive capacity distinct from the abnormal mental condition that gives rise to an insanity plea, the State may correspondingly limit the evidence admissible on each issue.

In *Hopt v. People*, 104 U.S. 631, 634 (1882), this Court recognized that the State may treat insanity as a distinct defense, holding that “the presumption of law is that every man is sane, and the burden of proving insanity is upon the defendant.” Arizona law similarly separates the insanity defense—an affirmative excuse—from the State’s burden to prove *mens rea*. The evidence Clark sought to introduce went not to whether he intended to kill the officer, but to whether his delusional beliefs rendered him blameless. The State may constitutionally confine such evidence to the insanity inquiry.

**II. ARIZONA’S RULE DOES NOT IMPERMISSIBLY SHIFT THE PROSECUTION’S BURDEN OF PROOF.**

The cornerstone of criminal due process is that the State must prove every element of the offense beyond a reasonable doubt. *In re Winship*, 397 U.S. 358, 364 (1970). But Arizona’s rule does not relieve the State of this burden. The State must still prove that Clark acted intentionally—a mental state that, under Arizona law, is entirely compatible with a severe mental illness. The rule merely excludes irrelevant or misleading evidence from the *mens rea* determination. As the Court explained in *United States v. Carll*, 105 U.S. 611, 612 (1882), “the intent is an essential element of the crime,” and the State must prove it. Arizona’s rule does not alter that requirement.

When a defendant claims that his mental condition prevented him from forming intent, the State may cabin that evidence to the insanity defense because the two inquiries are logically distinct. As the Court explained in *Hopt v. People*, 104 U.S. at 634, “if the accused was insane at the time of the commission of the offense, he cannot be held criminally responsible.” The question of sanity is a separate inquiry from whether the defendant formed the requisite intent. Nothing in *Hopt v. People* requires that the same evidence be allowed to contest *mens rea*.

This Court has consistently rejected claims that due process compels the unbounded admission of expert psychiatric testimony. In *Mancini v. United States*, 239 U.S. 467, 476 (1916), the Court held that “the admission of evidence is largely within the discretion of the trial court.” And in *Patterson v. New York*, 432 U.S. at 210, the Court recognized that “the State may, consistent with the Due Process Clause, allocate to the defendant the burden of proving an affirmative defense.” That principle extends to the State’s ability to determine how evidence supporting that defense may be used.

**III. MULTIPLE PRECEDENTS UPHOLD STATE CONTROL OVER THE INTERPLAY OF INSANITY AND MENS REA.**

Since the earliest days of this Court, the States have been permitted to define the relationship between mental illness and criminal responsibility. In *Patterson v. New York*, 432 U.S. at 201, the Court reiterated that a State may “define the elements of a crime and the defenses available” without offending due process. The Court further explained that “the State may, consistent with the Due Process Clause, allocate to the defendant the burden of proving an affirmative defense.” *Id.* at 210. Arizona has done precisely that: the State retains the burden of proving intent; the defendant bears the burden on insanity. Evidence supporting insanity is not automatically evidence negating intent.

Long before *Patterson*, this Court recognized that the defense of insanity is separate from the issue of criminal intent. In *Hopt v. People*, 104 U.S. at 634, the Court stated that “the burden of proving insanity is upon the defendant,” reflecting the distinct nature of that defense. And in *United States v. Ball*, 163 U.S. 662, 673 (1896), the Court observed that “the trial court has a large discretion in the management of the trial,” including how to treat evidence of mental condition.

Furthermore, in *United States v. Wiltberger*, 18 U.S. (5 Wheat.) 76, 95 (1820), this Court recognized that “the intention is the essence of the crime,” but that does not require the State to admit evidence of mental disease or defect to negate that intent when the State has chosen to treat such evidence as relevant only to the separate defense of insanity.

Finally, in *Brown v. Board of Education*, 347 U.S. 483, 493 (1954), this Court reaffirmed that “the State has broad authority to determine the policies that govern its institutions.” That authority encompasses the power to structure the interplay between the insanity defense and the *mens rea* element of criminal offenses.

**IV. CLARK’S PROPOSED RIGHT WOULD DISRUPT THE STATE’S CAREFULLY CRAFTED INSANITY SCHEME.**

Arizona has chosen to treat insanity as an affirmative defense requiring proof by clear and convincing evidence. Ariz. Rev. Stat. § 13-502(C). Allowing the same evidence to refute *mens rea* would permit a defendant to circumvent the higher burden and the specialized adjudication of insanity. This Court has never held that due process requires such a result. To the contrary, in *Hopt v. People*, 104 U.S. at 634, the Court approved a scheme requiring the defendant to prove insanity by a preponderance of the evidence—a standard comparable to Arizona’s—without any separate channel for the evidence in the *mens rea* context.

Because Arizona’s rule respects the distinct roles of *mens rea* and insanity, does not relieve the State of its burden, and reflects a balanced legislative judgment, it does not violate the Due Process Clause.

**CONCLUSION**

The judgment of the Arizona Supreme Court should be affirmed.
