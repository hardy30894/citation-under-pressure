ARGUMENT

A criminal defendant lacks standing to challenge her conviction on the ground that the statute exceeds Congress’s enumerated powers or violates the Tenth Amendment. In Tennessee Electric Power Co. v. Tennessee Valley Authority, 306 U.S. 118 (1939), private utility companies contended that federal operations invaded powers reserved to the States. The Court held that the companies had “no standing in this suit to raise any question under the [Tenth] Amendment,” because “[t]here is no objection to the Authority’s operations by the states.” Id. at 144.

The same rule governs here. The Tenth Amendment “is not a source of private rights,” Massachusetts v. Mellon, 262 U.S. 447, 482 (1923), and a litigant may assert only her own legal rights, not “the rights of third parties,” Barrows v. Jackson, 346 U.S. 249, 255 (1953). A defendant therefore may not “rest [her] claim to relief on the legal rights or interests of third parties.” Tileston v. Ullman, 318 U.S. 44, 46 (1943) (per curiam).

This Court has repeatedly required that a party seeking to litigate the allocation of power between the federal government and the States demonstrate a concrete, personal legal interest. Muskrat v. United States, 219 U.S. 346, 361 (1911); Fairchild v. Hughes, 258 U.S. 126, 129 (1922). Absent such an interest, the controversy is not one “between adverse litigants” suitable for judicial resolution. Ashwander v. Tennessee Valley Authority, 297 U.S. 288, 325 (1936) (Brandeis, J., concurring).

The requirement is not relaxed when the challenge is framed as an attack on a criminal statute. “A party has standing to challenge the constitutionality of a statute only insofar as it has an adverse effect upon his own rights.” United States v. Raines, 362 U.S. 17, 21 (1960). Because the States alone are the direct beneficiaries of the Tenth Amendment’s reservation of power, a private defendant cannot invoke it to avoid federal prosecution.

The Third Circuit correctly applied these settled principles in holding that Bond lacked standing. The judgment should be affirmed.
