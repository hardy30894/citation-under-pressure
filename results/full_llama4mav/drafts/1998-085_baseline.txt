The American Dental Association (ADA) respectfully submits that an employer's conduct must be "egregious" or "outrageous," independent of its state of mind, to sustain an award of punitive damages under Title VII of the 1964 Civil Rights Act.

The Civil Rights Act of 1991 allows plaintiffs to recover punitive damages if they demonstrate that the defendant engaged in a discriminatory practice "with malice or with reckless indifference to the federally protected rights of an aggrieved individual." 42 U.S.C. § 1981a(b)(1). While the statute sets a mental state requirement for punitive damages, the legislative history indicates that Congress intended to limit such awards to "extraordinarily egregious cases." 137 Cong. Rec. S 15473 (Oct. 30, 1991) (Interp. Mem. of Sen. Dole et al.).

The courts have long recognized that punitive damages are not available merely upon a showing of intentional discrimination. See, e.g., Smith v. Wade, 461 U.S. 30, 56 (1983) (requiring "reckless or callous disregard" for punitive damages under § 1983). In the context of § 1981, which also allows for punitive damages, courts have required a showing of "egregious" or "outrageous" conduct. See, e.g., Emmel v. Coca-Cola Bottling Co., 95 F.3d 627, 636 (7th Cir. 1996); Kim v. Nash Finch Co., 123 F.3d 1046, 1063 (8th Cir. 1997).

Requiring egregious or outrageous conduct, in addition to the requisite mental state, is consistent with the common law trend. See Restatement (Second) of Torts § 908(2) (1979) (punitive damages available for "outrageous" conduct). It also aligns with the legislative history of § 1981a, which suggests that Congress intended to adopt a higher standard for punitive damages. See H.R. Rep. No. 102-40(II), at 29 (1991) (punitive damages available only in cases involving "egregious conduct").

In this case, the Court of Appeals erred by remanding for a trial on punitive damages without requiring a showing of egregious or outrageous conduct. The ADA respectfully submits that the Court should reverse this decision and hold that an employer's conduct must be "egregious" or "outrageous" to sustain an award of punitive damages under Title VII.

The contrary interpretation, adopted by some courts, would render the punitive damages provision of § 1981a nearly coextensive with the liability standard, undermining the distinction between compensatory and punitive damages. See, e.g., Kolstad v. American Dental Ass'n, 108 F.3d 1431, 1438 (D.C. Cir. 1997). This result is inconsistent with the statutory text and legislative history, which indicate that punitive damages are reserved for cases involving particularly egregious conduct.

In conclusion, the ADA respectfully submits that the Court should adopt a standard for punitive damages under Title VII that requires a showing of egregious or outrageous conduct, in addition to the requisite mental state. This interpretation is consistent with the statutory text, legislative history, and common law principles, and ensures that punitive damages are reserved for the most egregious cases of intentional discrimination.
