The rule announced in Johnson v. United States, 576 U.S. 527 (2015), regarding what constitutes a violent offense under the Armed Career Criminal Act (ACCA) applies retroactively to cases on collateral review. The Supreme Court's decision in Johnson held that the residual clause of the ACCA, which defined a violent felony as one that "otherwise involves conduct that presents a serious potential risk of physical injury to another," was unconstitutionally vague. 18 U.S.C. § 924(e)(2)(B)(ii). This holding was a substantive change in the law, as it narrowed the scope of the ACCA's definition of a violent felony.

The Supreme Court has held that a new rule of constitutional law applies retroactively to cases on collateral review if it is substantive or a "watershed rule[] of criminal procedure." Teague v. Lane, 489 U.S. 288, 311 (1989). A substantive rule is one that "alters the range of conduct or the class of persons that the law punishes." Schriro v. Summerlin, 542 U.S. 348, 353 (2004). The rule announced in Johnson is substantive because it changed the definition of a violent felony under the ACCA, thereby altering the range of conduct that is punishable under the statute.

In Welch's case, the Eleventh Circuit's determination that his prior conviction for strong arm robbery was a violent felony under the ACCA was based on the residual clause, which was subsequently held to be unconstitutionally vague in Johnson. As a result, Welch's sentence was improperly enhanced under the ACCA. The rule announced in Johnson applies retroactively to Welch's case, and his sentence should be vacated and remanded for resentencing.

The Supreme Court's decision in Montgomery v. Louisiana, 577 U.S. 190 (2016), supports this conclusion. In Montgomery, the Court held that the rule announced in Miller v. Alabama, 567 U.S. 460 (2012), which prohibited mandatory life sentences without parole for juveniles, applied retroactively to cases on collateral review. The Court reasoned that the Miller rule was substantive because it "rendered unconstitutional a mandatory sentence previously thought constitutional." Montgomery, 577 U.S. at 212. Similarly, the rule announced in Johnson rendered unconstitutional the residual clause of the ACCA, which had previously been used to enhance Welch's sentence.

Therefore, the rule announced in Johnson applies retroactively to Welch's case, and his sentence should be vacated and remanded for resentencing.
