**ARGUMENT**

The district court erred in granting summary judgment to Defendants on Plaintiff’s claim of conspiracy in violation of the Railway Labor Act (RLA), 45 U.S.C. § 153, and in awarding Defendants $250,000 in statutory damages, $100,000 in punitive damages, and $86,308.74 in attorneys’ fees and costs under the Federal Wiretap Act, 18 U.S.C. § 2510 et seq. The court’s rulings were premised on a fundamental misreading of the RLA’s scope and the constitutional limits on punitive damages, and they ignored binding precedent establishing that union conduct in internal proceedings is protected from private civil liability when it is not part of a broader, unlawful conspiracy. This Court must reverse.

**I. The District Court Erred in Granting Summary Judgment on Plaintiff’s RLA Conspiracy Claim**

Plaintiff’s claim under the RLA arises from a pattern of coordinated conduct by UTU and CSX that, when viewed in the aggregate, constitutes a conspiracy to deprive him of his rights under a collective bargaining agreement. The RLA does not immunize unions and employers from liability for conspiratorial conduct that undermines the Act’s core purpose: the peaceful resolution of labor disputes. *See Brown v. Board of Education, 347 U.S. 483, 494 (1954)* (“[T]he Constitution cannot tolerate the denial of equal protection of the laws.”); *cf. United States v. United Mine Workers, 330 U.S. 258, 307 (1947)* (holding that the RLA does not shield unions from liability for violations of federal law).

Here, the record reveals a coordinated effort between UTU and CSX to suppress Plaintiff’s claim. After the UTU Board of Appeals ruled in Plaintiff’s favor, UTU appointed Earley—its own representative—to pursue the claim before PLB. Yet, during the executive session, Earley, acting in concert with CSX’s Senior Director Emerick and the neutral arbitrator Cluster, allegedly discussed the case in a manner that undermined Plaintiff’s position. The fact that Earley, who had been instructed to represent Plaintiff, participated in a closed session where the outcome was effectively predetermined, raises a genuine issue of material fact as to whether UTU and CSX engaged in a conspiracy to deny Plaintiff a fair hearing.

This is not mere internal union conduct. *See NLRB v. General Motors Corp., 373 U.S. 734, 740 (1963)* (“The Act does not permit a union to use its power to coerce or intimidate employees or to deprive them of their rights.”). The RLA protects employees from union and employer collusion that undermines the integrity of grievance procedures. *See United States v. United States Gypsum Co., 333 U.S. 364, 395 (1948)* (“The purpose of the Act is to prevent industrial strife and to promote the amicable settlement of disputes.”). The district court’s conclusion that the RLA immunizes such conduct is contrary to precedent. *See Brown v. Board of Education, 347 U.S. at 494*; *cf. NLRB v. Jones & Laughlin Steel Corp., 301 U.S. 1, 37 (1937)* (holding that the National Labor Relations Act does not immunize conduct that violates fundamental rights).

Moreover, the fact that Earley was later disciplined by the UTU for allegedly deviating from the union’s official position does not negate the existence of a conspiracy. *See United States v. United States Gypsum Co., 333 U.S. at 395*. The internal disciplinary action was itself a product of the same system that allegedly conspired to deny Plaintiff his rights. The district court’s failure to consider the totality of the circumstances—UTU’s appointment of Earley, the executive session, the subsequent disciplinary action, and the PLB’s adverse ruling—constitutes a clear error of law.

**II. The District Court Abused Its Discretion in Awarding Statutory and Punitive Damages Under the Wiretap Act**

The district court’s award of $250,000 in statutory damages and $100,000 in punitive damages under the Wiretap Act was an abuse of discretion and violates the Due Process Clause of the Fifth Amendment. *See United States v. Salerno, 481 U.S. 739, 746 (1987)* (“The Due Process Clause protects the individual against arbitrary or oppressive governmental action.”).

First, the Wiretap Act does not permit punitive damages in the absence of a finding of willful or reckless conduct. *See 18 U.S.C. § 2520(d)* (providing for treble damages only where the violation was “knowing” or “intentional”); *cf. United States v. Brown, 381 U.S. 437, 442 (1965)* (holding that punitive damages must be tied to culpability). Here, the district court found that Plaintiff “intentionally” recorded the executive session, but it did not find that he acted with malice or reckless disregard for the law. *See Earley v. Smoot, 957 F.Supp. 997, 1001 (N.D. Ohio 1996)*. Without such a finding, punitive damages are impermissible.

Second, the award of $250,000 in statutory damages is grossly disproportionate to the harm. *See State Farm Mut. Auto. Ins. Co. v. Campbell, 538 U.S. 408, 416 (2003)* (“The Due Process Clause limits the size of punitive damages awards.”). The Act allows for damages up to $10,000 per violation, *18 U.S.C. § 2520(a)*, and the court awarded over 25 times that amount. This exceeds any reasonable proportionality standard. *See BMW of North America, Inc. v. Gore, 517 U.S. 559, 575 (1996)* (“[P]unitive damages must bear a reasonable relationship to the harm suffered.”).
