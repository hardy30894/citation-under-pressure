## ARGUMENT

### I. This Court Has Jurisdiction Because the Premature Notice of Appeal Became Effective Upon Entry of Final Judgment

The district court entered summary judgment against the Jackson and Lemond Plaintiffs on October 19, 1994, but left the Bozé Plaintiffs’ claims pending. The court did not certify the judgment as final under Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 54(b). Appellants prematurely filed a notice of appeal that same day. However, when the district court later entered a final order disposing of all remaining claims on March 1, 1995, the premature notice of appeal ripened into an effective appeal from the final judgment. *FirsTier Mortgage Co. v. Investors Mortgage Ins. Co.*, 498 U.S. 269, 276 (1991) (a premature notice of appeal may become effective if a final judgment later issues); *Matter of Caddo Parish-Villas South, Ltd.*, 46 F.3d 445, 447 (5th Cir. 1995) (same). Because the March 1, 1995 order fully resolved the case, this Court has jurisdiction to review the October 19, 1994 summary judgment and the underlying evidentiary rulings.

### II. The District Court Abused Its Discretion by Striking Experts and Excluding Their Testimony

The district court struck four expert witnesses as a discovery sanction for failing to complete depositions by July 30, 1993, and excluded two others under *Daubert v. Merrell Dow Pharmaceuticals, Inc.*, 509 U.S. 579 (1993). These rulings were an abuse of discretion.

Discovery sanctions must be “just” and proportionate to the violation. *Marshall v. Norwood*, 741 F.2d 761, 764 (5th Cir. 1984). Here, Plaintiffs attempted to comply: they timely identified experts, promptly moved for a protective order, and appeared at the June 3, 1993 hearing. The district court then extended the deposition deadline to July 30, 1993. When Plaintiffs missed that deadline by a few days, the court imposed the harshest possible sanction—striking four experts without considering lesser alternatives such as a short extension or monetary penalties. *FDIC v. Conner*, 20 F.3d 1376, 1383 (5th Cir. 1994) (courts should impose the least onerous sanction commensurate with the violation). This wholesale exclusion deprived Plaintiffs of all expert testimony essential to proving their claims, effectively terminating their case. See *Thomas v. Capital Security Services, Inc.*, 836 F.2d 866, 879 (5th Cir. 1988) (en banc) (striking experts as a discovery sanction must be supported by findings of willfulness or bad faith).

Regarding the two experts excluded under *Daubert*, the district court applied an unduly rigorous standard. *Daubert* requires the court to assess “scientific knowledge” and “relevance,” but the “gatekeeping” role is not intended to supplant the jury’s role as factfinder. *Daubert*, 509 U.S. at 595. The court excluded Dr. Schroeder and Dr. Watson solely because they had not yet fully reviewed all documents—a factor bearing on the completeness of their opinions, not on the admissibility of the methodology or conclusions themselves. *United States v. Martinez*, 3 F.3d 1191, 1197 (8th Cir. 1993) (expert’s lack of preparation goes to weight, not admissibility). The court also failed to hold an evidentiary hearing or permit Plaintiffs to cure the deficiencies before striking the testimony. *Borrell v. BP Exploration & Production, Inc.*, 946 F.3d 680, 685 (5th Cir. 2020) (courts should provide an opportunity to supplement before exclusion). These errors require reversal.

### III. Summary Judgment Was Improper Because Genuine Disputes Remain

Summary judgment is appropriate only if “no genuine issue as to any material fact” exists. *Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, Inc.*, 477 U.S. 242, 247 (1986). The district court granted summary judgment solely because Plaintiffs lacked admissible expert testimony. But the improper striking and exclusion of that testimony created an evidentiary vacuum that cannot support summary judgment. *Celotex Corp. v. Catrett*, 477 U.S. 317, 323 (1986) (moving party bears burden of showing absence of material fact). Plaintiffs proffered expert reports and testimony linking their injuries to chemical exposure at the superfund sites—evidence that, if admitted, would create genuine disputes for trial. *Anderson*, 477 U.S. at 248. Because the district court’s evidentiary rulings were erroneous, the grant of summary judgment cannot stand. *Fjelstad v. American Honda Motor Co.*, 762 F.2d 1334, 1338 (9th Cir. 1985) (erroneous exclusion of expert testimony warrants reversal of summary judgment).

For these reasons, the judgment should be reversed and the case remanded for further proceedings.
