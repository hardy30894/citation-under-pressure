ARGUMENT

I. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN HOLDING THAT ERDMAN FAILED TO ACCUMULATE 1,250 HOURS OF SERVICE UNDER THE FMLA.

A. All Hours That Nationwide Suffered or Permitted Erdman to Work Count Toward the Statutory Threshold.

The FMLA extends its protections only to employees who have worked "for at least 1,250 hours" during the twelve months preceding leave. 29 U.S.C. § 2611(2)(A)(ii). The Department of Labor has directed that this calculation "is determined according to the principles established under the [FLSA] for determining compensable hours of work." 29 C.F.R. § 825.110(c)(1). Under those principles, work an employer "suffers or permits" is compensable, regardless of whether the employer formally approved it in advance. See Forrester v. Roth's I.G.A. Foodliner, Inc., 646 F.2d 413, 414 (9th Cir. 1981) (employer's actual or constructive knowledge that an employee is working is determinative); Reich v. Dep't of Conservation & Nat. Res., 28 F.3d 1076, 1082 (11th Cir. 1994) (same). The employer "cannot disclaim knowledge" of work it knew about and from which it derived a benefit. Reich, 28 F.3d at 1082.

That principle governs here. For years, Erdman's supervisor, Patty Sarno, "consistently authorized payment for these hours or allowed [Erdman] to use them as 'comp' time." Nationwide cannot now disown the very hours it accepted the benefit of simply because a new supervisor later disapproved of the practice. The relevant question under the FLSA-derived standard is not whether Getgen blessed each hour in real time, but whether Nationwide, through Sarno and through its own conduct, suffered or permitted Erdman to perform the work. A reasonable jury could find that it did.

B. Genuine Issues of Material Fact Preclude Summary Judgment on the Hours Calculation.

Summary judgment is proper only where there is no genuine dispute of material fact and the movant is entitled to judgment as a matter of law, viewing the record in the light most favorable to Erdman. McTernan v. City of York, 564 F.3d 636, 646 (3d Cir. 2009). The District Court's eligibility determination rested entirely on Nationwide's own timekeeping records — records that, on this record, cannot be squared with the undisputed history of comp-time use. Erdman's September 2002 e-mail seeking "clarification" whether she could continue banking comp time; Getgen's own concession in January 2003 that Erdman's comp-time-based vacation calculation was correct; and the fact that Getgen did not tell Erdman to stop working extra hours until February 10, 2003 — all create a triable question as to how many hours Erdman actually worked, and were suffered or permitted by Nationwide to work, during the twelve months preceding her April 2003 leave request. Where an employer's own records are incomplete or contradicted by other evidence of work actually performed, the employee is not held to a burden of "impossible or unreasonable" precision, and the factfinder—not the court—must resolve the resulting factual dispute. See Anderson v. Mt. Clemens Pottery Co., 328 U.S. 680, 687–88 (1946). The District Court usurped that factfinding role.

II. IN THE ALTERNATIVE, NATIONWIDE IS EQUITABLY ESTOPPED FROM CONTESTING ERDMAN'S ELIGIBILITY.

Even if the hours calculation were not itself in dispute, Nationwide is equitably estopped from denying that Erdman qualified for FMLA leave. This Court has recognized that "an employer may be estopped from asserting the FMLA's eligibility requirements" where its own representations lead an employee reasonably to believe she is covered and to rely on that belief to her detriment. Sommer v. The Vanguard Grp., 461 F.3d 397, 403 (3d Cir. 2006). The Department of Labor's own interpretation, discussed in Sommer, provides that "if the employer fails to advise the employee whether he or she is eligible" for FMLA leave "prior to the date the requested leave is to commence," eligibility may be assessed as of that later date. Id. at 407 (discussing 29 C.F.R. § 825.110(d)). Other circuits addressing this precise scenario have likewise held that an employer's silence or affirmative assurance of coverage can estop a later eligibility defense. Kosakow v. New Rochelle Radiology Assocs., P.C., 274 F.3d 706, 725 (2d Cir. 2001).

Here, when Erdman submitted her FMLA paperwork on April 21, 2003, a Nationwide human resources employee told her that "as far as the FMLA, I probably don't see any problems with this." Erdman reasonably relied on that representation, declined to pursue other options for the August absence she needed to prepare Amber for school, and was terminated less than three weeks later. Nationwide should not now be permitted to raise an eligibility defense it effectively waived through its own employee's assurance.

CONCLUSION

For these reasons, the District Court's grant of summary judgment on Erdman's FMLA claims should be reversed and the case remanded for trial.
