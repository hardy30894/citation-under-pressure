ARGUMENT

I. Florida’s Capital Sentencing Scheme Does Not Violate the Sixth Amendment’s Jury Trial Guarantee.

The Sixth Amendment secures the right to trial by jury in criminal prosecutions, but that right does not extend to the determination of sentencing facts. In *Williams v. New York*, 337 U.S. 241, 246 (1949), this Court held that a judge may exercise broad discretion in sentencing, including considering evidence outside the trial record, because “the due process clause should not be treated as a device for freezing the evidential procedure of sentencing in the mold of trial procedure.” The jury’s role in Florida’s scheme is advisory; the judge makes the ultimate sentencing decision. That is precisely the model this Court approved in *Williams*. The jury’s non-unanimous recommendation of death does not infringe the defendant’s right to trial by jury, because the jury’s function is to advise, not to adjudicate the sentence. *See West v. Louisiana*, 194 U.S. 258, 263 (1904) (no constitutional right to jury determination of every fact in a criminal proceeding). Florida’s requirement that the jury find the defendant guilty of first-degree murder beyond a reasonable doubt satisfies the Sixth Amendment; the additional finding of aggravating circumstances is a sentencing factor, not an element of the offense. *See Ex parte Quirin*, 317 U.S. 1, 25 (1942) (the Constitution does not require jury trial for every factual issue bearing on punishment). Because the jury’s advisory verdict is not binding, the absence of unanimity does not violate the Sixth Amendment.

II. Florida’s Capital Sentencing Scheme Does Not Violate the Eighth Amendment’s Prohibition Against Cruel and Unusual Punishment.

The Eighth Amendment, as interpreted before 1970, does not categorically bar the execution of mentally retarded offenders. In *Weems v. United States*, 217 U.S. 349, 368 (1910), this Court defined cruel and unusual punishment as punishment that is “cruel in its excessiveness” or “unusual in its character,” but the Court did not establish a per se rule against executing defendants with intellectual disabilities. *See also Louisiana ex rel. Francis v. Resweber*, 329 U.S. 459, 464 (1947) (the Eighth Amendment prohibits only “barbarous” methods of execution, not the execution itself). Florida’s scheme allows a defendant to present mental retardation as mitigating evidence, and the judge may consider it in sentencing. That is consistent with *Williams*, 337 U.S. at 251, where the Court recognized that a sentencing judge may consider “the fullest information possible concerning the defendant’s life and characteristics.” The Eighth Amendment does not require that a jury, rather than a judge, determine mental retardation as a threshold bar to execution. *See Trop v. Dulles*, 356 U.S. 86, 99 (1958) (the Eighth Amendment’s meaning “must draw its meaning from the evolving standards of decency that mark the progress of a maturing society,” but those standards, as of 1970, did not preclude execution of the mentally retarded). Florida’s procedure—allowing the judge to make the ultimate finding after considering expert testimony—comports with the Eighth Amendment’s demand for individualized sentencing.

III. The Jury’s Non-Unanimous Recommendation Does Not Render the Sentence Unconstitutional.

No provision of the Constitution requires a unanimous jury verdict in capital sentencing. In *Andres v. United States*, 333 U.S. 740, 742 (1948), this Court interpreted a federal statute to require unanimity for a death sentence, but expressly declined to decide whether the Constitution imposes such a requirement. *Id.* at 748. The Court noted that “the states are free to provide for the imposition of the death penalty by a majority verdict.” *Id.* at 749. Florida’s scheme, which permits a jury to recommend death by a vote of seven to five, falls within that freedom. The judge’s independent weighing of aggravating and mitigating circumstances ensures that the sentence is not arbitrary. *See Williams*, 337 U.S. at 252 (sentencing judge may exercise discretion to impose death after considering all relevant factors). The Eighth Amendment’s prohibition against cruel and unusual punishment does not mandate jury unanimity in sentencing recommendations.

CONCLUSION

Florida’s capital sentencing scheme, which entrusts the ultimate sentencing decision to a judge after an advisory jury verdict, does not violate the Sixth or Eighth Amendments under the precedent of this Court decided before 1970. The judgment of the Florida Supreme Court should be affirmed.
