# ARGUMENT

## THE OCSLA EXTENDS LONGSHORE ACT COVERAGE TO ANY INJURY CAUSED BY OPERATIONS ON THE OUTER CONTINENTAL SHELF, REGARDLESS OF WHERE THE INJURY OCCURS

Section 1333(b) of the OCSLA provides compensation for "disability or death of an employee resulting from any injury occurring as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf for the purpose of exploring for, developing, removing, or transporting by pipeline the natural resources . . . of the outer Continental Shelf." 43 U.S.C. § 1333(b). The statute's plain text imposes a situs requirement on the *operations*—they must be conducted "on the outer Continental Shelf"—but imposes no such geographic limitation on the *injury*. Petitioners' contrary reading requires this Court to insert words Congress did not write.

### A. The Text of Section 1333(b) Contains No Situs-of-Injury Requirement

Congress knew how to write a situs-of-injury requirement when it wanted one. The Longshore Act itself contains such language, limiting compensation to injuries "occurring upon the navigable waters of the United States (including any adjoining pier, wharf, dry dock, terminal, building way, marine railway, or other adjoining area customarily used by an employer in loading, unloading, repairing, or building a vessel)." 33 U.S.C. § 903(a); *see Director, OWCP v. Perini North River Assocs.*, 459 U.S. 297, 299 (1983) (quoting this provision). Section 1333(b) contains no comparable clause tethering the *injury* to a particular location. Instead, it employs causal language—"resulting from," "as the result of"—which asks only whether operations on the Shelf caused the harm, not where the harm was suffered.

This textual distinction is decisive. Where Congress uses different language in related statutory provisions, that difference is presumed intentional. Congress amended § 903(a) in 1972 to extend Longshore Act coverage landward to adjoining piers and terminals, recognizing that maritime injuries do not always occur on water itself. *Perini*, 459 U.S. at 299. Section 1333(b), by contrast, asks a *causal* question about the source of the injury—operations on the Shelf—not a *geographic* question about the situs of the injury.

### B. This Court's Decision in *Herb's Welding* Confirms That § 1333(b) Turns on the Source of Operations, Not the Location of Injury

This Court has already described how § 1333(b) operates. In *Herb's Welding, Inc. v. Gray*, 470 U.S. 414 (1985), the Benefits Review Board had granted OCSLA benefits to a worker injured in state waters because "his injury nonetheless could be said to have occurred, in the words of the statute, 'as a result of' operations on the outer shelf." *Id.* at 418. The Board so held because it considered the claimant's "work 'integrally related' to such operations." *Id.* This Court's opinion recounts that holding without disapproval, describing only a dissenting Board member's separate view "that the Lands Act provides LHWCA benefits only for injuries actually occurring in the geographic area of the outer shelf." *Id.* The Court's neutral recitation of this dispute, immediately followed by its own resolution of the case on other grounds (the LHWCA's maritime-employment status requirement), demonstrates that a causation-based reading of § 1333(b) was neither novel nor foreclosed.

### C. The *Tallentire* Footnote Does Not Control and Is Not Considered Dicta

Petitioners rely heavily on a footnote in *Offshore Logistics, Inc. v. Tallentire*, 477 U.S. 207 (1986), stating that § 1333(b) "makes compensation for the death or injury of an 'employee' resulting from certain operations on the Outer Continental Shelf payable under the [LHWCA]," and that "because this case does not involve a suit by an injured employee against his employer pursuant to § 1333(b), this provision has no bearing on this case." *Id.* at 219 n.2. That footnote cannot bear the weight petitioners place on it.

*Tallentire* concerned an entirely different provision—§ 1333(a)(2)(A)'s choice-of-law rule for wrongful-death actions arising from a helicopter crash over the high seas, not a workers'-compensation claim under § 1333(b) at all. *Id.* at 209, 219. The Court itself acknowledged that § 1333(b) "has no bearing on this case." *Id.* at 219 n.2. A statement expressly disclaimed as irrelevant to the case at hand, unbriefed by the parties and unaccompanied by any analysis of § 1333(b)'s text, is the paradigm of unconsidered dictum. As this Court has cautioned, "general expressions, in every opinion, are to be taken in connection with the case in which those expressions are used . . . their possible bearing on all other cases is seldom completely investigated." *Humphrey's Executor v. United States*, 295 U.S. 602, 627 (1935) (quoting *Cohens v. Virginia*, 19 U.S. (6 Wheat.) 264, 399–400 (1821)). The *Tallentire* footnote, resolving a question never presented, is not entitled to the deference petitioners seek.

Even accepting the footnote's use of the word "situs," moreover, that word is naturally read to describe the situs of *operations*—the only geographic limitation the statutory text actually contains—not an independent situs-of-*injury* requirement invented from whole cloth.

### D. Congress's Remedial Purpose Confirms This Reading

This Court has recognized that the OCSLA was designed "to define a body of law applicable to the seabed, the subsoil, and the fixed structures . . . on the outer Continental Shelf" and to ensure "the orderly development of offshore resources." *Shell Oil Co. v. Iowa Dep't of Revenue*, 488 U.S. 19, 25 (1988) (quoting *United States v. Maine*, 420 U.S. 515, 527 (1975)). A workforce that performs the same essential Shelf-related tasks—unloading platforms, maintaining pipeline infrastructure, processing extracted resources—should not have its compensation coverage depend on the fortuity of exactly where a forklift accident occurs. Petitioners' situs-of-injury rule would arbitrarily deny coverage to workers whose injuries are unquestionably caused by Shelf operations merely because the harm manifests onshore rather than offshore, undermining the very purpose of extending Longshore Act protection to this workforce.

### CONCLUSION

The judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
