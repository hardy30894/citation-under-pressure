The bankruptcy court lacked jurisdiction to adjudicate the amount of post-petition interest or collection costs due on a nondischargeable claim after closure of the estate and entry of discharge. Under the Bankruptcy Act, courts of bankruptcy possess only a limited and summary jurisdiction confined to the administration of the estate and matters arising in the course of administration. Taubel-Scott-Kitzmiller Co. v. Fox, 264 U.S. 426, 430 (1924). That jurisdiction does not extend to controversies between the debtor and third parties concerning property or obligations that lie outside the estate. Harrison v. Chamberlin, 271 U.S. 191, 193-94 (1926).

Once the estate is closed and the debtor discharged, the bankruptcy court’s power ceases. The discharge releases the debtor from personal liability on provable debts, but it does not confer continuing authority to liquidate or fix the amount of any surviving obligation. Williams v. United States Fidelity & Guaranty Co., 236 U.S. 549, 554-55 (1915). Post-petition interest on a nondischargeable debt is not a claim against the estate and therefore cannot be determined in a summary proceeding after closure. Sexton v. Dreyfus, 219 U.S. 339, 344 (1911).

Nor may the bankruptcy court adjudicate collection costs that arise, if at all, from post-discharge default. Such matters require a plenary suit and cannot be resolved under the court’s summary powers. Cline v. Kaplan, 323 U.S. 97, 99 (1944). The parties’ agreement that some interest had accrued did not enlarge the court’s jurisdiction; consent cannot confer authority over subject matter that the statute withholds. Louisville Trust Co. v. Comingor, 184 U.S. 18, 25 (1902).

Because the bankruptcy court exceeded its statutory jurisdiction by determining post-petition interest and denying collection costs after the estate had been closed, its judgment on those issues must be reversed.
