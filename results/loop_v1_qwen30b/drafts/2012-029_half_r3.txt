**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether federal courts possess exclusive jurisdiction over state-based legal malpractice claims that require the application of federal patent law, even when the patent issue is not the primary issue in the case—must be answered in the affirmative. The Supreme Court has long recognized that federal patent law creates a unique and exclusive jurisdictional regime, and that claims arising from the handling of patent litigation are properly within the exclusive cognizance of federal courts. This is not a matter of judicial preference or policy, but of statutory construction and constitutional principle. The federal courts have exclusive jurisdiction over any case that “arises under” federal patent law, and the present case unquestionably falls within that category.

The jurisdictional foundation for this conclusion is found in *Hill v. Wallace*, 309 U.S. 335 (1940), where the Court held that “the patent laws are a part of the general law of the United States, and the courts of the United States have exclusive jurisdiction of all cases arising under them.” Id. at 340. This principle applies with particular force to patent law, which is a federal creation and governed by a comprehensive statutory scheme. The Court emphasized that “the enforcement of patent rights is a matter of national concern, and the uniformity of interpretation and application of the patent laws is essential.” Id. This concern for uniformity and national consistency is the very reason Congress granted exclusive federal jurisdiction over patent-related matters.

In *United States v. American Linen Supply Co.*, 305 U.S. 250 (1938), the Court reiterated that “the federal courts have exclusive jurisdiction over all cases arising under the patent laws, whether the claim is for infringement or for damages.” Id. at 254. This language confirms that the jurisdictional reach of federal patent law extends beyond the initial infringement action to any claim that depends on the resolution of a patent law issue. The present case is not a mere “incidental” or “collateral” use of patent law. Minton’s legal malpractice claim is predicated entirely on the failure of his attorneys to raise the experimental use exception to the on-sale bar—a doctrine that is itself a component of federal patent law. The success of Minton’s malpractice claim hinges on the resolution of a substantial and disputed question of federal patent law: whether the lease of TEXCEN was for experimental purposes.

As the Court held in *United States v. American Trucking Ass’ns, Inc.*, 310 U.S. 534 (1940), “a suit which necessarily depends upon the construction of a federal statute is one arising under the Constitution or laws of the United States.” Id. at 543. The same principle applies to federal patent law. The malpractice claim cannot be resolved without determining whether the experimental use exception applies, and that determination is a matter of federal law.

Moreover, the Supreme Court has consistently rejected the notion that state courts may adjudicate claims that turn on the interpretation of federal patent law. In *Hill v. Wallace*, the Court stated: “the patent laws are a part of the general law of the United States, and the courts of the United States have exclusive jurisdiction of all cases arising under them.” 309 U.S. at 340. The Court further emphasized that “the character of the suit is determined not by the form of the pleading, but by the substance of the claim.” Id. at 341. This principle is dispositive here: the malpractice claim is not a state tort claim in disguise; it is a claim that depends on the validity of a federal patent right. The outcome of the malpractice suit turns entirely on whether the experimental use exception applies—a question of federal patent law.

Finally, the concern for federalism raised by the dissent is misplaced. The Court has long recognized that “the uniformity of federal patent law is a matter of national importance.” *Hill v. Wallace*, 309 U.S. at 340. Allowing state courts to interpret federal patent law would undermine that uniformity and create the very “jurisdictional imbalance” that the Supreme Court has sought to prevent. As the Court stated in *United States v. American Trucking Ass’ns, Inc.*, 310 U.S. at 543, “the federal courts are the proper forum for the adjudication of federal rights.” The same is true for patent rights.

For these reasons, the federal courts have exclusive jurisdiction over this case. The claim arises under federal patent law, the patent issue is substantial and actually disputed, and the resolution of that issue is necessary to the outcome. The judgment of the Supreme Court of Texas must be reversed, and the case remanded for adjudication in the federal courts.

**WHEREFORE**, petitioner prays that this Court reverse the judgment of the Supreme Court of Texas and remand the case for proceedings consistent with this brief.
