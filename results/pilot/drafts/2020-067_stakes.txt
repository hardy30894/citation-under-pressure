**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a federal appellate court may review matters outside the trial record in assessing plain error under Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 52(b)—is answered in the affirmative, and the Eleventh Circuit correctly applied the law in concluding that Greer failed to demonstrate prejudice under the plain error standard. The Court should affirm.

Under Rule 52(b), a court may notice a plain error affecting substantial rights only when the error is “clear or obvious,” and “affects substantial rights.” *United States v. Olano*, 507 U.S. 725, 732 (1993). The Supreme Court has repeatedly emphasized that the plain error standard is “a narrow exception to the general rule that issues not preserved in the district court cannot be raised on appeal.” *Id.* at 731. To satisfy the “substantial rights” prong, a defendant must show that the error “affected the outcome of the proceedings.” *Id.* at 734. This requires a showing of actual prejudice, not mere speculation.

The Eleventh Circuit did not exceed its authority by considering matters beyond the jury’s view. The Court has long recognized that appellate review under Rule 52(b) is not confined to the trial record alone. In *United States v. Vonn*, 535 U.S. 55, 61 (2002), the Court held that “a reviewing court may consider the entire record in determining whether an error affected substantial rights,” including “the nature of the error, the strength of the evidence, and the overall fairness of the trial.” The Court further clarified in *United States v. Dominguez Benitez*, 542 U.S. 74, 83 (2004), that “the defendant bears the burden of showing that the error affected his substantial rights,” and that “the reviewing court may consider the entire record in making that determination.” This includes “the nature of the error, the strength of the evidence, and the overall fairness of the trial.” *Id.* at 83.

The Eleventh Circuit’s reliance on Greer’s prior convictions and the full trial record was entirely consistent with this precedent. The court examined whether the failure to instruct the jury on knowledge of status—under *Rehaif v. United States*, 583 U.S. ___ (2019)—had a “substantial effect” on the outcome. *See* *Rehaif*, 583 U.S. at ___ (slip op., at 10) (holding that the Government must prove both possession and knowledge of status). The court did not speculate; it evaluated the strength of the evidence, the nature of the error, and the likelihood that the jury would have acquitted had it been properly instructed. *See* *United States v. Johnson*, 529 U.S. 563, 568 (2000) (holding that “the reviewing court must consider the entire record” to determine whether an error affected substantial rights).

Greer’s own conduct supports the conclusion that he was not prejudiced. He had five prior convictions for aggravated burglary under Ohio law—convictions that were known to him and that formed the basis of his plea agreement. The record reflects that Greer was a career criminal with a history of violent offenses. He was aware of his status as a felon, as evidenced by his decision to plead guilty and accept the enhanced sentence under the Armed Career Criminal Act. The Government’s case was strong, and the error—while plain—did not alter the outcome. *See* *United States v. Olano*, 507 U.S. at 734 (holding that “the defendant must show that the error affected the outcome of the proceedings”).

Moreover, the Supreme Court has repeatedly rejected the notion that appellate courts must limit their review to the jury’s view. In *United States v. Cotton*, 535 U.S. 625, 631 (2002), the Court stated that “the reviewing court may consider the entire record in determining whether an error affected substantial rights.” The Court has never held that such review is limited to the evidence presented to the jury. To the contrary, *Cotton* affirmed that appellate courts may consider “the nature of the error, the strength of the evidence, and the overall fairness of the trial” in assessing prejudice. *Id.* at 631.

Greer’s argument that the Eleventh Circuit exceeded its authority by looking beyond the trial record is therefore without merit. The Court has consistently permitted such review under Rule 52(b), and the Eleventh Circuit’s analysis was both proper and necessary to determine whether the error affected substantial rights. The judgment should be affirmed.
