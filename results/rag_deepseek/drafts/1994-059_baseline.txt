ARGUMENT

The Constitution establishes the exclusive qualifications for membership in Congress, and states may not add to or alter those qualifications. The Qualifications Clauses of Article I—§ 2, cl. 2 for the House and § 3, cl. 3 for the Senate as modified by the Seventeenth Amendment—set forth the only requirements: age, citizenship, and inhabitancy. This Court has long recognized that these provisions are exclusive. *Powell v. McCormack*, 395 U.S. 486, 547-48 (1969) (holding that the Constitution’s “carefully defined” qualifications “were intended to be exclusive” and that Congress cannot add qualifications by statute). The same logic applies to the States: the Framers’ decision to enumerate specific qualifications implicitly excludes any power in the States to impose additional ones. *Id.* at 550.

Arkansas’s Amendment 73 does precisely what the Constitution forbids. By barring from the ballot any person who has served three terms in the House or two terms in the Senate, the Amendment creates a new disqualification based solely on prior service. This is a de facto qualification for office. The Amendment does not merely regulate the “Times, Places and Manner” of elections under Article I, § 4, cl. 1; it disqualifies candidates who would otherwise satisfy every constitutional requirement. The power to prescribe the “manner” of elections encompasses procedural regulations, not the authority to add substantive qualifications. *Oregon v. Mitchell*, 400 U.S. 112, 119-20 (1970) (plurality opinion) (observing that state power under § 4 is subject to Congress’s overriding authority and that the “responsibility of the States for setting the qualifications of voters in congressional elections” is “made subject to the power of Congress”); *Wiley v. Sinkler*, 179 U.S. 58, 64 (1900) (explaining that the right to vote for a member of Congress “does not depend on the Constitution of the United States” but that the Constitution “adopts the qualification furnished” by the States for state elections; it does not grant States power to add qualifications for federal office).

The distinction between a qualification and a ballot-access restriction is one without a difference. The Amendment’s Section 3 does not merely regulate the ballot; it conclusively bars candidates who meet constitutional standards from appearing on the ballot at all. That is a qualification. Indeed, the Arkansas Supreme Court correctly recognized that the Amendment “provides that any person having been elected to three or more terms . . . shall not be certified as a candidate and shall not be eligible to have his/her name placed on the ballot . . . .” This is a direct addition to the constitutional qualifications.

Allowing each State to impose its own qualifications would destroy the uniformity essential to a national legislature. This Court has emphasized that “the right to vote for a member of Congress” is “derived from the Constitution” and that the “States, in prescribing the qualifications of voters for the most numerous branch of their own legislatures, do not do this with reference to the election for members of Congress. Nor can they prescribe the qualification for voters for those eo nomine.” *Wiley v. Sinkler*, 179 U.S. at 64. Just as States cannot prescribe different voter qualifications for federal elections, they cannot prescribe different candidate qualifications. The Framers intended Congress to represent the nation as a whole; state-imposed term limits would splinter that representation into a patchwork of disparate rules, undermining the federal character of the government. As this Court noted in *Newberry v. United States*, 256 U.S. 232, 257 (1921), the Constitution vests in Congress the power to regulate its own elections, and the Seventeenth Amendment further ensures that Senators are “elected by the people” of each State without state-imposed barriers.

Finally, the States possess no reserved power to alter the qualifications for federal office. The Constitution is a grant of enumerated powers; the power to set qualifications for members of Congress is not among those delegated to the States, nor is it reserved under the Tenth Amendment. *Powell*, 395 U.S. at 548-49. The Arkansas Amendment is thus an unconstitutional intrusion on the exclusive federal qualifications regime.

For these reasons, the judgment of the Arkansas Supreme Court should be affirmed: Section 3 of Amendment 73 is void, and because its provisions are inseverable from the remainder of the amendment, the entire Amendment must fall.
