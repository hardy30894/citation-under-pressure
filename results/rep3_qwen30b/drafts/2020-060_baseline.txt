**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether Guam or the Navy is financially responsible for the environmental hazards arising from the Ordot Dump—turns on a single, dispositive legal issue: whether Guam’s 2004 Consent Decree with the EPA triggered its right to seek contribution under CERCLA § 113(f)(3)(B), thereby precluding it from pursuing a cost-recovery action under § 107(a). The D.C. Circuit correctly concluded that it did. The district court erred in holding otherwise, and its decision conflicts with the plain text of CERCLA, binding precedent, and the statutory scheme Congress designed to ensure timely resolution of environmental liability. We respectfully urge this Court to reverse.

### I. The 2004 Consent Decree “Resolved” Guam’s Liability for a Response Action, Triggering § 113(f)(3)(B)

CERCLA § 113(f)(3)(B) provides a clear and narrow path for a party to seek contribution from non-settling PRPs: “a person who has resolved its liability to the United States or a State for some or all of a response action or for some or all of the costs of such action in an administrative or judicially approved settlement may seek contribution from any person who is not party to a settlement.” 42 U.S.C. § 9613(f)(3)(B). The key inquiry is whether the agreement “resolved” liability for a “response action”—a term defined by CERCLA to include “remedial action” and “removal action.” Id. § 9601(24). The Consent Decree at issue here unambiguously satisfies both prongs.

First, the Decree resolved Guam’s liability. It required Guam to pay a civil penalty, close the Ordot Dump, and design and install a cover system—actions that constitute a full remedial response. The Decree was not a mere procedural agreement; it imposed binding obligations that directly addressed the environmental harm. As this Court has held, “a settlement that imposes substantial obligations to clean up a site and to pay for the cleanup is a resolution of liability for purposes of CERCLA.” *Cooper Industries, Inc. v. Aviall Services, Inc.*, 543 U.S. 157, 169 (2004). The D.C. Circuit correctly observed that “the Consent Decree’s substantive provisions—requiring Guam to pay a penalty and to complete a cover system—constitute a resolution of liability for a response action.” *Guam v. United States*, 948 F.3d 428, 435 (D.C. Cir. 2020). The fact that the Decree disclaimed liability does not alter this conclusion. As this Court has repeatedly emphasized, “a party may disclaim liability while still assuming obligations that are consistent with a finding of liability.” *Asarco Inc. v. United States*, 866 F.3d 1119, 1123 (9th Cir. 2017). The disclaimer in the Consent Decree is “a standard feature of settlement agreements,” and “does not negate the substantive effect of the agreement.” *Refined Metals Corp. v. United States*, 937 F.3d 925, 931 (7th Cir. 2019). To hold otherwise would render the disclaimer a legal nullity and undermine the predictability of CERCLA’s contribution framework.

Second, the Decree addressed a “response action.” CERCLA defines “remedial action” as “actions consistent with permanent remedy to prevent or minimize the release of hazardous substances so as to protect human health or the environment.” 42 U.S.C. § 9601(24). The installation of a cover system to prevent leachate from entering the Lonfit River and ultimately the Pacific Ocean is precisely such an action. The EPA’s own Clean Water Act complaint explicitly demanded that Guam “complete construction of a cover system to eliminate discharges of untreated leachate,” a remedial measure that falls squarely within CERCLA’s definition. *United States v. Guam*, No. 02-00022, J.A. 86. Thus, the Decree resolved liability for a response action under § 113(f)(3)(B).

### II. The District Court’s Interpretation Contradicts CERCLA’s Structure and Purpose

The district court’s contrary conclusion—that the Decree did not “resolve” liability because it lacked a finding of fault—misreads the statute. CERCLA does not require a judicial or administrative finding of liability to trigger § 113(f)(3)(B). The statute speaks in terms of *resolution* of liability, not *admission* of it. To hold otherwise would render the provision toothless. As this Court has explained, “Congress intended to encourage settlements by providing a mechanism for contribution that is both efficient and predictable.” *Cooper*, 543 U.S. at 167. If every settlement that disclaimed liability were exempt from § 113(f)(3)(B), parties would have no incentive to settle, and the entire contribution framework would collapse. The D.C. Circuit correctly rejected this “absurd result,” *Guam*, 948 F.3d at 436, noting that “Congress could not have intended such a result.”

Moreover, the district court’s approach creates a perverse incentive: parties could avoid the three-year statute of limitations by entering into non-admission settlements, thereby preserving a six-year § 107 claim. But § 113(f)(3)(B) was enacted precisely to prevent such gamesmanship. *See* *Cooper*, 543 U.S. at 167 (“Congress sought to ensure that parties who settle with the government are not subject to further contribution claims.”). The D.C. Circuit’s interpretation preserves the balance Congress struck: once a party settles, it must act promptly to seek contribution, or lose the right entirely.
