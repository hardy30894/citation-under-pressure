**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Presumption Favoring Scienter Requires §875(c) to Be Construed to Demand Proof of Subjective Intent to Threaten.**

Section 875(c) does not specify the mental state required for conviction, but that silence cannot be read to dispense with intent altogether. This Court has explained that the requirement of a guilty mind "is no provincial or transient notion. It is as universal and persistent in mature systems of law as belief in freedom of the human will and a consequent ability and duty of the normal individual to choose between good and evil." *Morissette v. United States*, 342 U.S. 246, 250 (1952). Where Congress enacts a criminal statute without addressing mens rea, courts should ordinarily infer that the traditional intent requirement survives rather than assume it has been abolished by omission. *Id.* at 263.

That presumption applies with special force here because §875(c) is not the kind of regulatory or "public welfare" offense in which this Court has permitted strict liability. *Compare United States v. Balint*, 258 U.S. 250, 251–252 (1922) (upholding strict liability for sales of narcotics under a regulatory scheme). A prosecution for transmitting a "threat" is a true crime, carrying felony punishment and grave stigma — precisely the context in which this Court has insisted that intent be proven rather than presumed. *Morissette*, 342 U.S. at 260. Nor should the statute be stretched by inference to reach conduct Congress did not clearly intend to criminalize; this Court has cautioned that criminal statutes should not be extended "by strained construction" and that any doubt as to their scope should be resolved against turning that ambiguity into a trap for the unwary speaker. *United States v. Cardiff*, 344 U.S. 174, 176 (1952); *Boyce Motor Lines v. United States*, 342 U.S. 337, 340 (1952). Where, as here, an objective standard would punish defendants for the failure to foresee how listeners might interpret their words, rather than for any intended wrongdoing, the statute should instead be read to require proof that the defendant himself meant his words as a threat.

**II. The First Amendment's True-Threats Doctrine Requires Proof of the Speaker's Intent, Not Merely a Listener's Reaction.**

Even apart from ordinary rules of statutory construction, the Constitution independently compels a subjective-intent standard. Only certain narrowly defined categories of speech fall outside the First Amendment's protection, and this Court has cautioned that such categories must be defined with precision because they constitute "no essential part of any exposition of ideas." *Chaplinsky v. New Hampshire*, 315 U.S. 568, 571–572 (1942). Applying the true-threat concept in *Watts v. United States*, 394 U.S. 705 (1969) (per curiam), this Court refused to treat as a punishable threat a statement that could plausibly have alarmed a listener, holding instead that "we do not believe that the kind of political hyperbole indulged in by [the] petitioner fits within that statutory term." *Id.* at 708. *Watts* thus confirms that the inquiry cannot turn solely on how a reasonable listener might construe ambiguous words; it must account for the character of the speech and what the speaker actually meant to convey.

An objective standard risks chilling vast amounts of protected expression — hyperbole, black humor, artistic expression such as rap lyrics, or venting born of anger or distress — because speakers cannot reliably predict how an unknown audience will receive ambiguous language. This Court has long recognized that the First Amendment requires "breathing space" for such expression, and that statutes touching speech must not be construed to reach protected activity simply because some listeners might take alarm. *NAACP v. Button*, 371 U.S. 415, 433 (1963). Just as this Court held that a scienter requirement is constitutionally necessary to prevent booksellers from being held strictly liable for the content of material they had no intent to distribute unlawfully, *Smith v. California*, 361 U.S. 147, 154 (1959), so too must speakers be protected from liability turning solely on how their words are received rather than what they intended. Absent a scienter requirement, individuals of ordinary prudence will "steer far wider of the unlawful zone" than the Constitution demands, curtailing protected speech out of an abundance of caution. *Speiser v. Randall*, 357 U.S. 513, 526 (1958). And where advocacy or expression is at stake, this Court has insisted on proof that the speech was intended to produce the proscribed result before liability may attach. *Dennis v. United States*, 341 U.S. 494, 502 (1951).

**III. An Objective Standard Punishes Carelessness, Not Wrongdoing, and Cannot Be Reconciled With the Constitution's Demand for Fair Notice.**

Dispensing with subjective intent effectively transforms §875(c) into a negligence offense, punishing defendants not for what they meant, but for failing to anticipate how their words might be perceived. This Court has repeatedly cautioned against construing ambiguous criminal statutes to impose liability for such unwitting conduct, particularly where a defendant's fair notice of the legal consequences of his own words is at stake. *Screws v. United States*, 325 U.S. 91, 101–102 (1945) (plurality opinion); *Boyce Motor Lines*, 342 U.S. at 340. Both the statute and the Constitution require more. The judgment below should be reversed, and this Court should hold that a conviction under §875(c) requires proof that the defendant subjectively intended his communication to be understood as a threat.
