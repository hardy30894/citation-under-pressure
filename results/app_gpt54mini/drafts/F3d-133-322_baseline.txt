**Argument**

The convictions under 18 U.S.C. § 201(b)(2)(C) cannot stand because the Government proved, at most, unauthorized misconduct by an SSA clerk, not bribery of a “public official” acting “in relation to any official act.” 18 U.S.C. § 201(b)(2), (a)(3). The statute reaches only decisions or actions on a “question, matter, cause, suit, proceeding or controversy” pending before the official in an “official capacity.” 18 U.S.C. § 201(a)(3). Although Congress used broad language, the Supreme Court has made clear that bribery liability still turns on an identifiable official duty or action, not mere access to government processes. United States v. Birdsall, 233 U.S. 223, 230-31 (1914).

Mrs. Parker’s job was clerical. She assisted ALJ Aronson before and during hearings, recorded notes, typed and mailed decisions, and had access to the office computer system. She was not authorized to approve benefits or sign the ALJ’s name. On those facts, the indictment and proof failed to establish that the payments were made to influence any act within her official duty. A defendant cannot be convicted under § 201 by stretching “official act” beyond the limits of the actor’s assigned governmental responsibilities. See United States v. Gjieli, 717 F.2d 968, 972 (6th Cir. 1983); United States v. Birdsall, 233 U.S. at 230-31.

Birdsall confirms the point. There, the Court held that bribery statutes cover acts governed by settled administrative practice, but only when those acts are part of the official’s duty. 233 U.S. at 231. The Court explained that “duties not completely defined by written rules are established by settled practice,” and acts performed in that course fall within bribery laws. Id. That rationale does not help the Government here, because there was no settled practice authorizing Mrs. Parker to adjudicate SSI claims, reinstate benefits, or forge judicial signatures. Her admitted conduct was fraudulent, but fraud is not synonymous with bribery under § 201. The statute requires proof of corrupt intent to influence an official act, not merely proof that the defendant abused access to government files. See 18 U.S.C. § 201(b)(2)(C); Birdsall, 233 U.S. at 230-31.

The Sixth Circuit’s decision in Gjieli likewise demonstrates the statute’s limits. There, the court rejected an argument that bribery liability depended only on the actor’s formal authority, but it did not dispense with the requirement that the conduct be tied to a governmental function. 717 F.2d at 972. Even under Gjieli’s broader view, the Government still must identify an official action or omission that falls within the government actor’s role. Id. Here, the Government’s theory collapses that distinction by treating Mrs. Parker’s unauthorized clerical manipulation as if it were an official adjudicatory act. It was not.

Nor did the Government prove a valid conspiracy to commit public bribery as to Ralph Parker. Conspiracy under 18 U.S.C. § 371 requires proof of an agreement to commit an offense against the United States and an overt act in furtherance of that agreement. United States v. Feola, 420 U.S. 671, 694 (1975). Because the underlying bribery theory failed, the conspiracy conviction must fail as well. See id.; see also United States v. Jimenez Recio, 537 U.S. 270, 274 (2003). Ralph Parker’s conduct, as reflected in the record, was at most participation in Mrs. Parker’s fraudulent extortion scheme. Without a legally sufficient bribery offense, there can be no conspiracy to commit that offense. Feola, 420 U.S. at 694.

The Government’s proof also cannot salvage the convictions by pointing to Mrs. Parker’s admissions that she changed names and dates on letters and forged ALJ Aronson’s signature. Those admissions establish forgery and deceit, but they do not establish bribery under § 201(b)(2)(C) unless the bribe was intended to influence an official act attributable to the charged public official. United States v. Sun-Diamond Growers of California, 526 U.S. 398, 404-08 (1999). Sun-Diamond emphasized that § 201 requires a link between the thing of value and a specific official act, not a generalized expectation of favorable treatment. Id. at 404-05. The Government’s proof here is even weaker: it showed payments made to secure unlawful manipulation of records by a clerk acting outside any lawful authority.

At minimum, the indictment should have been dismissed because it charged conduct outside the statute’s reach. An indictment must allege each essential element of the offense so that the defendant is informed of the charge and protected against conviction for uncharged conduct. Hamling v. United States, 418 U.S. 87, 117-18 (1974). Here, the indictment’s theory depended on treating Mrs. Parker as a “public official” whose clerical tasks became bribeable “official acts” merely because she worked for the SSA. That theory is inconsistent with the text of § 201 and the Supreme Court’s instruction in Birdsall and Sun-Diamond that bribery liability must remain tied to an identifiable official function. Birdsall, 233 U.S. at 230-31; Sun-Diamond, 526 U.S. at 404-08.

For these reasons, Mrs. Parker’s and Mr. Parker’s convictions under 18 U.S.C. §§ 201(b)(2)(C) and 371 should be reversed.
