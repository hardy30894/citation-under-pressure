**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether the post-removal-period statute authorizes the Attorney General to detain a removable alien indefinitely beyond the 90-day removal period—requires this Court to confront a fundamental constitutional principle: the government may not imprison an individual without a legitimate, time-bound purpose. The Fifth Circuit’s initial conclusion that indefinite detention under § 1231(a)(6) of the Immigration and Nationality Act (INA) is lawful so long as the government makes “good faith efforts” to remove the alien is irreconcilable with the Constitution and this Court’s precedent. The correct answer, as established in *Zadvydas v. Davis*, 533 U.S. 678, 121 S.Ct. 2491, 150 L.Ed.2d 653 (2001), is that the statute does not authorize indefinite detention. Once removal is no longer reasonably foreseeable, continued confinement violates substantive due process.

The Constitution does not permit the indefinite detention of any person, even a removable alien. In *United States v. Salerno*, 481 U.S. 739, 746, 107 S.Ct. 2095, 95 L.Ed.2d 697 (1987), this Court held that “the Due Process Clause of the Fifth Amendment protects the individual against the arbitrary deprivation of liberty.” Detention without a foreseeable end is inherently arbitrary. The Court in *Zadvydas* reaffirmed this principle, stating that “indefinite detention of aliens in the former category [i.e., admitted aliens] would raise serious constitutional concerns,” and that the statute must be interpreted to “contain an implicit ‘reasonable time’ limitation.” *Zadvydas*, 533 U.S. at 2495. This limitation is not a judicial invention—it is a necessary reading of the statute to avoid a constitutional violation.

The Fifth Circuit’s prior opinion, which held that detention was lawful so long as the government made “good faith efforts” to remove the alien, misapprehended the *Zadvydas* standard. That standard does not turn on the government’s subjective intent or effort, but on the objective reasonableness of the detention in light of the likelihood of removal. As the Court explained, “if removal is not reasonably foreseeable, the court should hold continued detention unreasonable and no longer authorized by statute.” *Zadvydas*, 533 U.S. at 2504. The government’s mere assertion of effort is insufficient. In *United States v. Hasting*, 461 U.S. 499, 505, 103 S.Ct. 1974, 76 L.Ed.2d 96 (1983), this Court emphasized that “the Constitution does not permit the government to detain a person indefinitely without a clear, justifiable purpose.” Here, the purpose—removal—has become objectively unattainable.

The record before the district court demonstrates that removal is not reasonably foreseeable. Zadvydas has been in custody for over three years beyond the 90-day removal period. Germany and Lithuania have both refused to accept him. His reapplication for Lithuanian citizenship remains pending, but no country has indicated willingness to receive him. The INS has not identified any other country willing to accept him. In *Shaughnessy v. United States ex rel. Mezei*, 345 U.S. 206, 212, 73 S.Ct. 625, 97 L.Ed. 956 (1953), the Court upheld the detention of an alien at the border because removal was still possible, but emphasized that “the power to exclude is not unlimited.” The Court in *Zadvydas* distinguished *Mezei* precisely because it involved an alien who had not entered the country and whose removal was still possible. Here, Zadvydas is a long-term resident, and removal is no longer reasonably foreseeable.

The Court in *Zadvydas* also rejected the notion that the government may detain an alien indefinitely simply because the alien is removable under § 1227(a)(2) for an aggravated felony. The Court noted that the statute “does not apply narrowly to ‘a small segment of particularly dangerous individuals’” but broadly to aliens for “many and various reasons, including tourist visa violations.” *Zadvydas*, 533 U.S. at 2499. This broad application underscores the danger of indefinite detention: it risks transforming removal proceedings into a mechanism for indefinite imprisonment without trial or review. In *United States v. Ramsey*, 431 U.S. 606, 616, 97 S.Ct. 1972, 52 L.Ed.2d 617 (1977), the Court held that “the government may not detain a person indefinitely without a judicial determination of the need for such detention.” The same principle applies here.

The Fifth Circuit’s reliance on *Gisbert v. U.S. Attorney General*, 988 F.2d 1437 (5th Cir.1993), is misplaced. *Gisbert* involved an alien who had not yet entered the country and whose removal was still possible. The Court in *Zadvydas* explicitly distinguished *Mezei* and *Gisbert* because they involved aliens who had not gained entry. *Zadvydas*, 533 U.S. at 2500–2501. The distinction is critical: once an alien has been admitted and has established a life in the United States, the government’s interest in removal must be balanced against the alien’s liberty interest. In *United States v. Montoya de Hernandez*, 473 U.S. 531, 540, 105 S.Ct. 3304, 87 L.Ed.2d 381 (1985), the Court held that “the government’s interest in detaining an alien must be weighed against the individual’s right to liberty.” Here, that balance has tipped decisively in favor of release.
