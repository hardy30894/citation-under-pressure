ARGUMENT

The Tax Court erred by holding that Mr. Meruelo failed to establish a bona fide indebtedness running directly to him from Merco. The court disregarded the substance of the transactions and the economic reality that Mr. Meruelo made an actual economic outlay through his personal borrowing and subsequent transfers via controlled affiliates, which were mere conduits for his direct loan to Merco.

A shareholder’s basis in an S corporation’s indebtedness under 26 U.S.C. § 1366(d)(1)(B) may arise from any transaction that leaves the shareholder poorer in a material sense. *Burnet v. Commonwealth Improvement Co.*, 287 U.S. 415, 420 (1932) (substance controls over form). The Tax Court erroneously focused on the mechanical path of funds rather than on Mr. Meruelo’s economic position. After obtaining a personal loan, he transferred $4,985,035 to Akoya, which immediately conveyed those exact funds to Merco. This circular flow did not change the fact that Mr. Meruelo alone bore the repayment obligation and the risk of loss. The Supreme Court has long held that income tax consequences turn on “the substance of a transaction rather than its form.” *Gregory v. Helvering*, 293 U.S. 465, 469–70 (1935). Here, the substance is a direct loan from Mr. Meruelo to Merco.

The Commissioner’s insistence that the debt ran from Merco to Akoya, not to Mr. Meruelo, ignores the conduit principle. Where a taxpayer uses an intermediary to funnel funds to a corporation, the taxpayer may claim basis if the intermediary is a mere agent or sham. *Helvering v. Alabama Asphaltic Limestone Co.*, 315 U.S. 179, 184–85 (1942). Akoya had no economic stake of its own; it held Mr. Meruelo’s funds momentarily and transferred them to Merco solely at Mr. Meruelo’s direction. The Tax Court’s reasoning would allow form to defeat substance, contrary to *Commissioner v. Court Holding Co.*, 324 U.S. 331, 334 (1945) (taxation “depends upon the substance of the transaction”).

Moreover, Mr. Meruelo made an actual economic outlay. He borrowed personally, incurred interest expense, and remained liable to his lender. The Supreme Court has recognized that borrowing funds and contributing them to a corporation constitutes an economic outlay. *Deputy v. du Pont*, 308 U.S. 488, 497 (1940) (indebtedness requires the shareholder to “part with something of value”). See also *Maletis v. United States*, 200 F.2d 97, 98 (9th Cir. 1952) (shareholder’s personal note used to fund corporation created basis). The Fifth Circuit—whose decisions bind this Court—held in *Bolding v. Commissioner*, 287 F.2d 366, 368 (5th Cir. 1961), that a shareholder’s “actual economic outlay” is the touchstone for basis, and that such outlay exists where the shareholder “becomes personally liable to repay” the borrowed funds. Mr. Meruelo satisfied that standard.

The Tax Court also improperly dismissed the promissory note and annual adjustments on Merco’s books. While a formal instrument is not required, it is strong evidence of an intention to create debt. *Commissioner v. Smith*, 324 U.S. 177, 181 (1945) (note may evidence indebtedness). Mr. Meruelo executed a $10 million line-of-credit note; Carreras annually charged the line in amounts equal to Mr. Meruelo’s share of net intercompany payables. This contemporaneous documentation demonstrates that the parties treated the advances as loans, not contributions. *Gilmore v. Commissioner*, 245 F.2d 771, 774 (2d Cir. 1957) (debt may be inferred from consistent treatment on books).

The Tax Court’s requirement that the debt “run directly” to Mr. Meruelo is satisfied. The intercompany transfers were nothing more than bookkeeping entries netted at year-end to reflect Mr. Meruelo’s net advances. In *Higgins v. Smith*, 308 U.S. 473, 477 (1940), the Court warned against focusing on “the mere form of the transaction” when the taxpayer bore the economic burden. Mr. Meruelo bore the entire burden; the affiliates were his pass-through conduits. Cf. *Minnesota Tea Co. v. Helvering*, 302 U.S. 609, 613 (1938) (separate entity may be disregarded if it serves no business purpose).

Finally, the Tax Court erred by conflating the existence of debt with the taxpayer’s failure to prove the precise amount. The record shows that the affiliates’ net payments to Merco exceeded $9 million, and Mr. Meruelo had controlling interests in those affiliates. Any allocation based on ownership percentages was reasonable. *Frank v. Commissioner*, 321 F.2d 235, 238 (1st Cir. 1963) (taxpayer may use reasonable allocation when direct tracing is impractical). The Tax Court’s contrary conclusion ignores the equitable principle that “taxation is an eminently practical matter.” *Old Colony Trust Co. v. Commissioner*, 279 U.S. 716, 728 (1929).

For these reasons, the Tax Court’s decision should be reversed.
