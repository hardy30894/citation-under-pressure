**ARGUMENT**

I. The Double Jeopardy Clause bars retrial of a prior conviction allegation in a noncapital sentencing proceeding because such proceedings share the hallmarks of a trial on guilt or innocence.

The Double Jeopardy Clause of the Fifth Amendment, made applicable to the states through the Fourteenth Amendment, provides that no person shall “be subject for the same offence to be twice put in jeopardy of life or limb.” U.S. Const. amend. V. The Supreme Court has long recognized that the Clause protects against multiple punishments for the same offense. *Ex parte Lange*, 85 U.S. 163, 169 (1873). In *Stroud v. United States*, 251 U.S. 15 (1919), the Court held that retrial on a prior conviction allegation violated the Double Jeopardy Clause where the first trial had resulted in a finding that the prior conviction did not exist. *Id.* at 18. The Court reasoned that the prior conviction allegation was an “offense” within the meaning of the Clause, and that the acquittal on that allegation barred retrial. *Id.*

The Court has since refined its double jeopardy jurisprudence to focus on whether the proceeding at issue resembles a trial on guilt or innocence. In *Green v. United States*, 355 U.S. 184 (1957), the Court held that retrial on a greater offense was barred where the first trial had resulted in a conviction on a lesser included offense. *Id.* at 190. The Court explained that the Double Jeopardy Clause protects against multiple trials for the same offense, and that the acquittal on the greater offense in the first trial barred retrial. *Id.* at 187-88. Similarly, in *Price v. Georgia*, 398 U.S. 323 (1970), the Court held that retrial on a murder charge was barred where the first trial had resulted in a conviction on a lesser included offense of voluntary manslaughter. *Id.* at 329. The Court reasoned that the Double Jeopardy Clause protects against multiple trials for the same offense, and that the acquittal on the murder charge in the first trial barred retrial. *Id.* at 326-27.

II. The Supreme Court’s decision in *Bullington v. Missouri*, 451 U.S. 430 (1981), confirms that the Double Jeopardy Clause applies to sentencing proceedings that resemble trials on guilt or innocence.

In *Bullington*, the Supreme Court held that the Double Jeopardy Clause barred retrial on a capital sentencing proceeding where the first proceeding had resulted in a life imprisonment sentence rather than a death sentence. *Id.* at 446. The Court reasoned that the capital sentencing proceeding resembled a trial on guilt or innocence because it involved a jury, proof beyond a reasonable doubt, and a finding of specific aggravating factors. *Id.* at 438-39. The Court explained that the Double Jeopardy Clause protects against multiple trials for the same offense, and that the acquittal on the death penalty in the first proceeding barred retrial. *Id.* at 446.

The Court’s reasoning in *Bullington* applies with equal force to noncapital sentencing proceedings that involve a jury, proof beyond a reasonable doubt, and a finding of specific sentencing factors. *See also Rumsey v. Maryland*, 467 U.S. 216 (1984) (applying *Bullington* to bar retrial on a capital sentencing proceeding where the first proceeding had resulted in a life imprisonment sentence rather than a death sentence). The Court has recognized that the Double Jeopardy Clause protects against multiple trials for the same offense, and that the Clause applies to proceedings that resemble trials on guilt or innocence. *See, e.g., United States v. DiFrancesco*, 449 U.S. 117 (1980) (holding that the Double Jeopardy Clause does not bar retrial on a sentence where the first sentence had been vacated on appeal).

III. The California Supreme Court’s decision in *Monge v. California*, 18 Cal.4th 724 (1998), conflicts with the Supreme Court’s double jeopardy jurisprudence and should be reversed.

The California Supreme Court held that the Double Jeopardy Clause does not apply to noncapital sentencing proceedings. *Monge*, 18 Cal.4th at 732. This holding conflicts with the Supreme Court’s decisions in *Stroud*, *Green*, *Price*, *Bullington*, and *Rumsey*, which establish that the Double Jeopardy Clause applies to proceedings that resemble trials on guilt or innocence. The California Supreme Court’s decision also conflicts with the Supreme Court’s recognition that the Double Jeopardy Clause protects against multiple trials for the same offense. *See, e.g., Ex parte Lange*, 85 U.S. at 169; *Stroud*, 251 U.S. at 18.

The California Supreme Court’s decision is also inconsistent with the Supreme Court’s recognition that the Double Jeopardy Clause applies to sentencing proceedings that involve a jury, proof beyond a reasonable doubt, and a finding of specific sentencing factors. *See Bullington*, 451 U.S. at 438-39; *Rumsey*, 467 U.S. at 216. The prior conviction allegation in this case was tried to the court, with proof beyond a reasonable doubt, and resulted in a finding that the prior conviction did not exist. Under the Supreme Court’s double jeopardy jurisprudence, this finding bars retrial of the prior conviction allegation.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the Double Jeopardy Clause bars retrial of the prior conviction allegation in this case. The judgment of the California Supreme Court should be reversed.
