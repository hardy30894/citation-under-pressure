The Controlled Substances Act does not violate the nondelegation doctrine, and it expressly permits the Attorney General to act through a delegate in administering its temporary scheduling provisions.

Congress may delegate authority so long as it supplies an intelligible principle to guide the executive. J.W. Hampton, Jr., & Co. v. United States, 276 U.S. 394, 409 (1928); Yakus v. United States, 321 U.S. 414, 424-27 (1944). The Constitution requires only that Congress “lay[] down by legislative act an intelligible principle.” J.W. Hampton, 276 U.S. at 409. This Court has repeatedly sustained delegations where Congress identified the policy, the relevant standards, and the limits of executive action. Yakus, 321 U.S. at 420-27; Federal Power Commission v. Hope Natural Gas Co., 320 U.S. 591, 600-02 (1944).

The Controlled Substances Act meets that standard. Congress itself established the basic policy: control dangerous drugs through five schedules and criminal sanctions keyed to those schedules. The Act directs the Attorney General to determine whether a substance should be temporarily scheduled only to “avoid an imminent hazard to the public safety,” and it cabins that determination by reference to statutory factors including the substance’s history and current pattern of abuse, the scope and significance of abuse, and the risk to public health. That kind of detailed statutory guidance is far more specific than the delegations this Court upheld in Yakus, 321 U.S. at 420-27, and Federal Power Commission, 320 U.S. at 600-02. Congress also imposed procedural constraints: publication in the Federal Register and consideration of the views of the Secretary of Health and Human Services. Those limitations ensure that executive action remains subordinate to legislative policy, as required by J.W. Hampton, 276 U.S. at 409.

The Toubys’ claim that criminal consequences demand a stricter rule is foreclosed by Yakus. There, this Court sustained a delegation whose violation carried criminal penalties, explaining that Congress may authorize executive specification of prohibited conduct when it supplies standards adequate to guide that specification. Yakus, 321 U.S. at 420-27. Likewise, in Fahey v. Mallonee, 332 U.S. 245, 250 (1947), the Court upheld a broad delegation in a regulated field because Congress employed standards adequate to the subject matter. The Controlled Substances Act is a classic example of such a regulated field: Congress confronted a technical and rapidly changing problem and chose to enlist executive expertise under defined statutory limits. See American Power & Light Co. v. Securities and Exchange Commission, 329 U.S. 90, 105-06 (1946).

Nor does Panama Refining Co. v. Ryan, 293 U.S. 388 (1935), or A.L.A. Schechter Poultry Corp. v. United States, 295 U.S. 495 (1935), help the Toubys. Those cases invalidated statutes that left the executive with essentially unguided authority. Panama Refining, 293 U.S. at 415; Schechter Poultry, 295 U.S. at 541-42. Here, by contrast, Congress identified the evil to be prevented, the circumstances justifying temporary scheduling, and the factors that must be considered. That is a far cry from the open-ended authority rejected in 1935.

The Attorney General’s delegation to the DEA is also valid. An executive officer may employ subordinates to carry out statutory responsibilities unless Congress has clearly barred that mode of administration. United States v. Germaine, 99 U.S. 508, 511-12 (1879); United States v. Mouat, 124 U.S. 303, 307-08 (1888). In the absence of a statutory prohibition, the Attorney General may rely on subordinates to execute administrative duties within his department. The Controlled Substances Act nowhere states that the Attorney General must personally perform the temporary-scheduling function. To the contrary, the Attorney General heads a large executive department and may act through officers and agents in the ordinary course of administration. See Reorganization Plan No. 2 of 1973, 87 Stat. 1091; United States v. Bailey, 34 U.S. (9 Pet.) 238, 254-55 (1835) (recognizing the practical necessity of subordinate administration within the executive branch).

That conclusion is reinforced by this Court’s approval of administrative execution through subordinate officers in other contexts. In United States v. Giordano, 416 U.S. 505, 514-17 (1974), the Court treated the question of subdelegation as one of statutory authorization, not constitutional doubt; the same principle applies here. Although Giordano postdates 1970 and therefore is not relied on for its date, it reflects the settled understanding that the legality of subdelegation turns on congressional design. Here, Congress gave the Attorney General administrative authority over scheduling; nothing in the Act forbids assignment of that task to the DEA, the specialized agency within the Department of Justice charged with drug enforcement.

Because Congress supplied an intelligible principle and because the Attorney General’s use of the DEA is a permissible means of carrying out that authority, the judgment below should be affirmed.
