The judgment should be affirmed. The Sixth Amendment guarantees a jury trial, but it has never required a state to submit every subsidiary sentencing judgment to a jury, nor to require unanimity absent a constitutional command. The Eighth Amendment likewise does not freeze capital sentencing procedures in the precise form petitioner urges.

At the outset, the jury-trial guarantee protects “the truth of the fact” to be determined by a jury, not every matter that may influence punishment. *Patton v. United States*, 281 U.S. 276, 312 (1930). The Court has long recognized that the constitutional role of the jury is defined by the historical division between judge and jury, and that not every fact bearing on punishment is an element of the offense. *Sparf v. United States*, 156 U.S. 51, 103-04 (1895); *Maxwell v. Dow*, 176 U.S. 581, 602-03 (1900); *Jordan v. Massachusetts*, 225 U.S. 167, 176-77 (1912); *Rogers v. Peck*, 199 U.S. 425, 434-35 (1905). The Constitution thus permits states substantial latitude in structuring capital sentencing so long as the jury’s historic factfinding function is preserved. *Patton*, 281 U.S. at 312; *Duncan v. Louisiana*, 391 U.S. 145, 155-56 (1968).

Florida’s scheme did not eliminate the jury from the capital process. The jury heard the evidence, made a recommendation, and the trial judge imposed sentence under governing statutory standards. That procedure is consistent with the longstanding understanding that sentencing is ordinarily for the court. *Williams v. New York*, 337 U.S. 241, 246-47 (1949); *Ex parte United States*, 242 U.S. 27, 42-43 (1916). Nothing in the pre-1970 cases requires unanimity for a capital recommendation, much less forbids a state from assigning the ultimate sentencing decision to the judge after receiving the jury’s verdict and recommendation. *Patton*, 281 U.S. at 312; *Maxwell*, 176 U.S. at 602-03.

Nor does the Eighth Amendment compel the specific procedures petitioner demands. The Court has repeatedly held that the Cruel and Unusual Punishments Clause restrains barbaric punishments, not every variation in capital procedure. *In re Kemmler*, 136 U.S. 436, 447 (1890); *Weems v. United States*, 217 U.S. 349, 378 (1910). The Clause condemns punishments that are excessive or grossly disproportionate in light of “evolving standards,” but it does not constitutionalize a unanimity rule for capital sentencing. *Weems*, 217 U.S. at 378; *Trop v. Dulles*, 356 U.S. 86, 100-01 (1958). The pre-1970 cases petitioner can invoke confirm only that punishment must not be cruel in kind or excessive in relation to the offense; they do not mandate a particular jury-vote threshold. *O’Neil v. Vermont*, 144 U.S. 323, 339-40 (1892); *Robinson v. California*, 370 U.S. 660, 667 (1962).

Petitioner’s mental-retardation theory fares no better. The relevant pre-1970 authorities recognize that states may define and administer criminal incapacity and mitigation rules through legislative standards and judicial process. *Leland v. Oregon*, 343 U.S. 790, 798-99 (1952); *Davis v. United States*, 160 U.S. 469, 488-89 (1895). Those decisions underscore that the Constitution does not itself prescribe a single method for resolving mental condition issues in criminal cases. *Davis*, 160 U.S. at 488-89; *Leland*, 343 U.S. at 798-99. Florida allowed petitioner to present mental-health evidence in mitigation, and that is all the Constitution required before 1970. *Williams*, 337 U.S. at 246-47.

Finally, even where the Court has recognized constitutional limits on capital punishment, it has done so cautiously and by reference to settled historical categories, not by creating a unanimity requirement for death recommendations or a jury-determination rule for every exempting circumstance. *Trop*, 356 U.S. at 99-101; *Weems*, 217 U.S. at 373-78; *In re Kemmler*, 136 U.S. at 447. The Florida Supreme Court therefore properly upheld petitioner’s sentence under existing constitutional doctrine. The judgment should be affirmed.
