## ARGUMENT

### The district court committed plain error by allowing Agent Jones to give deceptive-profile testimony and direct credibility opinions.

Because Stanley did not contemporaneously object, review is for plain error. *United States v. Olano*, 507 U.S. 725, 732–36 (1993); *United States v. Gonzalez-Huerta*, 403 F.3d 727, 732 (10th Cir. 2005) (en banc). Under that standard, Stanley must show error that is plain and affects substantial rights, and this Court should correct the error if it “seriously affects the fairness, integrity or public reputation of judicial proceedings.” *Olano*, 507 U.S. at 736; *United States v. Domingo-Gomez*, 238 F.3d 726, 728 (5th Cir. 2001).

Rule 702 permits expert testimony only if it will “help the trier of fact to understand the evidence or to determine a fact in issue.” Fed. R. Evid. 702(a). But “expert testimony [that] does nothing more than vouch for the credibility of another witness encroaches upon the jury’s vital and exclusive function to make credibility determinations.” *United States v. Toledo*, 985 F.2d 1462, 1470 (10th Cir. 1993). This Court has repeatedly held that an expert may not testify that a defendant or witness is lying, truthful, deceptive, or otherwise credible. *United States v. Charley*, 189 F.3d 1251, 1267–68 (10th Cir. 1999); *United States v. Adams*, 271 F.3d 1236, 1245–46 (10th Cir. 2001); *United States v. Hall*, 653 F.2d 1002, 1006–07 (5th Cir. Unit A Aug. 1981).

Agent Jones did exactly what these cases forbid. He told the jury that he had training in “special tactics and ways to identify [] deception in statements and truths in statements,” then opined that many of Stanley’s answers were “not worthy of credence” and “did not make sense.” He testified that Stanley’s behaviors were “common among the criminal element to keep law enforcement at bay.” Most starkly, when Stanley said he would rather die than face charges, Jones said, “Never in my career have I seen that with an innocent person.” And when Stanley invoked God to support his truthfulness, Jones testified that such statements were “deceptive statements.” That is not permissible expert analysis; it is a direct credibility assessment dressed in the garb of expertise. *Charley*, 189 F.3d at 1267–68; *Toledo*, 985 F.2d at 1470.

The error was plain. A legal error is plain when it is clear under current law. *Olano*, 507 U.S. at 734. Long before Stanley’s trial, this Court had held that expert testimony cannot supplant the jury’s role on credibility. *Toledo*, 985 F.2d at 1470; *Charley*, 189 F.3d at 1267–68. Nor may the government elicit testimony that certain verbal or nonverbal behaviors reveal deception; such testimony improperly tells jurors how to interpret a defendant’s statements. *United States v. Adams*, 271 F.3d at 1245–46. Jones’s testimony fell squarely within that forbidden territory.

The error affected Stanley’s substantial rights. To satisfy this prong, Stanley need only show a reasonable probability that, but for the error, the result would have been different. *United States v. Olano*, 507 U.S. at 734; *United States v. Goode*, 483 F.3d 676, 681 (10th Cir. 2007). The government’s second-trial theory was circumstantial and depended heavily on linking Stanley to the East Pine residence and to the robbery through inference. In that setting, Jones’s testimony was uniquely damaging because it supplied the jury with an FBI expert’s conclusion that Stanley was lying. Credibility was central: Stanley’s post-arrest statements included both an initial false identity and later admissions, and the government used those statements to support consciousness of guilt. A federal agent’s expert opinion that Stanley was deceptive and that his references to God were deceptive likely carried substantial weight. Where inadmissible opinion testimony bears directly on the decisive issue of truthfulness, prejudice is evident. *Charley*, 189 F.3d at 1268; *Adams*, 271 F.3d at 1246.

Finally, the error seriously affected the fairness, integrity, and public reputation of the proceedings. The jury alone must decide whether a defendant is telling the truth. *Toledo*, 985 F.2d at 1470. Allowing the government’s expert to tell the jury that Stanley’s statements were deceptive “invade[d] the province of the jury” and risked conviction on the basis of purported law-enforcement expertise rather than admissible evidence. *Charley*, 189 F.3d at 1268. This is precisely the type of error appellate courts correct even under plain-error review. *Olano*, 507 U.S. at 736.

For these reasons, Stanley’s conviction should be reversed and the case remanded for a new trial.
