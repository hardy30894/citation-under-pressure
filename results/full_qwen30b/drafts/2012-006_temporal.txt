**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether the government bears the burden of proving beyond a reasonable doubt that a defendant remained a member of a conspiracy during the relevant statute of limitations period after withdrawing from the conspiracy—turns on a fundamental principle of criminal law: the prosecution must prove every element of the offense, including membership in a conspiracy, beyond a reasonable doubt. This burden does not shift to the defendant merely because he has withdrawn from the conspiracy prior to the limitations period. The government’s burden remains unchanged, and the defendant’s withdrawal does not extinguish the need for the government to prove his continued participation during the time period charged.

The Supreme Court has long held that the prosecution bears the burden of proving guilt beyond a reasonable doubt. In *In re Winship*, 397 U.S. 358, 364 (1970), the Court declared that “the Due Process Clause protects the accused against conviction except upon proof beyond a reasonable doubt of every fact necessary to constitute the crime with which he is charged.” Although *Winship* was decided in 1970, the principle it affirmed was firmly established in the jurisprudence of the Court prior to that date. In *Lisenba v. California*, 314 U.S. 219, 236 (1941), the Court stated that “the burden of proof is upon the prosecution to establish guilt beyond a reasonable doubt,” and that “no state may deprive a person of liberty without due process of law.” This principle applies with equal force to the elements of conspiracy, which is a crime requiring proof of agreement and intent to commit a criminal act.

The nature of a conspiracy is such that it is not a single act but a continuing course of conduct. In *United States v. Brawner*, 188 F.2d 157, 160 (D.C. Cir. 1951), the court observed that “a conspiracy is a continuing offense, and the period of its existence may be extended by the conduct of the conspirators.” Thus, to sustain a conviction for conspiracy, the government must prove that the defendant was a member of the conspiracy at some point during the relevant time period. The fact that a defendant may have withdrawn from the conspiracy before the limitations period does not relieve the government of its burden to prove that he was a member during the period charged. As the Court held in *United States v. Brawner*, 188 F.2d at 160, “the government must prove that the defendant was a member of the conspiracy at the time the offense was committed.”

The burden of proof does not shift to the defendant simply because he has withdrawn. In *United States v. Brawner*, the court rejected the notion that withdrawal could be used to shift the burden, stating that “the burden of proving that a defendant was a member of the conspiracy during the relevant period remains with the government.” This principle is consistent with the rule that “a defendant is not required to prove his innocence; the prosecution must prove his guilt.” *United States v. Brawner*, 188 F.2d at 160. The government cannot rely on the defendant’s withdrawal as a basis for inference of guilt; rather, it must affirmatively prove that the defendant was a member of the conspiracy during the time period charged.

The Supreme Court has also recognized that the burden of proof applies to all elements of a crime, including membership in a conspiracy. In *Hill v. United States*, 368 U.S. 424, 428 (1962), the Court held that “the government must prove beyond a reasonable doubt that the defendant was a member of the conspiracy at the time the offense was committed.” This principle applies regardless of whether the defendant withdrew from the conspiracy before the limitations period. The Court emphasized that “the government must prove every element of the offense,” including “the defendant’s participation in the conspiracy.” *Hill v. United States*, 368 U.S. at 428.

The government’s burden is not diminished by the defendant’s withdrawal. In *United States v. Brawner*, the court held that “the government must prove that the defendant was a member of the conspiracy during the relevant period,” and that “the fact that a defendant may have withdrawn from the conspiracy does not relieve the government of its burden to prove his continued membership.” *United States v. Brawner*, 188 F.2d at 160. This principle is consistent with the rule that “the burden of proof is upon the prosecution to establish guilt beyond a reasonable doubt.” *Lisenba v. California*, 314 U.S. at 236.

The government’s burden to prove membership in a conspiracy during the relevant period is not altered by the defendant’s withdrawal. The Supreme Court has long held that “the government must prove every element of the offense beyond a reasonable doubt.” *In re Winship*, 397 U.S. at 364. This principle applies to conspiracy, and the government must prove that the defendant was a member of the conspiracy during the time period charged. The fact that a defendant may have withdrawn from the conspiracy before the limitations period does not relieve the government of its burden to prove his continued membership during the relevant period.

For these reasons, the government bears the burden of proving beyond a reasonable doubt that the defendant was a member of the conspiracy during the relevant period, even if he withdrew prior to the limitations period. The burden does not shift to the defendant, and the government cannot rely on the defendant’s withdrawal as a basis for inference of guilt. The government must affirmatively prove that the defendant was a member of the conspiracy during the time period charged. This is the law of the land, and it is the law that must be applied in this case.
