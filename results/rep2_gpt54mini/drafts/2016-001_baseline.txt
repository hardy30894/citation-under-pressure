**Argument**

The Patent Act does not authorize awarding a design-patent plaintiff the infringer’s **entire** profit when the patented design covers only a component of the accused product. Design-patent damages are governed by statute, and the text limits recovery to the “total profit” of the “article of manufacture” to which the patented design has been applied. 35 U.S.C. § 289. That language does not erase the article-of-manufacture boundary. Rather, it confirms that the relevant profits are those tied to the infringing article, not necessarily the defendant’s whole end product. See *Dobson v. Dornan*, 118 U.S. 10, 17–18 (1886); *Bush & Lane Piano Co. v. Becker Bros.*, 222 F. 902, 907 (2d Cir. 1915).

This Court has long recognized that design-patent protection is limited to the “appearance of an article,” *Gorham Co. v. White*, 81 U.S. (14 Wall.) 511, 525 (1871), and that remedies should track the scope of the patented design. In *Dobson*, the Court refused to permit recovery untethered to the value contributed by the design, warning against awards based on the defendant’s entire business where the design itself was only a part of the commercial product. 118 U.S. at 17–18. Congress responded by enacting what is now § 289, but it did so to create a remedy for design infringement, not to confer a windfall disconnected from the patented article. *Samsung Elecs. Co. v. Apple Inc.*, 580 U.S. 53, 60–61 (2016).

The Federal Circuit’s approach below effectively reads the phrase “article of manufacture” out of § 289. That phrase must do work. Statutes should be construed so that no word is rendered surplusage. *Duncan v. Walker*, 533 U.S. 167, 174 (2001). If a design patent on a component of a multicomponent product always permits recovery of profits from the entire product, then the statutory reference to the relevant “article of manufacture” becomes meaningless. But this Court has already rejected interpretations that collapse distinct statutory terms into one. *Russello v. United States*, 464 U.S. 16, 23 (1983).

The better reading is that the “article of manufacture” may be either the end product sold to consumers or a component of that product, depending on the design and the facts of the case. *Samsung Elecs.*, 580 U.S. at 65. This Court in *Samsung* held that “the term ‘article of manufacture’ in § 289 encompasses both a product sold to a consumer and a component of that product.” Id. at 57. That holding necessarily forecloses any categorical rule awarding the defendant’s total profits whenever the infringing design appears somewhere in the sold product. Instead, the factfinder must first identify the relevant article of manufacture and then award the infringer’s “total profit” on that article. Id. at 65. Because the Federal Circuit did not perform that inquiry here, its judgment cannot stand.

Apple may argue that *Samsung* preserved the possibility of total-product damages. It did, but only where the whole product is the article of manufacture. 580 U.S. at 65. That is not this case. Where the patented design is limited to a component, and where the accused product contains many unrelated features, awarding all profits from the entire finished product would ignore the statute’s component-based limit and contravene *Samsung*’s express recognition that the article of manufacture can be smaller than the end product. Id. at 57, 65.

Nor is there any basis to presume that the patented design necessarily drives the defendant’s entire profit. This Court has repeatedly required causal and evidentiary rigor when patent remedies seek monetary awards. See *Aro Mfg. Co. v. Convertible Top Replacement Co.*, 377 U.S. 476, 507 (1964); *Garretson v. Clark*, 111 U.S. 120, 121 (1884). Although § 289 displaces ordinary apportionment once the article of manufacture is identified, it does not authorize selecting the wrong article in the first place. *Samsung Elecs.*, 580 U.S. at 65.

The judgment should therefore be reversed, and the case remanded for a proper damages determination limited to the profits attributable to the relevant article of manufacture.
