**Argument**

The court of appeals’ rule cannot be squared with this Court’s conspiracy and limitations precedents. The government bears the burden to prove that an indicted conspiracy continued into the limitations period, but that burden does not include disproving an individual defendant’s prior withdrawal beyond a reasonable doubt unless the defendant has properly placed withdrawal in issue. A conspiracy is presumed to continue until its objectives are achieved or the conspiracy is abandoned, and once the government proves the defendant joined the conspiracy, the defendant remains a member absent proof of withdrawal. *Smith v. United States*, 568 U.S. 106, 111-13 (2013); *United States v. Jimenez Recio*, 537 U.S. 270, 274-75 (2003); *Hyde v. United States*, 225 U.S. 347, 369-70 (1912).

This Court has long held that withdrawal is an affirmative defense. In *Smith v. United States*, the Court unanimously explained that “withdrawal terminates the defendant’s liability for postwithdrawal acts of his co-conspirators” and that “the defendant bears the burden of proving withdrawal.” 568 U.S. at 110, 113. That allocation flows from the nature of the defense: withdrawal does not negate the elements of the conspiracy offense the government must prove, but rather cuts off liability for later consequences. *Id.* at 110-12; *Hyde*, 225 U.S. at 369-70. The same principle controls limitations. “A defendant’s participation in a conspiracy is presumed to continue until he withdraws from the conspiracy by affirmative action.” *United States v. Read*, 658 F.2d 1225, 1231 (7th Cir. 1981); see also *United States v. Grimmett*, 236 F.3d 452, 454-55 (8th Cir. 2001) (limitations is satisfied by proof that conspiracy continued into period and defendant remained a member unless he withdrew); *United States v. Eppolito*, 543 F.3d 25, 49-50 (2d Cir. 2008).

The D.C. Circuit’s approach improperly shifted that burden to the government. It required the government to prove beyond a reasonable doubt, not merely that the conspiracy continued into the limitations period, but that each accused remained a member during that period despite evidence of prior division among conspiracy leaders. That is inconsistent with *Smith* and with the settled rule that a statute-of-limitations defense ordinarily must be raised and supported by the defendant, after which the government proves timeliness of the offense. *United States v. Cook*, 84 U.S. (17 Wall.) 168, 179-82 (1872); *Toussie v. United States*, 397 U.S. 112, 115 (1970); *Smith*, 568 U.S. at 114-15.

This Court has also emphasized that conspiracy liability turns on agreement, not on uninterrupted personal involvement by every participant. In *Pinkerton v. United States*, 328 U.S. 640, 646-48 (1946), the Court held that a conspirator remains liable for co-conspirators’ acts done in furtherance of the scheme so long as he has not withdrawn. Likewise, in *Krulewitch v. United States*, 336 U.S. 440, 444-45 (1949), the Court explained that conspiracy is a continuing offense whose duration depends on the life of the unlawful agreement. Nothing in those cases suggests that once a defendant introduces evidence of departure, the prosecution must disprove withdrawal beyond a reasonable doubt as part of its prima facie limitations case. To the contrary, the continuing nature of the offense makes withdrawal an exception to ongoing liability, not an element of the crime. *Smith*, 568 U.S. at 110-13.

The D.C. Circuit’s rule also conflicts with the ordinary burden framework in criminal cases. The prosecution must prove the elements of the charged offense beyond a reasonable doubt, *In re Winship*, 397 U.S. 358, 364 (1970), but legislatures and courts may assign defendants the burden of proving affirmative defenses that do not negate any element. *Patterson v. New York*, 432 U.S. 197, 206-10 (1977); *Dixon v. United States*, 548 U.S. 1, 7-9 (2006). Withdrawal fits that model. It does not deny that the defendant knowingly joined an unlawful agreement; it seeks to avoid liability thereafter. *Smith*, 568 U.S. at 110-12. Requiring the government to negate withdrawal beyond a reasonable doubt would collapse the distinction between offense elements and affirmative defenses.

Finally, practical and doctrinal considerations confirm the error. Conspiracies are secretive by nature, and evidence of a participant’s departure will often be uniquely within the defendant’s knowledge. *Smith*, 568 U.S. at 113-14. Placing the burden on the government to disprove withdrawal in every limitations case would create an unwarranted obstacle to prosecution of continuing conspiracies and invite dismissal based on speculative claims of exit. That is not the law. The proper rule is that once the government proves the conspiracy continued into the limitations period, the defendant who claims withdrawal bears the burden of establishing it. *Id.* at 113-15; *Hyde*, 225 U.S. at 369-70.

For those reasons, the judgment below should be reversed.
