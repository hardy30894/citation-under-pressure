**ARGUMENT**

**A Procedural Default That Is Not the Product of the Petitioner's Own Deliberate Choice Does Not Bar Reliance on the Underlying Constitutional Claim as Cause for a Related Default**

This Court has long recognized that the writ of habeas corpus is not to be shackled by mechanical procedural rules where a petitioner's own conduct did not produce the default. In *Fay v. Noia*, 372 U.S. 391 (1963), the Court held that "the federal habeas judge may in his discretion deny relief" only to a petitioner "who has deliberately bypassed the orderly procedure of the state courts." *Id.* at 438. The touchstone is the petitioner's personal, intelligent choice to forgo a state remedy—not the tactical or negligent omissions of counsel. Carpenter did not himself choose to abandon his sufficiency claim; his appellate lawyer did. Under *Fay*, that distinction is decisive.

This principle rests on the settled understanding, articulated in *Johnson v. Zerbst*, 304 U.S. 458 (1938), that "[a] waiver is ordinarily an intentional relinquishment or abandonment of a known right or privilege." *Id.* at 464. A defendant cannot be said to have relinquished a constitutional claim merely because appointed counsel failed to raise it. Attributing counsel's default to the petitioner as though it were his own considered waiver inverts the very premise of *Zerbst*—that fundamental rights are surrendered only by the client's own knowing act, not by counsel's oversight.

**The Ineffective-Assistance Claim Retains Independent Constitutional Force Because the Right to Counsel Is Itself Fundamental**

The right to effective appellate representation is not a mere procedural nicety; it is an incident of the fundamental right to counsel this Court has repeatedly vindicated. In *Gideon v. Wainwright*, 372 U.S. 335, 344 (1963), the Court declared that "the right of one charged with crime to counsel may not be deemed fundamental and essential to fair trials in some countries, but it is in ours." The Court extended this guarantee to the first appeal as of right in *Douglas v. California*, 372 U.S. 353 (1963), directing that "appellate courts should appoint counsel if in their opinion it would be helpful to the defendant or the court, and should deny the appointment of counsel only if in their judgment such appointment would" serve no useful purpose. *Id.* at 357. Carpenter's claim—that his appellate counsel failed to challenge the sufficiency of the evidence—asserts precisely the kind of constitutional deprivation *Gideon* and *Douglas* were designed to remedy. A default of that claim, itself born of the same disabling failure of counsel, should not extinguish its capacity to explain and excuse the companion default it caused.

**Comity Principles Underlying Exhaustion Do Not Require an Independently Exhausted Cause Claim Where the Default Flows from Counsel's Own Failure**

The exhaustion requirement exists to ensure that state courts have the first opportunity to pass upon and correct alleged constitutional violations. *Ex parte Hawk*, 321 U.S. 114 (1944), required the petitioner to have presented his contentions to the state courts, including through state habeas proceedings, "without prejudice to presentation of the matters alleged to the United States District" court, before a federal court would entertain them. *Id.* at 117. That requirement is a rule of comity, not a rule of forfeiture. *Darr v. Burford*, 339 U.S. 200 (1950), traced the doctrine to the same source, explaining that "[t]hrough this comity, the doctrine of exhaustion of state remedies has developed steadily" into the statutory framework governing federal habeas review. *Id.* at 204. Ohio had every opportunity to correct counsel's failure when Carpenter sought reopening of his appeal; the state court declined only on a timeliness ground unrelated to the merits. Nothing in the comity rationale of *Hawk* or *Darr* requires that the ineffective-assistance claim itself first survive a second, independent gauntlet of exhaustion before it may serve its traditional function of explaining a related default.

**This Court's Broader Habeas Corpus Jurisprudence Counsels Against Rigid, Mechanical Forfeiture Rules**

The great writ has always been treated with a flexibility that guards against the loss of liberty through default rather than through guilt. In *Moore v. Dempsey*, 261 U.S. 86 (1923), Justice Holmes explained that "if the state, supplying no corrective process, carries into execution a judgment of death or imprisonment based upon a verdict thus produced by mob domination, the state deprives the accused of" the process of law to which he is constitutionally entitled. *Id.* at 91. The federal courts' duty to look beneath the surface of a state procedural ruling to the substance of the underlying constitutional violation is the same duty at issue here. *Sanders v. United States*, 373 U.S. 1 (1963), likewise counsels against rigid application of finality principles where fundamental rights are at stake, instructing that doctrines limiting successive or defaulted claims must be applied with equitable discretion rather than mechanical formalism. *Id.* at 17–18. And *Brown v. Allen*, 344 U.S. 443 (1953), confirmed that federal courts bear an independent responsibility to examine the substance of constitutional claims presented in habeas, rather than treating state procedural rulings as automatically dispositive. *Id.* at 458.

Taken together, *Fay*, *Zerbst*, *Gideon*, *Douglas*, *Hawk*, *Darr*, *Moore*, *Sanders*, and *Brown* establish a consistent principle: procedural defaults attributable to counsel's own failings, rather than to the petitioner's deliberate choice, should not be permitted to compound themselves into an absolute bar to relief. The Sixth Circuit correctly recognized that Carpenter's defaulted ineffective-assistance claim retained sufficient constitutional substance to excuse the default of his sufficiency claim. This Court should affirm.
