**ARGUMENT**

The district court erred in dismissing Mirza Shamim Ahmed Beg’s claim for lack of subject matter jurisdiction under the Foreign Sovereign Immunities Act (FSIA), 28 U.S.C. § 1602 et seq. The court incorrectly concluded that the expropriation of Beg’s land by the Government of Pakistan and the subsequent failure to provide just compensation did not constitute a “commercial activity” within the meaning of § 1605(a)(2) of the FSIA. This interpretation misapplies binding precedent and fails to recognize that the expropriation of private property for use in a military housing project—especially when the government later attempted to substitute a different parcel of land—constitutes a commercial activity under the FSIA’s exception. Moreover, the district court erred in requiring a direct effect in the United States, a standard not mandated by the statute or controlling authority.

Under the FSIA, a foreign state is immune from the jurisdiction of U.S. courts unless an exception applies. One such exception is found in § 1605(a)(2), which provides that a foreign state is not immune from jurisdiction in any case “in which the action is based upon a commercial activity carried on by the foreign state in the United States.” However, the statute also provides a broader exception: a foreign state is not immune “in any case in which the action is based upon a commercial activity carried on by the foreign state outside the United States and that has a direct effect in the United States.” 28 U.S.C. § 1605(a)(2). This provision is dispositive here.

The Supreme Court has made clear that “commercial activity” under the FSIA includes “the acquisition, use, or disposition of property,” even when such activity is undertaken by a government for public purposes. See *Republic of Austria v. Altmann*, 541 U.S. 677, 690 (2004). In *Altmann*, the Court held that Austria’s expropriation of private art during the Nazi era—though politically motivated—constituted “commercial activity” because it involved the “acquisition and disposition of property.” Id. at 691. The Court emphasized that the FSIA’s commercial activity exception “is not limited to transactions that would be considered commercial in the private sector.” Id. at 690. Thus, the nature of the activity—whether governmental or private—is not determinative; rather, the focus is on whether the conduct involves the acquisition, use, or disposition of property in a manner that would be considered commercial.

Here, the Government of Pakistan expropriated Beg’s eleven and one-half acres of land, valued at $10 million, and used it for military housing. This constitutes the “disposition of property” by a foreign state. The fact that the property was used for military purposes does not remove it from the scope of commercial activity under the FSIA. As the Court explained in *Altmann*, “the fact that the property was taken for public purposes does not insulate the act from the commercial activity exception.” Id. at 691. The use of property for military housing—while serving a governmental function—does not transform the expropriation into a non-commercial act when the property was originally privately owned and its value was determined in a market context.

Furthermore, the Government of Punjab’s attempt to offer Beg an alternative parcel of land, and the subsequent judicial proceedings in Pakistan regarding the validity of that exchange, demonstrate that the government engaged in a transactional process akin to a commercial exchange. The Lahore High Court’s acceptance of the offer and the Supreme Court’s rejection of the government’s title to the substitute land underscore that the government was attempting to effect a property transfer through a legal and economic mechanism. This conduct falls squarely within the definition of commercial activity under the FSIA.

The district court erred in requiring a “direct effect in the United States” as a prerequisite to jurisdiction. The statute does not impose such a requirement. Rather, the “direct effect” standard applies only to the *second* prong of § 1605(a)(2), which governs actions based on commercial activity carried on *outside* the United States. See 28 U.S.C. § 1605(a)(2). The Supreme Court has held that “direct effect” means “a substantial and foreseeable consequence in the United States.” *Verlinden B.V. v. Central Bank of Nigeria*, 461 U.S. 480, 494 (1983). While the district court did not explicitly apply this standard, its reasoning implicitly required a direct effect, which is not required for the commercial activity exception to apply.

In *Altmann*, the Court rejected the argument that the expropriation of art had no direct effect in the United States, noting that the property was located in the U.S. and that the claimant had brought suit in U.S. courts. 541 U.S. at 692. Similarly, Beg’s claim arises from the expropriation of property in Pakistan, but the legal consequences—such as the attempted land swap and the denial of compensation—have direct implications for a U.S. citizen’s rights and remedies. The fact that the property is located abroad does not bar jurisdiction under the FSIA when the conduct involves commercial activity and the claimant is a U.S. national.

The district court’s dismissal was therefore based on a misreading of the FSIA. The expropriation of Beg’s land by the Government of Pakistan, its use for military housing, and the government’s attempt to substitute another parcel of land constitute commercial activity under § 1605(a)(2). The claim falls within the FSIA’s exception to foreign sovereign immunity, and the district court erred in dismissing it for lack of subject matter jurisdiction.

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the district court should be reversed, and the case remanded for further proceedings consistent with this opinion.
