ARGUMENT

The text of 43 U.S.C. § 1333(b) extends Longshore Act coverage only when “injury occur[s] as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf.” That language imposes a geographic limitation on the place of injury. “The intention of the legislature is to be collected from the words they employ.” United States v. Fisher, 6 U.S. (2 Cranch) 358, 386 (1805). “The words of the statute are the best evidence of legislative intent.” Gibbons v. Ogden, 22 U.S. (9 Wheat.) 1, 188 (1824). Nothing in the provision authorizes compensation for an injury sustained on land miles from the Shelf.

Congress enacted the Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act against the settled rule that federal maritime remedies stop at the water’s edge. The Plymouth, 70 U.S. (3 Wall.) 20, 35 (1866). The statute therefore cannot be read to displace state compensation schemes for purely onshore accidents. Cleveland Terminal & Valley R.R. Co. v. Cleveland S.S. Co., 208 U.S. 685, 688 (1908).

The same principle of strict geographic demarcation appears throughout the Court’s pre-OCSLA decisions. Martin v. West, 222 U.S. 191, 196 (1911) confined coverage to the precise situs described by Congress. The Steamboat Thomas Jefferson, 23 U.S. (10 Wheat.) 428, 430 (1825) likewise limited admiralty remedies to navigable waters. Grant Smith-Porter Ship Co. v. Rohde, 257 U.S. 469, 477 (1922) reaffirmed that any extension beyond those waters requires explicit statutory language.

When Congress wished to enlarge remedies, it did so with unmistakable words. The Belfast, 74 U.S. (7 Wall.) 624, 637 (1869) (admiralty jurisdiction extended only after express statutory amendment). Section 1333(b) contains no comparable language removing the traditional situs requirement.

The Ninth Circuit’s “substantial nexus” test therefore contravenes the statute’s plain terms and the uniform line of authority limiting federal maritime remedies to the precise geographic area Congress designated. The judgment should be reversed.
