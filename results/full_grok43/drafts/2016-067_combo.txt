The Fair Debt Collection Practices Act regulates only those who collect debts “owed or due another.” 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(6). Statutory language is to be given its ordinary meaning, and resort to legislative history is unnecessary when the text is unambiguous. Caminetti v. United States, 242 U.S. 470, 490 (1917); United States v. Missouri Pacific R. Co., 278 U.S. 269, 278 (1929); Iselin v. United States, 270 U.S. 245, 250 (1926). The phrase “owed or due another” refers to the status of the obligation at the moment of collection. Crooks v. Harrelson, 282 U.S. 55, 59 (1930). A purchaser that has acquired title therefore collects its own debt, not the debt of another. Burnet v. Guggenheim, 288 U.S. 280, 284-85 (1933).

The Act’s definitional structure confirms the distinction. The term “creditor” encompasses any person to whom a debt is owed, 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(4), while the exclusions in § 1692a(6)(A)-(F) operate only after a person first satisfies one of the affirmative definitions of “debt collector.” Helvering v. City Bank Farmers Trust Co., 296 U.S. 85, 89 (1935). Because Santander collects debts it owns outright, it never enters the definitional gateway. United States v. American Trucking Ass’ns, 310 U.S. 534, 543 (1940).

The same textual command precludes any inquiry into the debt’s status at the time of assignment. Packard Motor Car Co. v. NLRB, 330 U.S. 485, 492 (1947). Ownership, not the circumstances of acquisition, determines whether collection is undertaken for another. NLRB v. Hearst Publications, Inc., 322 U.S. 111, 124 (1944). An entity that regularly collects its own accounts is therefore a creditor, not a debt collector, regardless of how it obtained title. Addison v. Holly Hill Fruit Products, Inc., 322 U.S. 607, 616 (1944).

Santander purchased the loans before commencing the challenged communications. It therefore acted as a creditor collecting debts owed to itself. The complaint accordingly fails to allege that Santander meets the statutory definition of “debt collector.”
