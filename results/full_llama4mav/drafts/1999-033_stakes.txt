The Equal Protection Clause does not give rise to a cause of action on behalf of a "class of one." The Seventh Circuit's decision in this case, allowing Olech to proceed with her equal protection claim as a "class of one," is contrary to established precedent.

The Equal Protection Clause is typically invoked to protect classes or groups of individuals from discriminatory state action. See, e.g., Brown v. Board of Education, 347 U.S. 483 (1954); Loving v. Virginia, 388 U.S. 1 (1967). The Clause is not intended to constitutionalize every tort or zoning dispute by allowing individuals to claim that they have been treated unfairly or irrationally by the government.

In Esmail v. Macrane, 53 F.3d 176 (7th Cir. 1995), the Seventh Circuit recognized that an equal protection claim can be brought by an individual who alleges that they have been singled out for adverse treatment by the government without a rational basis. However, this exception is narrowly tailored to cases where the government's action is motivated by illegitimate animus or ill will. See id. at 178-79.

The Seventh Circuit's decision in this case, however, goes beyond this narrow exception. By allowing Olech to proceed with her equal protection claim as a "class of one," the court effectively transforms the Equal Protection Clause into a federal "due process" or "rational basis" review of state action, which is not its intended purpose. See, e.g., FCC v. Beach Communications, Inc., 508 U.S. 307, 313-14 (1993) (rational basis review is not a license for courts to judge the wisdom, fairness, or logic of legislative choices).

Moreover, allowing "class of one" claims to proceed would open the floodgates to a torrent of litigation, as every disappointed applicant or individual who feels they have been treated unfairly by the government could potentially bring an equal protection claim. See, e.g., City of Cleburne v. Cleburne Living Center, 473 U.S. 432, 442-44 (1985) (rejecting the idea that the Equal Protection Clause requires "a heightened level of scrutiny" for every government decision that is challenged as arbitrary or irrational).

In conclusion, the Equal Protection Clause does not give rise to a cause of action on behalf of a "class of one." The Seventh Circuit's decision in this case should be reversed, and Olech's equal protection claim should be dismissed for failure to state a cognizable claim under the Equal Protection Clause.
