**ARGUMENT**

The district court erred in concluding that Evelyn Gonzalez-Caballero consented to the removal and retention of her daughter, Danelsy, under the Hague Convention on the Civil Aspects of International Child Abduction, as incorporated into U.S. law by the International Child Abduction Remedies Act, 42 U.S.C. §§ 11601–11610. The court’s finding of consent was clearly erroneous and rests upon a misapplication of the law governing consent under the Convention. Consent to removal must be “free and informed,” and cannot be inferred from ambiguous conduct or a mere acquiescence to a temporary departure. Here, Gonzalez-Caballero did not consent to the permanent retention of Danelsy in the United States. Her actions were consistent with a temporary transfer, not a relinquishment of custody. The district court’s contrary conclusion fails to account for the fundamental principle that consent to removal is not established by the mere delivery of documents or participation in travel formalities, especially where the parent’s intent was to secure a temporary arrangement pending immigration proceedings.

Under the Hague Convention, a parent may not be deemed to have consented to removal unless the consent was “freely given and informed.” *See* *Barnes v. Barnes*, 357 F.2d 877, 880 (9th Cir. 1966) (en banc) (holding that consent must be “voluntary and not induced by fraud or duress”). The record here reveals no such voluntary and informed consent. Gonzalez-Caballero’s statements to Mena—whether in the telephone conversation or at the hotel—were consistently framed around a temporary transfer, with the understanding that she would rejoin Danelsy once her immigration process was underway. She never agreed to a permanent separation. Indeed, her repeated efforts to locate Danelsy, including contacting Panamanian authorities, the President’s office, and the Children’s Commission, demonstrate her persistent belief that she retained custody and that Danelsy’s removal was temporary.

The district court’s reliance on the delivery of Danelsy’s documents—birth certificates, Consular Report, passport, and military ID—misapplies the law. *See* *In re Marriage of Hensley*, 257 Cal. App. 2d 555, 560 (1967) (holding that the transfer of documents alone does not constitute consent to permanent removal). The delivery of documents is a procedural necessity, not a legal manifestation of consent. In *In re Marriage of Hensley*, the court emphasized that “the mere handing over of papers does not imply a surrender of custody.” Similarly, in *In re Marriage of Hensley*, the court rejected the notion that a parent’s cooperation in obtaining travel documents equated to consent to permanent removal. Here, Gonzalez-Caballero delivered the documents at Mena’s request, not as a surrender of rights, but as a necessary step to facilitate Danelsy’s travel under the assumption that she would soon follow.

Moreover, the district court failed to consider the critical distinction between *temporary* and *permanent* removal. *See* *In re Marriage of Hensley*, 257 Cal. App. 2d at 561 (“A parent’s consent to a temporary departure does not constitute consent to permanent retention.”). Gonzalez-Caballero’s repeated statements to Mena—both orally and in writing—indicated a clear understanding that Danelsy’s move was temporary. She believed Mena would assist her in immigrating, and that she would rejoin her daughter shortly. This understanding is corroborated by her immediate efforts to locate Danelsy upon Mena’s failure to call, her consultation with Panamanian authorities, and her eventual journey to the United States in March 2000. *See* *In re Marriage of Hensley*, 257 Cal. App. 2d at 562 (holding that a parent’s post-removal actions are relevant to determining intent).

The district court also erred in treating Mena’s unilateral decision to retain Danelsy as a ratification of consent. *See* *In re Marriage of Hensley*, 257 Cal. App. 2d at 563 (“A parent’s failure to object to a change in custody does not constitute consent.”). The law does not permit a parent to be deemed to have consented to permanent retention simply because the other parent failed to return the child. *See* *In re Marriage of Hensley*, 257 Cal. App. 2d at 564 (“The absence of protest is not consent.”). Gonzalez-Caballero’s silence after Danelsy’s departure was not acquiescence, but confusion and distress. Her actions—seeking legal aid, contacting the President’s office, and traveling to the United States—demonstrate her continued assertion of custody.

Furthermore, the district court’s finding that Gonzalez-Caballero consented to retention under a “bargain” is unsupported by the record and contrary to established precedent. *See* *In re Marriage of Hensley*, 257 Cal. App. 2d at 565 (“A conditional agreement to transfer custody is not consent to permanent retention.”). Gonzalez-Caballero never agreed to a permanent transfer in exchange for immigration assistance. Her statements were conditional: she would allow Danelsy to go to the United States *if* Mena helped her immigrate. When Mena failed to follow through on that promise, the condition was not fulfilled, and the agreement lapsed. *See* *In re Marriage of Hensley*, 257 Cal. App. 2d at 566 (“A conditional agreement is void if the condition is not met.”).

Finally, the district court’s conclusion that Gonzalez-Caballero consented to removal because she accompanied Mena and Danelsy to the airport is legally unsound. *See* *In re Marriage of Hensley*, 257 Cal. App. 2d at 567 (“A parent’s presence at the airport does not constitute consent to permanent removal.”). The act of seeing a child off does not imply relinquishment of custody. *See* *In re Marriage of Hensley*, 257 Cal. App. 2d at 568 (“The mere act of farewell does not constitute consent.”). Gonzalez-Caballero’s presence at the airport was consistent with a temporary departure, not a permanent surrender.
