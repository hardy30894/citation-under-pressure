**ARGUMENT**

I. Clemency proceedings create no protected life or liberty interest under the Due Process Clause.

Pardon and commutation decisions “have not traditionally been the business of courts; as such, they are rarely, if ever, appropriate subjects for judicial review.” Connecticut Bd. of Pardons v. Dumschat, 452 U.S. 458, 464 (1981). The Ohio Constitution vests the Governor with “almost unfettered discretion” to grant or deny clemency. Because the decision whether to extend mercy “is rarely, if ever, appropriate” for judicial oversight, id., Ohio’s clemency procedure itself creates no constitutionally protected interest. See also Meachum v. Fano, 427 U.S. 215, 225 (1976).

The Sixth Circuit’s contrary conclusion—that clemency is “an integral part of the . . . system for finally adjudicating the guilt or innocence of a defendant”—misapplies Evitts v. Lucey, 469 U.S. 387 (1985). Evitts held only that when a State elects to provide a first appeal as of right, that appeal must comport with due process. Id. at 393. The Court expressly limited the right to counsel to that first appeal and declined to extend it to habeas corpus. Id. at 394 (citing Ross v. Moffitt, 417 U.S. 600 (1974)). Executive clemency lies even farther removed from trial than collateral review. Dumschat therefore controls: no protected interest arises merely because the State affords an opportunity for mercy. 452 U.S. at 464-65.

Sandin v. Conner, 515 U.S. 472 (1995), reinforces the point. States may create procedures without thereby generating liberty interests that trigger federal due-process scrutiny. Id. at 483-84. Ohio’s clemency regulation is precisely such a procedure; it confers no substantive right to commutation.

II. The voluntary clemency interview imposes no unconstitutional condition on the Fifth Amendment privilege.

The regulation permits, but does not require, an inmate to request a personal interview. “Only the prisoner and the person or persons assigned by the parole board chair shall be present.” Nothing in the regulation compels answers to any question. A prisoner “remains free to say as much or as little at the interview as he wishes.”

In Baxter v. Palmigiano, 425 U.S. 308 (1976), the Court refused to presume that adverse inferences drawn from silence in prison disciplinary proceedings would be used in future criminal prosecutions. Id. at 324. The same restraint applies here. Any hypothetical use of a voluntary statement in a later prosecution is “premature” for federal adjudication. Id. at 324-25; see also Wolff v. McDonnell, 418 U.S. 539, 558 (1974).

Because the interview is wholly optional and the regulation contains no waiver requirement, the procedure does not place Woodard to an unconstitutional choice between asserting the Fifth Amendment and participating in clemency. The judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
