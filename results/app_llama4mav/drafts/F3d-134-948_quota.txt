ARGUMENT

I. The District Court Erred in Denying Selam's Habeas Corpus Petition on the Grounds that He Was Not Denied Compulsory Process.

The Indian Civil Rights Act ("ICRA") guarantees tribal court defendants the right "to have compulsory process for obtaining witnesses in his favor." 25 U.S.C. § 1302(6). To determine whether Selam was denied this right, we must examine the actions taken by his appointed counsel, Tribal Spokesperson Patricia Leno-Baker, and the tribal court's role in facilitating the attendance of defense witnesses.

In White v. Ragen, 324 U.S. 760 (1945), the Supreme Court held that a defendant's right to compulsory process is violated when the state fails to provide a meaningful mechanism for securing the attendance of witnesses. Here, although Leno-Baker was able to locate three of Selam's potential character witnesses, two of them informed her that their testimony would be damaging to Selam. The tribal judge and Leno-Baker advised Selam of his right to compel the appearance of witnesses, but Selam did not request the judge to subpoena anyone. However, as the Court noted in Washington v. Texas, 388 U.S. 14, 19 (1967), "the right to compulsory process is not limited to the mere issuance of a subpoena; it also includes the right to have the subpoena enforced." The tribal court's failure to take more proactive steps to assist Selam in securing the attendance of his witnesses, despite his failure to request subpoenas, raises concerns about whether Selam was afforded a meaningful opportunity to exercise his right to compulsory process.

Furthermore, the Ninth Circuit has held that a defendant's failure to request a subpoena does not necessarily waive their right to compulsory process. See United States v. Henry, 615 F.2d 1223, 1234 (9th Cir. 1980) (holding that the government's failure to disclose exculpatory evidence can be a violation of the defendant's right to compulsory process, even if the defendant did not request it). Similarly, in this case, the tribal court's failure to take more affirmative steps to facilitate Selam's access to witnesses may have constituted a denial of his right to compulsory process.

II. The District Court Erred in Holding that Selam Waived His Confrontation Clause Claim.

The district court held that Selam waived his Confrontation Clause claim by failing to raise it in the tribal court of appeals. However, the Supreme Court has held that a defendant's failure to exhaust state remedies does not necessarily bar federal habeas corpus review. See O'Sullivan v. Boerckel, 526 U.S. 838, 847 (1999) (holding that a defendant's failure to exhaust state remedies can be excused if the state court would not have considered the claim). Similarly, in this case, the tribal court of appeals' dismissal of Selam's appeal without a hearing may have rendered further exhaustion futile.

Moreover, the Ninth Circuit has held that a prisoner's failure to object to a magistrate judge's findings and recommendations does not necessarily waive their right to appeal. See Martinez v. Ylst, 951 F.2d 1153, 1156 (9th Cir. 1991) (holding that a prisoner's failure to object to a magistrate judge's findings and recommendations does not waive their right to appeal if the district court's order adopting the magistrate judge's recommendations is not a final order). In this case, Selam's failure to object to the magistrate judge's findings and recommendations may not have waived his Confrontation Clause claim.

Furthermore, the Confrontation Clause claim is closely tied to the compulsory process claim, and the two issues are intertwined. See United States v. Valenzuela-Bernal, 458 U.S. 858, 867 (1982) (noting that the right to compulsory process and the right to confrontation are "closely related" and "often overlap"). Therefore, the district court should have considered the Confrontation Clause claim in conjunction with the compulsory process claim.

Additionally, the Supreme Court has held that the Confrontation Clause guarantees a defendant's right to confront witnesses against them. See Crawford v. Washington, 541 U.S. 36, 51 (2004) (holding that the Confrontation Clause applies to "testimonial" statements). In this case, the five-year-old victim did not testify, and instead, her statements were introduced through four witnesses. This raises concerns about whether Selam was denied his right to confrontation.

The ICRA's Confrontation Clause provision, 25 U.S.C. § 1302(6), is analogous to the Sixth Amendment's Confrontation Clause. See Santa Clara Pueblo v. Martinez, 436 U.S. 49, 62-63 (1978) (noting that the ICRA's provisions are modeled after the Bill of Rights). Therefore, the district court should have applied the same principles in evaluating Selam's Confrontation Clause claim.

In conclusion, the district court erred in denying Selam's habeas corpus petition on the grounds that he was not denied compulsory process and that he waived his Confrontation Clause claim. The tribal court's failure to facilitate Selam's access to witnesses and the introduction of the five-year-old victim's statements through hearsay witnesses raise serious concerns about Selam's rights under the ICRA.
