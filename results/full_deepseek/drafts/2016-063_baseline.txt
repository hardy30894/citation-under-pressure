**ARGUMENT**

**THE D.C. CIRCUIT ERRED IN HOLDING THAT KLOECKNER DOES NOT ABROGATE THE DISTINCTION BETWEEN JURISDICTIONAL AND PROCEDURAL DISMISSALS FOR PURPOSES OF JUDICIAL REVIEW FORUM.**

The D.C. Circuit concluded that *Kloeckner v. Solis*, 568 U.S. 41 (2012), did not effectively overrule its precedent in *Powell v. Department of Defense*, 158 F.3d 597 (D.C. Cir. 1998), which held that Federal Circuit review is proper when the MSPB dismisses a mixed-case appeal for lack of jurisdiction. That conclusion is irreconcilable with *Kloeckner*’s reasoning and the plain text of the governing statutes.

**A. *Kloeckner* Rejected Any Distinction Between Procedural and Jurisdictional Dismissals.**

In *Kloeckner*, the MSPB dismissed the petitioner’s mixed-case appeal as untimely—a procedural ground. The question presented was whether judicial review lies in district court or the Federal Circuit when the MSPB dismisses a mixed case on a procedural ground without reaching the merits. *Id.* at 44. The Court held that district court review is proper, reasoning that the statutory text of 5 U.S.C. §§ 7702 and 7703 “makes the district court the proper forum for judicial review whenever an employee brings a mixed case appealing an action to the MSPB and alleging discrimination—no matter how the MSPB disposes of the case.” *Id.* at 49.

The Court emphasized that the statute “divides mixed cases from other MSPB cases, and for mixed cases, it directs judicial review to the district court.” *Id.* at 51. Critically, the Court did not limit its holding to procedural dismissals. To the contrary, the unanimous opinion stated that an employee “may appeal a mixed case to the MSPB ... and then to a district court for review of the MSPB’s decision.” *Id.* at 48. Nothing in the text of § 7702 or § 7703 supports a carve-out for jurisdictional dismissals. The statute’s operative condition is whether the employee “has been affected by an action which the employee may appeal to the Merit Systems Protection Board.” 5 U.S.C. § 7702(a)(1)(A). That condition looks to the nature of the *action*, not the Board’s ultimate decision about its jurisdiction. When an employee alleges discrimination and challenges an adverse action on its face appealable to the Board, the case is a mixed case for jurisdictional purposes, regardless of whether the Board later dismisses the appeal for lack of jurisdiction. *See Kloeckner*, 568 U.S. at 49.

**B. The D.C. Circuit’s Reliance on a Procedural/Jurisdictional Distinction Is Unsupported by *Kloeckner*.**

The D.C. Circuit held that *Kloeckner* did not abrogate *Powell* because *Kloeckner* involved only procedural dismissals, whereas *Powell* involved jurisdictional dismissals. Pet. App. 14–15. But *Kloeckner* itself rejected that very reasoning. The Court framed the question broadly: whether the proper forum for judicial review of a dismissed mixed case depends on the *ground* for dismissal. *Id.* at 44, 49. The Court answered that question with a clear, categorical rule: the district court is the proper forum for all mixed cases, regardless of how the Board disposes of them. *Id.* at 49, 51.

The D.C. Circuit attempted to distinguish jurisdictional dismissals on the ground that, unlike procedural dismissals, they involve actions that were never “appealable to the MSPB” within the meaning of § 7702(a)(1)(A). Pet. App. 19. But this analysis conflates the concept of “appealability” with the Board’s ultimately erroneous jurisdictional determination. When an employee brings a mixed case alleging discrimination and challenging an adverse action—such as removal or suspension—the action is *prima facie* appealable. *See* 5 U.S.C. § 7512 (defining appealable actions). The Board’s post hoc determination that it lacks jurisdiction does not retroactively erase the fact that the employee “may appeal” such actions to the Board. *Kloeckner*, 568 U.S. at 48–49.

Moreover, *Kloeckner* cited with approval decisions from the Second and Tenth Circuits that equated procedural and jurisdictional dismissals for forum purposes. *Id.* at 46 n.3 (citing *Harms v. IRS*, 321 F.3d 1001, 1007–08 (10th Cir. 2003); *Downey v. Runyon*, 160 F.3d 139, 145–46 (2d Cir. 1998)). In *Harms*, the Tenth Circuit expressly held that “the forum for judicial review does not depend on the *ground* for dismissal—whether jurisdictional, procedural, or on the merits.” 321 F.3d at 1007 (emphasis added). *Kloeckner*’s reliance on *Harms* demonstrates that the Court intended to adopt that broader principle.

**C. The D.C. Circuit’s Distinction Creates Unworkable Uncertainty.**

The D.C. Circuit’s approach forces litigants and courts to engage in a preliminary jurisdictional inquiry—often intertwined with the merits of the discrimination claim—to determine the proper forum for appeal. In this very case, the MSPB’s jurisdictional determination rested on whether Perry’s retirement and suspension were “voluntary,” a question that overlaps substantially with his discrimination claims. Pet. App. 5–6. *Kloeckner* wisely avoided this confusion by adopting a bright-line rule: the district court is the proper forum for all mixed cases. *See* 568 U.S. at 51 (noting that “the statutes ‘make the district court the exclusive forum’ for review of mixed cases” (quoting *Ballard v. Tenn. Valley Auth.*, 768 F.2d 756, 759 (6th Cir. 1985))).

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the Court should hold that judicial review of MSPB decisions disposing of mixed cases on jurisdictional grounds lies in the district court, not the Federal Circuit.
