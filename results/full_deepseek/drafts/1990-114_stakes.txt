**ARGUMENT**

**I. The ADEA Does Not Apply to Appointed State Judges**

**A. Congress Did Not Clearly Express an Intent to Apply the ADEA to State Judges**

The ADEA defines “employee” to exclude “any person elected to public office in any State or political subdivision of any State by the qualified voters thereof, or any person chosen by such officer to be on such officer’s personal staff, or an appointee on the policymaking level or an immediate advisor with respect to the exercise of the constitutional or legal powers of the office.” 29 U.S.C. § 630(f). The question is whether appointed state judges fall within the “appointee on the policymaking level” exception.

In determining whether Congress intended to preempt state law in this area, this Court has required a clear statement. “In traditionally sensitive areas, such as legislation affecting the federal balance, the requirement of clear statement assures that the legislature has in fact faced, and intended to bring into issue, the critical matters involved in the judicial decision.” *United States v. Bass*, 404 U.S. 336, 349 (1971). The tenure of state judges is a matter of considerable importance to the states and has traditionally been left to them. The ADEA contains no clear statement that it applies to state judges. To the contrary, the statutory text excludes appointed policymakers.

**B. Appointed State Judges Are “Appointees on the Policymaking Level”**

The First Circuit has correctly held that “policymaking is indisputably a part of the function of judging to the extent that judging involves lawmaking to fill the interstices of authority found in constitutions, statutes, and precedents.” *EEOC v. Massachusetts*, 858 F.2d 52, 55 (1st Cir. 1988). Judges exercise discretion and judgment in resolving unsettled questions of law, and their decisions create binding precedent. In Missouri, judges also exercise supervisory and administrative responsibilities. *See* Mo. Const. art. V, § 4. These functions place them on the policymaking level.

The legislative history cited by petitioners does not compel a different result. The Joint Explanatory Statement’s instruction that the exemption be “construed narrowly” does not mean that judges are not policymakers. The state judiciary is an agency of state government, and the exemption applies to appointees at the highest levels of state government. The Eighth Circuit correctly rejected the argument that the exemption is limited to executive and legislative appointees.

**C. The Method of Selection Does Not Change the Analysis**

Petitioners argue that because they are initially appointed and then retained by election, they are not “appointees.” But the powers and responsibilities of appointed and elected judges are identical. The policymaking level is determined by the nature of the office, not the method of selection. As the Eighth Circuit observed, “[t]he powers and responsibilities of appointed and elected judges are identical and it is these powers and responsibilities that determine whether a judge is on the ‘policymaking level.’” *Gregory v. Ashcroft*, 898 F.2d 598, 604 n.5 (8th Cir. 1990). The fact that some judges are elected does not alter the policymaking character of the judicial office.

**II. The Mandatory Retirement Provision Does Not Violate the Equal Protection Clause**

**A. Rational Basis Review Applies**

Age is not a suspect class, and the right to state employment is not fundamental. “This Court’s decisions give no support to the proposition that a right of governmental employment per se is fundamental.” *Massachusetts Bd. of Retirement v. Murgia*, 427 U.S. 307, 313 (1976) (per curiam). Therefore, the mandatory retirement provision need only be rationally related to a legitimate state interest.

**B. The Provision Is Rationally Related to Legitimate State Interests**

Missouri has a legitimate interest in ensuring that its judges possess the mental and physical faculties necessary to perform their duties. It is rational to treat “judges differently from other officials on the ground that the work of judges makes unique and exacting demands on faculties that age tends to erode.” *Trafelet v. Thompson*, 594 F.2d 623, 627 (7th Cir. 1979). Mandatory retirement also reduces delays in the administration of justice caused by death or disabling illness, and avoids the “anguish, time, delay, expense, and embarrassment of the supervision and removal of older judges of failing competence.” *Apkin v. Treasurer and Receiver Gen.*, 401 Mass. 427, 517 N.E.2d 141, 146 (1988). These are legitimate rationales.

**C. The Age Seventy Line Is Reasonable**

The state could rationally conclude that a uniform retirement age is preferable to individualized assessment of each judge’s competence. The line at seventy is within the range of permissible judgment. The provision is not arbitrary.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Eighth Circuit should be affirmed.
