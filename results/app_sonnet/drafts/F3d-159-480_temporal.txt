# ARGUMENT

## I. Section 1951, As Applied, Exceeds Congress's Commerce Power

Congress may regulate activity substantially affecting interstate commerce, *NLRB v. Jones & Laughlin Steel Corp.*, 301 U.S. 1, 37 (1937), and may reach even local conduct that, aggregated with similar conduct nationwide, exerts a substantial economic effect, *Wickard v. Filburn*, 317 U.S. 111, 127-28 (1942). Even under this broad reading, however, the government must still demonstrate the effect on commerce that the statute demands. In *Stirone v. United States*, 361 U.S. 212, 215 (1960), this Court held that a Hobbs Act conviction must rest on proof of "some effect" on interstate commerce, and that the indictment and proof must actually establish the interstate nexus charged. Here, the only connection to interstate commerce was that a single local sandwich shop purchased some out-of-state goods and that robbery proceeds "would have been used" to buy such goods. This attenuated, speculative link does not satisfy *Stirone*'s requirement of a demonstrated effect on commerce; permitting such a showing to sustain federal jurisdiction over what is otherwise a garden-variety local robbery and homicide would erase the distinction between local and national concern that *Jones & Laughlin* presupposes.

## II. The District Court Erred in Excluding Mr. Pearson's Alibi Evidence

Exclusion of Mr. Pearson's timely-offered alibi testimony violated his right to present a defense. "The right to offer the testimony of witnesses, and to compel their attendance, if necessary, is in plain terms the right to present a defense." *Washington v. Texas*, 388 U.S. 14, 19 (1967). This right is "a fundamental element of due process of law." *Id.* at 19. Whatever procedural default may have occurred, the sanction of total exclusion swept aside testimony corroborated by an independent watch-store receipt and an unaffiliated employee's recollection—evidence bearing precisely on the identity of the perpetrator, the central issue at trial. Due process requires that discovery or notice sanctions not be applied so rigidly as to deprive a defendant of material, corroborated defense evidence going to the heart of the charge. *Cf. In re Oliver*, 333 U.S. 257, 273 (1948) (due process guarantees a "meaningful opportunity to defend").

## III. The Evidence Was Insufficient to Sustain the Convictions

On review, this Court asks whether there is substantial evidence, direct or circumstantial, together with the reasonable inferences to be drawn therefrom, from which a jury might find guilt beyond a reasonable doubt. *Glasser v. United States*, 315 U.S. 60, 80 (1942); *Curley v. United States*, 160 F.2d 229, 232 (D.C. Cir. 1947) (verdict must be sustained if there is substantial evidence, taking the view most favorable to the government, to support it—but the evidence must be more than "mere speculation"). Here, the identification of Mr. Pearson as a participant rests almost entirely on Ms. Starks's in-court identification, which followed a failed photo-array attempt and prior television exposure to Mr. Pearson's photograph, and which occurred in a courtroom where Mr. Pearson was the only black male of appropriate age. Such procedures raise the very due-process concerns this Court addressed in *Simmons v. United States*, 390 U.S. 377, 383 (1968), where identification testimony tainted by unnecessarily suggestive circumstances was held to create "a very substantial likelihood of irreparable misidentification." *Id.* at 384. Coupled with Ms. Starks's contrary identification of Mr. Martin as the armed robber, and Mr. Pearson's corroborated alibi evidence, the remaining testimony—chiefly that of an accomplice with an obvious incentive to cooperate—does not constitute the substantial evidence *Glasser* and *Curley* require.

## IV. The District Court Misinstructed the Jury on Felony Murder

A defendant may be held liable for a killing committed by a confederate during a felony only where the government proves the elements of accomplice or conspirator liability recognized by this Court. Under *Pinkerton v. United States*, 328 U.S. 640, 646-47 (1946), a co-conspirator is liable for reasonably foreseeable acts of confederates done in furtherance of the conspiracy; under *Nye & Nissen v. United States*, 336 U.S. 613, 619 (1949), aiding-and-abetting liability requires that the defendant "in some sort associate himself with the venture" and seek by his action to make it succeed. The instruction given here did not require the jury to find that Mr. Pearson associated himself with the specific venture that produced Ms. Montgomery's death, nor that the shooting was a foreseeable consequence of any agreement to which he was a party, effectively permitting conviction without the mental-state findings *Pinkerton* and *Nye & Nissen* demand.

## V. The District Court Erred in Refusing Mr. Pearson's Lesser-Included-Offense Instructions

A defendant is entitled to an instruction on a lesser offense where the evidence would permit a rational jury to find the defendant guilty of the lesser but not the greater offense. *Sansone v. United States*, 380 U.S. 343, 349 (1965); *Berra v. United States*, 351 U.S. 131, 134 (1956). Given the conflicting testimony regarding Mr. Pearson's role—if any—in the underlying robbery, the jury should have been permitted to consider whether Mr. Pearson was guilty only of a lesser offense rather than facing the binary choice presented at trial. The refusal to give the requested instructions, under *Sansone*, requires reversal.
