In Canizio v. New York, 327 U.S. 82 (1946), the Court held that a state court's failure to advise a defendant of his right to counsel did not necessarily render the proceedings invalid. Similarly, in this case, the District Court's failure to advise Peguero of his right to appeal did not prejudice him, as he was already aware of that right.

The purpose of Rule 32(a)(2) is to ensure that defendants are informed of their appellate rights. However, when a defendant is already aware of those rights, the rule's purpose is not served by vacating the sentence. As this Court noted in Gryger v. Burke, 334 U.S. 728 (1948), "the sentence is not rendered invalid by the mere fact that a procedural error occurred." Here, Peguero's awareness of his right to appeal renders the District Court's error harmless.

Furthermore, this Court has consistently held that habeas relief is not available when a defendant has not been prejudiced by a procedural error. In Hunter v. Martin, 334 U.S. 302 (1948), the Court held that a prisoner was not entitled to habeas relief when the state sentence had expired, and the federal sentence was being served. Similarly, in this case, Peguero's awareness of his right to appeal and his decision not to exercise it mean that he was not prejudiced by the District Court's error.

The District Court's failure to advise him of that right did not affect the outcome.

O'GRADY, 312 U.S. 329 (1941), the Court noted that a defendant's ignorance of their rights can be a factor in determining whether a procedural error is prejudicial. Conversely, Peguero's awareness of his right to appeal means that the District Court's error did not prejudice him.

Oklahoma, 358 U.S. 576 (1959), the Court held that a defendant's sentence was not rendered invalid by the introduction of evidence that was not relevant to the crime for which he was being sentenced.
