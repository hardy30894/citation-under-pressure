# ARGUMENT

## THE OCSLA'S PLAIN TEXT EXTENDS COVERAGE TO ANY INJURY "RESULTING FROM" OPERATIONS ON THE SHELF, WITHOUT REGARD TO WHERE THE INJURY OCCURS

Section 1333(b) extends Longshore Act coverage to disability or death "resulting from any injury occurring as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf." 43 U.S.C. § 1333(b). This Court's task begins and ends with that text. As the Court has long recognized, "there is, of course, no more persuasive evidence of the purpose of a statute than the words by which the legislature undertook to give expression to its wishes." *United States v. American Trucking Ass'ns*, 310 U.S. 534, 543 (1940).

The statute's causal chain is unambiguous. Congress required that the *operations* occur "on the outer Continental Shelf"—it did not require that the *injury* occur there. Congress knew how to impose a situs requirement when it wished to do so. In the very same statute, § 1333(a)(2)(A) applies state law as surrogate federal law specifically to "the subsoil and seabed of the outer Continental Shelf, and artificial islands and fixed structures erected thereon." 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(2)(A). Section 1333(b) contains no such geographic limitation on the situs of injury; it speaks only to the situs of operations. Where "Congress includes particular language in one section of a statute but omits it in another," courts presume that the omission is intentional. Cf. *Russell Motor Car Co. v. United States*, 261 U.S. 514, 519 (1923) (statutory language must be given its ordinary meaning absent contrary indication).

## PETITIONER'S RELIANCE ON DICTUM IN *TALLENTIRE* IS MISPLACED

Petitioner will invoke a footnote from this Court's decision addressing an entirely different provision of the OCSLA. But that footnote does not control here, for the issue of § 1333(b)'s scope was neither presented, briefed, nor necessary to that decision.

This Court has consistently instructed that "general expressions, in every opinion, are to be taken in connection with the case in which those expressions are used[;] . . . their possible bearing on all other cases is seldom completely investigated." *Cohens v. Virginia*, 19 U.S. (6 Wheat.) 264, 399-400 (1821). This principle was reaffirmed in *Humphrey's Executor v. United States*, 295 U.S. 602, 627 (1935), where the Court cautioned against treating passing statements as binding precedent when the underlying question was not actually before the Court.

The distinction between holding and dictum is not a mere technicality—it reflects the reality that unconsidered statements lack the benefit of adversarial testing. As Chief Justice Marshall explained in *Cohens*, judicial statements made without the benefit of full argument on the precise question "are not entitled to the same respect" as considered holdings. 19 U.S. at 399-400. Any statement regarding § 1333(b)'s situs requirements in a case addressing only § 1333(a)(2)(A)'s choice-of-law provision falls squarely within this category of unconsidered dictum.

## THE OCSLA'S REMEDIAL PURPOSE CONFIRMS THAT NO SITUS-OF-INJURY REQUIREMENT SHOULD BE READ INTO THE STATUTE

The OCSLA was enacted to fill a jurisdictional void and ensure that workers engaged in the hazardous enterprise of extracting resources from the outer Continental Shelf would receive compensation for their injuries. This Court has long recognized that workers' compensation statutes are to be construed liberally in favor of coverage, consistent with their remedial purposes. See *Voris v. Eikel*, 346 U.S. 328, 333 (1953) (Longshore Act "must be liberally construed in conformance with its purpose, and in a way which avoids harsh and incongruous results").

A situs-of-injury requirement would produce precisely the "harsh and incongruous results" this Court has cautioned against. A worker injured while performing shelf-related duties—loading equipment destined for the platform, transporting extracted materials, or performing tasks integral to shelf operations—would be arbitrarily denied coverage based solely on the fortuity of his physical location at the moment of injury, even where the operations causing that injury were conducted entirely on the shelf.

This Court has recognized that statutory schemes governing maritime and extractive industries must be interpreted with attention to the practical realities of the industries they regulate. See *O'Leary v. Brown-Pacific-Maxon, Inc.*, 340 U.S. 504, 506-07 (1951) (workmen's compensation coverage should extend to injuries arising from the "obligations or conditions of employment," construed broadly to serve the statute's protective purpose). The extractive operations Congress sought to cover in § 1333(b) frequently require integrated onshore-offshore activities, including transportation of personnel, equipment, and extracted resources. A rule tethering coverage to the precise geographic point of injury—rather than to the causal relationship between the injury and shelf operations—would frustrate rather than serve Congress's evident purpose.

## CONCLUSION

The statute's text draws no distinction based on the situs of injury; it requires only that the injury result from operations conducted on the shelf. This construction is compelled by the statutory language itself, is unaffected by inapposite dictum addressing a different provision, and honors the remedial purposes underlying the Act's protection of workers engaged in shelf-related extractive operations.
