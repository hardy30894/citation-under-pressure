ARGUMENT

The Third Circuit erred in holding that a grant of Temporary Protected Status (TPS) under 8 U.S.C. § 1254a does not constitute an "admission" into the United States under 8 U.S.C. § 1255. A straightforward reading of the relevant statutes, informed by the principles of statutory interpretation and the relevant case law, demonstrates that TPS recipients are eligible for adjustment of status.

I. The Statutory Text and Structure Support the Petitioners' Interpretation

The INA defines "admission" and "admitted" as "the lawful entry of the alien into the United States after inspection and authorization by an immigration officer." 8 U.S.C. § 1101(a)(13)(A). However, the statute also provides that an alien granted TPS "shall be considered as being in, and maintaining, lawful status as a nonimmigrant" for purposes of adjustment of status under § 1255. 8 U.S.C. § 1254a(f)(4). The Ninth Circuit correctly held in Ramirez v. Brown, 852 F.3d 954, 960 (9th Cir. 2017), that this provision effectively treats TPS recipients as if they had been admitted for purposes of § 1255.

The Third Circuit's contrary holding relies on a narrow reading of the term "admission" that ignores the broader statutory context. As the Sixth Circuit noted in Flores v. USCIS, 718 F.3d 548, 554 (6th Cir. 2013), the INA often uses the terms "admission" and "status" interchangeably, suggesting that Congress intended to treat TPS recipients as having been admitted.

Furthermore, the structure of § 1255 supports the petitioners' interpretation. Section 1255(k) provides an exception to the "inspected and admitted or paroled" requirement for certain aliens, including those who are "present in the United States pursuant to a lawful admission." 8 U.S.C. § 1255(k)(1). The fact that Congress used the phrase "lawful admission" in § 1255(k) rather than "inspected and admitted or paroled" suggests that the two phrases are not identical, and that "lawful status" under § 1254a(f)(4) can satisfy the admission requirement.

II. Relevant Case Law Supports the Petitioners' Interpretation

The Third Circuit's holding is inconsistent with the decisions of other circuits, including the Sixth and Ninth Circuits. In Flores, the Sixth Circuit held that a grant of TPS constitutes an "admission" for purposes of § 1255, reasoning that the statute's language and structure support this interpretation. 718 F.3d at 554. Similarly, in Ramirez, the Ninth Circuit held that TPS recipients are eligible for adjustment of status under § 1255, citing the statutory text and the legislative history of the TPS program. 852 F.3d at 960-61.

Other cases have also recognized the importance of considering the broader statutory context when interpreting the INA. For example, in I.N.S. v. Cardoza-Fonseca, 480 U.S. 421, 432 (1987), the Supreme Court noted that the INA's provisions should be interpreted in light of their statutory context and the legislative history of the Act. See also Hibbs v. Winn, 542 U.S. 88, 101 (2004) (statutes should be construed to give effect to all their provisions).

The Third Circuit's reliance on Hanif v. Att'y Gen., 694 F.3d 479, 485 (3d Cir. 2012), and Taveras v. Att'y Gen., 731 F.3d 281, 290 (3d Cir. 2013), is misplaced. Those cases involved different statutory provisions and did not address the specific issue presented here. In contrast, the Sixth and Ninth Circuits' decisions in Flores and Ramirez directly addressed the question of whether a grant of TPS constitutes an "admission" under § 1255.

III. The Purpose of the TPS Program Supports the Petitioners' Interpretation

The TPS program was created to provide temporary relief to aliens who are unable to return to their home countries due to extraordinary conditions. 8 U.S.C. § 1254a(b)(1). Allowing TPS recipients to adjust their status under § 1255 is consistent with this purpose, as it provides a pathway to permanent residence for individuals who have been living in the United States for extended periods.

The Third Circuit's holding, on the other hand, would undermine the purpose of the TPS program by creating a barrier to adjustment of status for TPS recipients who entered the United States without inspection or admission. As the Ninth Circuit noted in Ramirez, this would "yield an anomalous result" by denying relief to individuals who have been living in the United States for years. 852 F.3d at 962.

In Serrano v. Att'y Gen., 655 F.3d 1260 (11th Cir. 2011) (per curiam), the Eleventh Circuit held that an alien with TPS is not exempt from § 1255(a)'s admission requirement. However, that decision is distinguishable from the present case, as it involved a different statutory provision and did not address the specific issue presented here.

In conclusion, a grant of TPS under 8 U.S.C. § 1254a constitutes an "admission" into the United States under 8 U.S.C. § 1255. The statutory text and structure, relevant case law, and the purpose of the TPS program all support this interpretation. The Third Circuit's contrary holding should be reversed.
