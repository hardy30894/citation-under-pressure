# ARGUMENT

## I. An Inherited IRA Is Not "Retirement Funds" Within the Meaning of § 522 Because It No Longer Serves the Purpose the Exemption Was Designed to Protect

Congress crafted the retirement-fund exemption to serve the same end that animates every exemption in the Bankruptcy Code: preserving for the individual debtor the means to begin life anew, not to shelter windfalls that pass from one generation to the next. This Court has long recognized that the exemption provisions of the bankruptcy laws exist "to give to the honest but unfortunate debtor...a new opportunity in life and a clear field for future effort, unhampered by the pressure and discouragement of preexisting debt." *Local Loan Co. v. Hunt*, 292 U.S. 234, 244 (1934). That purpose is personal to the debtor. It has nothing to do with permitting an heir to receive, income-tax-deferred and creditor-free, a lump sum that she is legally compelled to draw down and spend well before her own retirement.

This Court's decisions construing bankruptcy exemptions have consistently examined the *function* an asset serves for the debtor, not merely its historical label. In *Burlingham v. Crouse*, 228 U.S. 459 (1913), the Court explained that exemption statutes are designed "to protect the unfortunate debtor and enable him to begin his business life anew," and looked to whether the property in question actually served that protective purpose for the person claiming it. So too in *Holden v. Stratton*, 198 U.S. 202 (1905), the Court did not treat an insurance policy as exempt simply because it bore the label "insurance"; it examined the nature of the interest the bankrupt actually held. The inquiry has always been substantive, not nominal.

That substantive inquiry defeats the Clarks' claim. An inherited IRA is, by operation of the Internal Revenue Code, incapable of serving as a retirement fund for its non-spousal beneficiary. No further contributions may be made, and the entire balance must be distributed on an accelerated schedule that bears no relationship to the beneficiary's retirement date. Whatever protective purpose an IRA serves for its original owner, that purpose is extinguished the moment the account passes, by inheritance, to someone who is required to drain it during her working life.

## II. Retaining the Label "IRA" Cannot Substitute for the Retirement Function the Exemption Requires

Petitioners' position rests on the premise that because the tax code calls the account an "inherited individual retirement account," it must retain the character of retirement funds regardless of how it functions in the beneficiary's hands. This Court rejected an analogous argument in *Gregory v. Helvering*, 293 U.S. 465 (1935), refusing to let the form of a transaction override its economic substance, for "[t]o hold otherwise would be to exalt artifice above reality and to deprive the statutory provision in question of all serious purpose." *Id.* at 470. The same principle governs here. Calling a mandatory, non-rolloverable, rapidly-depleting account an "IRA" does not restore to it the retirement-savings substance that Congress required as the predicate for the § 522 exemption.

Nor should this Court adopt a literalistic reading of "retirement funds" that produces a result plainly at odds with Congress's design. Where a literal reading of statutory text would frustrate the evident statutory purpose, this Court has looked past the bare words to the object the legislature sought to achieve. *United States v. American Trucking Ass'ns*, 310 U.S. 534 (1940); *United States v. Missouri Pacific R. Co.*, 278 U.S. 269 (1929). Reading § 522 to exempt inherited IRAs would permit exactly the result Congress sought to avoid when it limited the tax deferral available to non-spousal beneficiaries in the first place—transforming a temporary tax-deferral vehicle into a permanent, creditor-proof reservoir of wealth that can pass from generation to generation without ever being used for retirement or, for that matter, ever being subjected to creditors' legitimate claims.

## III. The Character of the Funds Must Be Assessed as of the Petition Date, Not by Reference to Their History in the Decedent's Hands

Bankruptcy exemptions are fixed by the character of the debtor's interest as of the date the petition is filed. *White v. Stump*, 266 U.S. 310, 313 (1924) (exemption law "must be applied as of" the filing date); *Burlingham v. Crouse*, 228 U.S. 459 (1913). This Court's decision in *Segal v. Rochelle*, 382 U.S. 375 (1966), confirms that what matters is the debtor's actual interest at the time of filing, assessed for what it truly is in the debtor's hands—not what it once represented for a predecessor. By the time the Clarks filed their petition, the funds in the inherited IRA were not retirement savings belonging to Heidi Heffron-Clark; they were, in her hands, a mandatory-payout asset available for present consumption. That the funds were once Ruth Heffron's retirement savings is irrelevant under the temporal rule this Court has consistently applied.

## CONCLUSION

For these reasons, the judgment of the Seventh Circuit should be affirmed.
