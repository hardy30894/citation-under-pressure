**ARGUMENT**

The judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed. The district court's failure to advise petitioner of his right to appeal under Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 32(a)(2) was harmless error because petitioner knew of that right and deliberately chose not to appeal. Habeas relief under 28 U.S.C. § 2255 requires a showing of actual prejudice, and petitioner cannot make that showing.

**I. The Rule 32 Violation Is Subject to Harmless-Error Review**

Rule 32(a)(2) is a procedural rule, not a constitutional command. Nonconstitutional errors are harmless unless they "affect substantial rights." *Kotteakos v. United States*, 328 U.S. 750, 764-65 (1946). Even constitutional errors may be harmless if the government proves beyond a reasonable doubt that the error did not contribute to the verdict. *Chapman v. California*, 386 U.S. 18, 22 (1967). *A fortiori*, a purely procedural omission is subject to harmless-error review.

This Court applied that principle to Rule 32 in *Hill v. United States*, 368 U.S. 424, 428 (1962), holding that a trial court's failure to ask a defendant whether he wished to speak before sentencing—a violation of Rule 32(a)—was not "a fundametal defect which inherently results in a complete miscarriage of justice" and could not be raised for the first time in a §2255 motion. The same reasoning governs here. The failure to advise a defendant of his right to appeal is no more fundamental than the failure to invite allocuton.

**II. Petitioner Cannot Show the Prejudice Required for Habeas Relief**

Section 2255 is not a substitute for direct appeal. *United States v. Morgan*, 346 U.S. 502, 512 (1954). A prisoner seeking collateral relief must demonstrate that the alleged error deprived him of a right guaranteed by federal law. *Fay v. Noia*, 372 U.S. 391, 439 (1963). Petitioner cannot do so.

The district court found, after an evidentiary hearing, that petitioner knew of his right to appeal at sentencing. That finding is not clearly erroneous. Because petitioner had actual knowledge, the court's failure to repeat the advice did not deprive him of any opportunity. He could have appealed; he chose not to. That choice was a knowing and voluntary waiver. *Johnson v. Zerbst*, 304 U.S. 458, 464 (1938) (waiver is "an intentional relinquishment or abandonment of a known right or privilege").

Petitioner's claim is also barred by his failure to pursue a direct appeal. A defendant who knowingly declines to appeal cannot later use a procedural omission to obtain collateral relief. *Sunal v. Large*, 332 U.S. 174, 178 (1947) (a motion to vacate cannot be used "to review questions which could have been raised on appeal"). And a right may be forfeited by failure to assert it at the proper time. *Yakus v. United States*, 321 U.S. 414, 444 (1944). Petitioner knew his right and forfeited it by his own deliberate choice.

**III. The Purpose of Rule 32 Was Fully Satisfied**

Rule 32(a)(2) exists to ensure that a defendant is informed of his right to appeal. When the defendant already knows of that right, the rule's purpose is served. Procedural rules are "devised to promote the ends of justice, not to defeat them." *United States v. Hark*, 320 U.S. 531, 536 (1944). To vacate a sentence because a court failed to recite advice the defendant already possessed would exalt form over substance and defeat the rule's purpose.

Petitioner admitted that he knew he could appeal. The district court credited that testimony. The omission had no effect on his decision. It did not cause him to miss an appeal he would otherwise have taken. It did not prejudice him in any way.

**IV. The Courts Below Correctly Applied Harmless-Error Review**

The court of appeals held that the Rule 32 violation was subject to harmess-error review and that the error was harmess because petitioner knew of his right. That conclusion is consistent with *Kotteakos*, *Chapman*, and *Hill*. The district court's factual finding of actual knowledge is dispositive. Because petitioner suffered no prejudice, there was no fundamental defect warranting collateral relief. *Hill*, 368 U.S. at 428.

Petitioner asks this Court to hold that any violation of Rule 32, regardless of the defendant's knowledge, requires vacatur of a sentence. Such a rule would convert a procedural safeguard into a technical trap, rewarding defendants who knowingly decline to appeal and later seek a second chance. It would also ignore the settled principle that habeas relief is reserved for actual prejudice, not form error.

For these reasons, the judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
