The Equal Protection Clause does not give rise to a cause of action on behalf of a "class of one." The Court of Appeals erred in holding that a plaintiff can allege an equal protection violation by asserting that state action was motivated solely by a "spiteful effort to 'get' him for reasons wholly unrelated to any legitimate state objective."

The Equal Protection Clause is traditionally understood to protect against discrimination based on membership in a particular class or group. See, e.g., Brown v. Board of Education, 347 U.S. 483 (1954) (striking down segregation in public schools); Skinner v. Oklahoma, 316 U.S. 535 (1942) (invalidating a statute that authorized sterilization of certain classes of criminals). The Clause is not intended to constitutionalize a general principle of "equal treatment" that would allow individuals to challenge government actions as irrational or arbitrary.

The Court of Appeals' "class of one" theory is unsupported by precedent. In fact, the Supreme Court has consistently held that the Equal Protection Clause is concerned with protecting against discrimination based on class membership or status. See, e.g., McGowan v. Maryland, 366 U.S. 420 (1961) (upholding a Sunday closing law against an equal protection challenge, noting that the law did not discriminate against a particular class); Williamson v. Lee Optical Co., 348 U.S. 483 (1955) (rejecting an equal protection challenge to a statute regulating opticians, noting that the legislature could have rationally concluded that the statute was necessary to protect public health).

Furthermore, allowing "class of one" claims would effectively constitutionalize a vast array of government decisions that are traditionally left to the discretion of state and local authorities. See, e.g., City of Cleburne v. Cleburne Living Center, 473 U.S. 432 (1985) (noting that government decisions are often based on a multitude of factors, and that the Equal Protection Clause is not a "license for courts to judge the wisdom, fairness, or logic" of those decisions).

In this case, Olech's claim that the Village's demand for an additional 18-foot easement was motivated by a "spiteful effort" to harm her is not cognizable under the Equal Protection Clause. The Village's decision to require a larger easement from Olech may have been irrational or arbitrary, but it does not rise to the level of a constitutional violation.

The judgment of the Court of Appeals should be reversed, and the case should be remanded for further proceedings.
