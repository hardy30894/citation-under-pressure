The federal courts have exclusive jurisdiction over Minton's state-based legal malpractice claim because it necessarily depends on the resolution of a substantial question of federal patent law. Under 28 U.S.C. § 1338(a), federal courts have exclusive jurisdiction over cases "arising under" federal patent law. The Supreme Court has construed this language to extend to cases where a state-law claim necessarily depends on the resolution of a substantial question of federal patent law. Christianson v. Colt Indus. Operating Corp., 486 U.S. 800, 808-09 (1988).

The Grable test, as articulated by the Supreme Court, provides a four-part inquiry to determine whether a state-law claim arises under federal jurisdiction: (1) whether resolving a federal issue is necessary to resolution of the state-law claim; (2) whether the federal issue is actually disputed; (3) whether the federal issue is substantial; and (4) whether federal jurisdiction will disturb the balance of federal and state judicial responsibilities. Grable & Sons Metal Prods., Inc. v. Darue Eng'g & Mfg., 545 U.S. 308, 314 (2005). 

Here, Minton's legal malpractice claim against Gunn necessarily depends on the resolution of a substantial question of federal patent law, specifically the experimental use exception to the on-sale bar under 35 U.S.C. § 102(b). The experimental use exception is a critical element of Minton's malpractice claim, as it directly impacts the determination of whether Gunn's alleged negligence proximately caused Minton's damages. See Alexander v. Turtur & Assocs., 146 S.W.3d 113, 117 (Tex. 2004). The resolution of this federal patent issue is necessary to determine the merits of Minton's state-law claim.

Furthermore, the federal issue is actually disputed, as the parties disagree on the application of the experimental use exception to the facts of the case. The issue is also substantial, as it involves a critical aspect of patent law that is central to the malpractice claim. See Singh v. Duane Morris LLP, 538 F.3d 334, 338 (5th Cir. 2008).

Finally, exercising federal jurisdiction over this case will not disturb the balance of federal and state judicial responsibilities. The federal courts have a strong interest in maintaining uniformity in the application of patent law, and resolving this issue in federal court will ensure consistency with federal patent law jurisprudence. See Grable, 545 U.S. at 313-14.

The Federal Circuit's decisions in Air Measurement Technologies, Inc. v. Akin Gump Strauss Hauer & Feld, L.L.P., 504 F.3d 1262 (Fed. Cir. 2007), and Immunocept, LLC v. Fulbright & Jaworski, LLP, 504 F.3d 1281 (Fed. Cir. 2007), support this conclusion. Although not binding on this Court, these decisions demonstrate that the Federal Circuit has consistently held that federal courts have exclusive jurisdiction over state-law claims that necessarily depend on the resolution of substantial patent law issues.

In conclusion, Minton's state-based legal malpractice claim arises under exclusive federal patent law jurisdiction. The Court should reverse the court of appeals' judgment and dismiss the case for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction.
