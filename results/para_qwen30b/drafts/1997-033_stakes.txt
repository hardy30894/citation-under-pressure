**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a federal district court conducting “pretrial proceedings” under 28 U.S.C. § 1407(a) may invoke § 1404(a) to assign a transferred case to itself for trial—requires a straightforward application of statutory text and settled precedent. The answer is no. The Ninth Circuit’s decision to deny mandamus relief and uphold the District of Arizona’s transfer of this case to itself for trial rests on a fundamental misreading of the statutory framework established by Congress in the Multidistrict Litigation Act. The court’s conclusion that § 1404(a) empowers a transferee district court to assume trial jurisdiction over a case after pretrial proceedings are complete is not only contrary to the plain language of § 1407(a), but also undermines a core policy of the statute: the preservation of the plaintiff’s right to trial in the original forum.

Congress enacted § 1407(a) to centralize pretrial proceedings in multidistrict litigation (MDL) for the sake of judicial economy and consistency. The statute explicitly authorizes the Judicial Panel on Multidistrict Litigation (JPML) to transfer civil actions “to any district for coordinated or consolidated pretrial proceedings,” but it further mandates that “each action so transferred shall be remanded by the panel” at or before the conclusion of those proceedings. 28 U.S.C. § 1407(a). This language is not merely directory—it is mandatory. The word “shall” imposes a binding obligation on the JPML to remand the case to the original district for trial. The statute does not grant the transferee court any authority to assume trial jurisdiction, nor does it authorize the court to transfer the case to itself under § 1404(a) after pretrial proceedings conclude.

The Ninth Circuit’s reliance on *In re Fine Paper Antitrust Litigation*, 685 F.2d 810 (3d Cir. 1982), and *Pfizer v. Lord*, 447 F.2d 122 (2d Cir. 1971), to justify the transferee court’s power to transfer the case to itself is misplaced. Those cases did not address the statutory command of § 1407(a) that a case must be remanded after pretrial proceedings. In *Pfizer*, the court acknowledged that “there is nothing in the language of 28 U.S.C. § 1407(a) or § 1404(a) which directly allows, or even suggests, that the transferee judge has the power to transfer cases to his district, or any district, for purposes of trial.” 342 F. Supp. 907, 909 (D.N.H. 1971). The court in *Fine Paper* similarly recognized the tension between the statute and the practice, but concluded that the “practical necessity” of avoiding delay justified the transfer. 685 F.2d at 820. These policy-based justifications, however, cannot override the clear statutory directive that the case “shall be remanded” by the JPML. As this Court has repeatedly held, “when the language of a statute is unambiguous, our inquiry ends.” *United States v. Ron Pair Enters., Inc.*, 489 U.S. 235, 242 (1989). Here, the language is unambiguous: remand is required.

The Ninth Circuit’s assertion that Multidistrict Litigation Rule 14(b) “explicitly authorizes” a transferee court to transfer the action to itself for trial is a mischaracterization of the rule. Rule 14(b) provides that “the transferee court may transfer the action to itself for trial,” but only “after the conclusion of pretrial proceedings.” J.P.M.L.R. 14(b). This rule, however, is not a grant of authority from Congress—it is a procedural rule adopted by the JPML under its delegated authority. The JPML may not, by rule, confer power that Congress has withheld. *United States v. Mead Corp.*, 533 U.S. 218, 226 (2001). The JPML’s authority is limited to “coordinated or consolidated pretrial proceedings,” and it may not, by rule, override the statutory mandate to remand. The rule’s existence does not transform an unlawful transfer into a lawful one.

Moreover, the Ninth Circuit’s conclusion that petitioners are not prejudiced because they have already been litigating in Arizona for over a year is legally irrelevant. The prejudice at issue is not the inconvenience of travel, but the deprivation of a fundamental right: the right to trial in the original forum. Congress has long recognized that “the plaintiff’s choice of forum is a vital component of the federal judicial system.” *Hilton v. Braunskill*, 481 U.S. 770, 776 (1987). The MDL statute does not eliminate that right; it merely suspends it temporarily for the purpose of pretrial coordination. Once pretrial proceedings are complete, the right to trial in the original district is restored. To allow a transferee court to assume trial jurisdiction is to permit a de facto permanent transfer, contrary to the statutory design.

The dissent’s invocation of *Wash. Pub. Util. Group v. U.S. Dist. Court*, 843 F.2d 319 (9th Cir. 1987), is also inapposite. That case upheld the transferee court’s authority to transfer a case under § 1404(a) *during* pretrial proceedings, not after they have concluded. The court in *WPPSS* did not address the remand requirement of § 1407(a), nor did it purport to authorize a transfer for trial. The dissent’s claim that the practice is “oft-repeated” and “illicit” is not a legal justification for continuing it. The law must be followed, not merely convenient.

This case presents a clear and important question of statutory interpretation. The Ninth Circuit’s decision permits a district court to circumvent a mandatory remand provision by invoking a general venue transfer statute. That result is not only contrary to the text of § 1407(a), but also undermines the policy of preserving the plaintiff’s right to trial in the original forum.
