**Argument**

The judgment below should be affirmed because this case became moot when Georgia Gwinnett College revised the challenged policies, and a request for nominal damages alone cannot preserve a live Article III controversy. Mootness turns on whether there remains “a live controversy at every stage of the litigation.” *Iron Arrow Honor Society v. Heckler*, 464 U.S. 67, 70 (1983). Once a defendant “unambiguously terminated” the challenged policy and there is no reasonable expectation it will recur, claims for prospective relief are moot. *Deakins v. Monaghan*, 484 U.S. 193, 200 (1988); *Iron Arrow Honor Society v. Heckler*, 464 U.S. at 71-72. That is precisely what happened here: GGC replaced the former speech policy with a new one allowing student speech across campus, and removed the challenged conduct-code provision. The Eleventh Circuit correctly held that those changes mooted appellants’ claims for declaratory and injunctive relief.

Petitioners nonetheless contend their nominal-damages request keeps the case alive. It does not. This Court has repeatedly explained that nominal damages are a symbolic remedy available when a plaintiff proves a completed constitutional violation, but they are not a freestanding basis to maintain jurisdiction after the underlying controversy has evaporated. In *Carey v. Piphus*, 435 U.S. 247, 266-67 (1978), the Court held that nominal damages may be awarded for the denial of procedural due process without proof of actual injury; but *Carey* was a merits decision about the remedy available for a past violation, not a mootness case. Likewise, in *Memphis Community School District v. Stachura*, 477 U.S. 299, 308-10 (1986), the Court rejected “the abstract value” of constitutional rights as a measure of compensatory damages and reiterated that damages must be tied to actual injury. And in *Farrar v. Hobby*, 506 U.S. 103, 112 (1992), the Court described nominal damages as appropriate where a plaintiff proves a violation but cannot show compensable harm. None of those decisions suggests that nominal damages convert a moot case into a live one.

Nor does the First Amendment setting alter the analysis. Public university students plainly enjoy constitutional speech rights. *Healy v. James*, 408 U.S. 169, 180 (1972); *Papish v. Board of Curators of the University of Missouri*, 410 U.S. 667, 670 (1973); *Widmar v. Vincent*, 454 U.S. 263, 269-70 (1981). But those cases addressed the validity of restrictions on speech, not whether a request for nominal damages alone can sustain jurisdiction after the challenged rule has been repealed. Once GGC abandoned the policy, there was no ongoing restraint to enjoin or declare unlawful. See *Board of Ed. of Westside Community Schools (Dist. 66) v. Mergens*, 496 U.S. 226, 253 (1990) (public schools may not discriminate against student speech based on content in a limited public forum); *Rumsfeld v. Forum for Academic and Institutional Rights, Inc.*, 547 U.S. 47, 60-61 (2006) (distinguishing speech claims from access claims). Petitioners’ reliance on abstract injury cannot supply Article III jurisdiction after the school’s voluntary cessation has eliminated the challenged policies.

Petitioners also fail to identify any concrete injury demanding adjudication after the policy change. The complaint, as the Eleventh Circuit held, did not plead compensatory damages supported by actual injury. Damages under 42 U.S.C. § 1983 require proof of injury caused by the defendant, not merely the deprivation of constitutional rights in the abstract. *Carey v. Piphus*, 435 U.S. at 254-57; *Stachura*, 477 U.S. at 308-09; *Slicker v. Jackson*, 215 F.3d 1225, 1230-31 (11th Cir. 2000). Because appellants did not plead a viable claim for compensatory relief, their case reduced to a moot request for prospective relief plus nominal damages.

Finally, the district court did not abuse its discretion in declining to treat appellants’ passing reference to “clarifying” their complaint as a proper motion to amend. A request for leave to amend embedded in an opposition memorandum is not properly presented. *Rosenberg v. Gould*, 554 F.3d 962, 967 (11th Cir. 2009); *Cita Trust Co. AG v. Fifth Third Bank*, 879 F.3d 1151, 1157 (11th Cir. 2018). And after judgment, amendment must proceed through Rule 59 or Rule 60. *Jacobs v. Tempur-Pedic International, Inc.*, 626 F.3d 1327, 1344 (11th Cir. 2010); *Czeremcha v. International Ass’n of Machinists & Aerospace Workers, AFL-CIO*, 724 F.2d 1552, 1556 (11th Cir. 1984). The court left appellants free to pursue that route; it simply was not required to keep a moot case open.

For these reasons, the judgment should be affirmed.
