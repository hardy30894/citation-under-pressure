**ARGUMENT**

I. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED BY ADMITTING OPINION TESTIMONY FROM A WITNESS WHO WAS NOT QUALIFIED TO GIVE EXPERT CAUSATION OPINION.

Rule 702 permits expert testimony only when the witness is “qualified as an expert by knowledge, skill, experience, training, or education.” Fed. R. Evid. 702; *Daubert v. Merrell Dow Pharmaceuticals, Inc.*, 509 U.S. 579, 589-95 (1993). A witness may not be presented as an expert merely because he observed the event or worked in the same field; the testimony must “assist the trier of fact” and rest on specialized knowledge. *Id.* at 591-92; *Kumho Tire Co. v. Carmichael*, 526 U.S. 137, 147-49 (1999).

Here, American Steamship’s witness was allowed to offer his opinion on the cause of the line’s parting even though the opinion was not expert testimony. The issue was whether the jury should hear a specialized causation opinion about a rope failure and the conditions under which the line parted. That type of opinion required a proper foundation showing expertise and reliable methodology. *Daubert*, 509 U.S. at 592-95. Because that foundation was absent, the district court abused its discretion in admitting the testimony. *General Electric Co. v. Joiner*, 522 U.S. 136, 141-43 (1997).

The error was not harmless. The cause of the line’s failure was central to liability, and the improperly admitted opinion went directly to the contested question whether the vessel and its equipment were unsafe. Where evidentiary error bears on a core liability issue, reversal is required unless the error did not affect substantial rights. Fed. R. Evid. 103(a); *General Electric Co.*, 522 U.S. at 142-43. Here, it plainly did.

II. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN DENYING COOK’S MOTION FOR JUDGMENT AS A MATTER OF LAW ON UNSEAWORTHINESS.

A shipowner owes an absolute duty to furnish a seaworthy vessel and appurtenances reasonably fit for their intended use. *Mitchell v. Trawler Racer, Inc.*, 362 U.S. 539, 550 (1960); *Mahnich v. Southern S.S. Co.*, 321 U.S. 96, 100-01 (1944). The duty is not dependent on fault; liability arises when the vessel, its gear, or equipment is not reasonably fit. *Usner v. Luckenbach Overseas Corp.*, 400 U.S. 494, 499-500 (1971). Whether the condition rendered the vessel unseaworthy is ordinarily a question for the jury, but where the evidence points one way and no rational factfinder could conclude otherwise, judgment as a matter of law is appropriate. Fed. R. Civ. P. 50; *Reeves v. Sanderson Plumbing Prods., Inc.*, 530 U.S. 133, 150-51 (2000).

The undisputed evidence established that Cook was injured when the line supporting him in a bos’n’s chair parted and he fell. A line used to suspend a crewman over the dock must be reasonably fit for that purpose. *Mitchell*, 362 U.S. at 550; *Mahnich*, 321 U.S. at 100-01. On these facts, no reasonable jury could find the vessel seaworthy once the supporting line failed in the course of the very operation for which it was employed. The district court therefore erred in refusing to direct judgment for Cook on unseaworthiness.

III. THE DISTRICT COURT DID NOT ERR IN SUBMITTING COMPARATIVE NEGLIGENCE TO THE JURY.

Under the Jones Act, contributory negligence does not bar recovery, but damages may be diminished in proportion to the plaintiff’s fault. 45 U.S.C. § 53; *Socony-Vacuum Oil Co. v. Smith*, 305 U.S. 424, 431-32 (1939); *Gautreaux v. Scurlock Marine, Inc.*, 107 F.3d 331, 339-40 (5th Cir. 1997) (en banc). A seaman has a duty to exercise ordinary prudence under the circumstances. *Gautreaux*, 107 F.3d at 339-40. The question is whether evidence permits a reasonable inference that the plaintiff’s conduct contributed to the injury. *Norfolk & Western Ry. Co. v. Ayers*, 538 U.S. 135, 161-62 (2003).

Here, there was evidence from which the jury could infer that Cook’s own conduct contributed to the accident. He was deicing the boom with a propane torch in freezing conditions while another crewman rigged the line. Because the accident occurred amid a sequence of actions involving Cook’s own handling of the rigging operation and equipment, the court properly allowed the jury to assess comparative fault. See *Gautreaux*, 107 F.3d at 339-40. The district court therefore correctly denied Cook’s Rule 50 motion on this issue.

IV. THE DISTRICT COURT DID NOT ERR IN ADMITTING EVIDENCE OF COOK’S ALCOHOL-RELATED AILMENTS.

Relevant evidence is admissible unless its probative value is substantially outweighed by unfair prejudice. Fed. R. Evid. 401, 402, 403; *Old Chief v. United States*, 519 U.S. 172, 180-83 (1997). Trial courts have broad discretion in applying Rule 403. *Sprint/United Management Co. v. Mendelsohn*, 552 U.S. 379, 384-85 (2008). Evidence may be admitted when it bears on damages, causation, life expectancy, employability, or alternative explanations for claimed injury. *Daubert*, 509 U.S. at 596-97.

The evidence of Cook’s alcohol-related ailments was admissible because it was relevant to the extent and cause of his claimed losses. The jury was entitled to hear evidence affecting Cook’s physical condition, credibility, and damages. The district court’s balancing under Rule 403 was not an abuse of discretion. *Old Chief*, 519 U.S. at 180-83; *Sprint/United*, 552 U.S. at 384-85.

For these reasons, the judgment should be vacated and the case remanded for a new trial.
