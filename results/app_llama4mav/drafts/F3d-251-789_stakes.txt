ARGUMENT

I. The District Court Erred in Finding that Gonzalez-Caballero Consented to the Removal and Retention of Danelsy

The Hague Convention on the Civil Aspects of International Child Abduction ("the Hague Convention") aims to prevent the wrongful removal or retention of children across international borders. The International Child Abduction Remedies Act ("the Act"), 42 U.S.C. §§ 11601-11610, incorporates the Hague Convention into United States law. Under the Hague Convention, a removal or retention is considered wrongful if it is in breach of the petitioner's rights of custody under the law of the country where the child was habitually resident. See Hague Convention, art. 3. However, a respondent may defend against a petition for return by establishing that the petitioner consented to the removal or retention. See Hague Convention, art. 13(a).

The district court found that Gonzalez-Caballero consented to Mena's removal and retention of Danelsy. However, this finding is clearly erroneous. The evidence presented at trial demonstrates that Gonzalez-Caballero's agreement to allow Mena to take Danelsy to the United States was conditional upon Mena's assistance in her immigration to the United States. See Friedrich v. Friedrich, 78 F.3d 1060, 1067 (6th Cir. 1996) (holding that a petitioner's consent must be "clear and unequivocal").

Gonzalez-Caballero's testimony reveals that she was uncertain about the terms of the agreement and that Mena's promises were not fulfilled. Moreover, the fact that Gonzalez-Caballero immediately sought assistance from Panamanian authorities and the police upon realizing that Mena had not contacted her as promised, and continued to pursue various avenues to recover Danelsy, undermines the district court's finding of consent. See Wanninger v. Wanninger, 850 F. Supp. 78, 81 (D. Mass. 1994) (noting that a petitioner's prompt action to recover the child is inconsistent with consent).

Furthermore, the district court's reliance on Mena's testimony regarding Gonzalez-Caballero's alleged consent is misplaced. Mena's account of the events is self-serving and lacks corroboration. In contrast, Gonzalez-Caballero's testimony, although inconsistent at times, is supported by her subsequent actions, which demonstrate a clear intent to maintain custody of Danelsy.

The district court's finding of consent is not supported by the record and is therefore clearly erroneous. See Anderson v. City of Bessemer City, 470 U.S. 564, 573 (1985) (holding that a finding is clearly erroneous when it is not supported by the record).

II. The District Court's Decision Should be Reversed

Given the clearly erroneous finding of consent, the district court's decision denying Gonzalez-Caballero's petition for return should be reversed. The Hague Convention requires that a child wrongfully removed or retained be returned to their country of habitual residence unless an exception applies. See Hague Convention, art. 12. As the district court's finding of consent was the basis for denying Gonzalez-Caballero's petition, reversal is warranted.

The judgment of the district court should be reversed, and the case remanded with instructions to grant Gonzalez-Caballero's petition for return.
