Minton’s malpractice claim does not fall within the federal courts’ exclusive patent jurisdiction. Section 1338(a) gives federal jurisdiction over civil actions “arising under” the patent laws, but the Supreme Court has long held that this phrase is narrow. 28 U.S.C. § 1338(a); Louisville & Nashville Railroad Co. v. Mottley, 211 U.S. 149, 152 (1908); Shulthis v. McDougal, 225 U.S. 561, 569 (1912); Gully v. First National Bank in Meridian, 299 U.S. 109, 112-13 (1936). A case does not arise under federal law merely because a federal issue appears somewhere in the dispute. It must appear on the face of a properly pleaded claim and be essential to the plaintiff’s right to relief. Mottley, 211 U.S. at 152; Smith v. Kansas City Title & Trust Co., 255 U.S. 180, 199-201 (1921).

That rule is decisive here. Minton sues his former lawyers for malpractice under Texas law. The elements of malpractice are supplied by state law, including duty, breach, causation, and damages. See Alexander v. Turtur & Associates, Inc., 146 S.W.3d 113, 117 (Tex. 2004). The patent issue enters only as part of the “case within a case” needed to prove causation. Id. But a state-law claim does not become a federal patent case merely because the plaintiff must show that he would have won the underlying patent suit if counsel had acted differently. Christianson v. Colt Industries Operating Corp., 486 U.S. 800, 808-09 (1988), makes that point clear: federal jurisdiction exists only where patent law creates the cause of action or where the plaintiff’s right to relief necessarily depends on resolution of a substantial question of patent law. Here, patent law does neither.

The Court’s pre-1970 cases reinforce that conclusion. In Hartell v. Tilghman, the Court rejected federal jurisdiction where the patent question was only incidental to the controversy. 99 U.S. 547, 549-50 (1879). In Excelsior Wooden Pipe Co. v. Pacific Bridge Co., the Court likewise explained that a suit does not arise under patent law simply because a patent is involved; the claim itself must be one arising under the patent laws. 185 U.S. 282, 287-88 (1902). And in The Fair v. Kohler Die & Specialty Co., the Court declined to treat a patent issue embedded in a broader dispute as sufficient to confer exclusive federal jurisdiction. 228 U.S. 22, 25-26 (1913). Those decisions show that the mere presence of a patent issue does not transform a state claim into a federal one.

Shulthis and Gully confirm the same point in broader federal-question doctrine. In Shulthis, the Court held that a suit does not arise under federal law unless it “really and substantially involves a dispute or controversy respecting the validity, construction, or effect” of federal law, “upon the determination of which the result depends.” 225 U.S. at 569. Gully repeated that the Court should not open the federal courthouse door by allowing a party to attach a federal issue to a state cause of action; jurisdiction exists only when the federal issue is substantial in the jurisdictional sense. 299 U.S. at 112-18. Minton’s claim fails that test because the federal patent question is not the source of his cause of action; it is only one evidentiary step in proving malpractice.

Nor does the federal issue become substantial merely because the underlying case involved a patent defense. In Tennessee v. Union & Planters’ Bank, the Court rejected jurisdiction where a federal question was not essential to the plaintiff’s own claim. 152 U.S. 454, 464-65 (1894). In Skelly Oil Co. v. Phillips Petroleum Co., the Court again refused to expand federal jurisdiction based on a federal issue that would arise only in response to the claim. 339 U.S. 667, 672-73 (1950). The same reasoning applies here. Whether Minton’s lawyers should have raised the experimental-use defense may require consulting patent law, but that does not mean the malpractice suit itself “arises under” the patent laws.

Exclusive federal jurisdiction would also upset the federal-state balance Congress preserved. State courts routinely adjudicate legal-malpractice suits, and nothing in § 1338 suggests Congress intended to strip them of that authority whenever the plaintiff’s proof requires a patent-law inquiry. The Court has cautioned against such jurisdictional expansion. Gully, 299 U.S. at 114-15; Shulthis, 225 U.S. at 570. If every malpractice case connected to a patent dispute belonged exclusively in federal court, state courts would be displaced from a traditional area of state authority without any clear congressional command.

Accordingly, because Minton’s complaint asserts a state-law malpractice claim and the patent issue is only incidental to proving it, the federal courts lack exclusive jurisdiction. The judgment should be reversed.
