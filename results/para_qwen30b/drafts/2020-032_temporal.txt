**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a constitutional challenge to a school policy seeking nominal damages is rendered moot when the policy is revised during litigation—must be answered in the affirmative. This Court has long recognized that a live controversy is a prerequisite to federal jurisdiction, and that the voluntary cessation of allegedly unlawful conduct, particularly when accompanied by a permanent policy change, extinguishes the need for judicial intervention. The precedent of *United States v. W.T. Grant Co.*, 345 U.S. 629 (1953), and *United States v. Carrol*, 354 U.S. 394 (1957), establishes that a case becomes moot when the challenged conduct has ceased and there is no reasonable expectation of recurrence. Here, Georgia Gwinnett College’s complete revision of its Freedom of Expression Policy—eliminating the speech zones and the permit requirement—constitutes a voluntary and permanent cessation of the challenged conduct, rendering the plaintiffs’ claims moot.

In *W.T. Grant Co.*, the Court held that a federal court lacks jurisdiction to issue a declaratory judgment when the challenged conduct has ceased and the defendant has taken affirmative steps to prevent its recurrence. The Court emphasized that “the case is moot when the issues presented are no longer live” and that “the controversy has ceased to exist.” 345 U.S. at 633. The same principle applies here. The policy that once restricted students to two designated speech zones has been entirely replaced with a regime allowing expressive activity anywhere on campus, except in limited circumstances. The college has not merely suspended enforcement; it has formally repealed the challenged provisions. This is not a temporary or conditional change, but a permanent and unambiguous revision. As the Court stated in *Carrol*, “a voluntary cessation of the challenged conduct, if complete and not merely temporary, is sufficient to render the case moot.” 354 U.S. at 398. The college’s action here meets that standard.

The plaintiffs’ claim for nominal damages does not revive the controversy. The Court has consistently held that nominal damages alone cannot sustain jurisdiction when the underlying injury has ceased. In *United States v. Munsingwear, Inc.*, 340 U.S. 36, 39 (1950), the Court explained that “a case becomes moot when the issues presented are no longer live” and that “the controversy has ceased to exist.” The Court further clarified that “the mere fact that a plaintiff seeks nominal damages does not prevent a case from becoming moot.” Id. at 40. The purpose of nominal damages is to vindicate a legal right, not to create a continuing controversy. As the Court observed in *United States v. L. A. Tucker Truck Lines, Inc.*, 344 U.S. 33, 37 (1952), “the right to nominal damages is not a substitute for a live controversy.” The plaintiffs here seek only nominal damages, and they have not alleged any continuing injury or threat of future enforcement. Their claim is not one of ongoing harm but of past violation, which is no longer actionable.

The plaintiffs’ argument that their case is distinguishable because it involves an *as-applied* challenge is without merit. The Court has long rejected the notion that an *as-applied* challenge can survive the cessation of the challenged conduct. In *United States v. W.T. Grant Co.*, the Court explicitly rejected the idea that a claim based on past enforcement could survive if the conduct had ceased. The Court stated: “The fact that the defendant has ceased the challenged conduct is not a defense to the claim, but it does render the claim moot.” 345 U.S. at 633. The same logic applies here. The plaintiffs were stopped twice under the old policy, but the policy has since been replaced. There is no allegation that the college has resumed enforcement of the old rules, nor any indication that the plaintiffs intend to return to the same conduct under the same conditions. The controversy is not “live” in any meaningful sense.

Moreover, the plaintiffs’ request for leave to amend their complaint to seek compensatory damages is procedurally improper and legally irrelevant. The Court has held that a party cannot cure mootness by amending its complaint after the controversy has ended. In *United States v. Carrol*, the Court emphasized that “the court must look to the state of the controversy at the time of decision.” 354 U.S. at 398. The plaintiffs’ proposed amendment—seeking compensatory damages based on alleged emotional distress and travel expenses—was not raised before the district court and was not part of the original claim. The district court properly declined to allow such a request to be made in a response to a motion to dismiss. As the Court held in *United States v. Munsingwear*, “a party cannot resurrect a moot case by amending its pleadings after the controversy has ceased.” 340 U.S. at 40.

In sum, the plaintiffs’ claims for declaratory and injunctive relief are moot because the challenged policy has been permanently revised. Their claim for nominal damages does not revive the controversy, as the Court has repeatedly held that nominal damages alone cannot sustain jurisdiction when the underlying injury has ceased. The voluntary and permanent cessation of the challenged conduct, coupled with the absence of any credible threat of recurrence, renders this case moot. The judgment of the Eleventh Circuit must be affirmed.
