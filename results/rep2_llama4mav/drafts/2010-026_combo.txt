The Ninth Circuit erred in exempting documents relating to the effects of explosions from disclosure under the FOIA because they are "predominantly" for internal use and present a risk of circumventing agency regulation. The court's adoption of the "predominantly internal" standard is unfounded and contradicts the statutory language and legislative history of the FOIA.

The FOIA exemptions are to be narrowly construed, as the Supreme Court has held in Department of the Air Force v. Rose, 425 U.S. 352, 361 (1976) (quoting S.Rep. No. 813-89, at 3 (1965)). The Court has also emphasized that the exemptions are "explicitly exclusive," as stated in Administrator, FAA v. Robertson, 422 U.S. 255, 262 (1975). The Ninth Circuit's broad interpretation of Exemption 2 contravenes this principle.

Exemption 2 shields matters "related solely to the internal personnel rules and practices of an agency." 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(2). The Supreme Court has interpreted this exemption to apply to materials that are not of "genuine and significant public interest," as seen in Rose, 425 U.S. at 363-64. The Court has also noted that the exemption was intended to protect "minor or trivial administrative matters," as stated in the Senate Report accompanying the FOIA, S.Rep. No. 813-89, at 8 (1965).

The Ninth Circuit's reliance on Crooker v. Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco & Firearms, 670 F.2d 1051 (D.C.Cir.1981) (en banc), is misplaced, as that decision was rendered after the relevant statutory enactment and is not controlling. Instead, we look to the legislative history and early case law interpreting the FOIA. In Benson v. GSA, 289 F. Supp. 590, 594-95 (W.D. Wash. 1968), the court held that Exemption 2 applies to internal agency matters that are not of public interest. Similarly, in Consumers Union of United States, Inc. v. Veterans Admin., 301 F. Supp. 796, 802-03 (S.D.N.Y. 1969), the court applied Exemption 2 to internal personnel rules.

The documents at issue here relate to the effects of explosions and are not "internal personnel rules and practices" within the meaning of Exemption 2. As the court held in Epstein v. Resor, 296 F. Supp. 214, 216-17 (N.D. Cal. 1969), aff'd, 421 F.2d 930 (9th Cir. 1970), the exemption is not applicable to documents that are not related to personnel matters. Furthermore, in Long v. IRS, 288 F. Supp. 45, 46 (W.D. Wash. 1968), rev'd on other grounds, 431 F.2d 324 (9th Cir. 1970), the court emphasized that the FOIA exemptions should be narrowly construed.

The Navy's affidavits, while entitled to substantial weight, do not establish that the documents are exempt under Exemption 2. As the court held in Bristol-Myers Co. v. FTC, 284 F. Supp. 745, 747 (D.D.C. 1968), the agency bears the burden of proving that the documents fall within a claimed exemption. In this case, the Navy has not met that burden.

The Ninth Circuit's decision also conflicts with the principle that FOIA exemptions should be narrowly construed, as stated in Rose, 425 U.S. at 361. By adopting a broad interpretation of Exemption 2, the court has effectively created a new exemption not contemplated by Congress. This approach is inconsistent with the holdings in cases such as Getman v. NLRB, 450 F.2d 670, 672-73 (D.C.Cir. 1971) (pre-1970 lower court opinion), which emphasized the importance of narrowly construing FOIA exemptions.

In conclusion, the Ninth Circuit's decision to exempt the documents relating to the effects of explosions from disclosure under the FOIA is unfounded and contradicts the statutory language, legislative history, and relevant case law. The court should reverse the Ninth Circuit's decision and hold that the documents are not exempt from disclosure under Exemption 2.
