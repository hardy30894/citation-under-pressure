**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE FIRST CIRCUIT CORRECTLY UPHELD THE MASSACHUSETTS BUFFER ZONE LAW AS A CONSTITUTIONALLY VALID TIME, PLACE, AND MANNER RESTRICTION.**

The Massachusetts buffer zone law, Mass. Gen. Laws ch. 266, § 120E½, is a content-neutral, viewpoint-neutral regulation of the time, place, and manner of speech that survives intermediate scrutiny under the First Amendment. *See Ward v. Rock Against Racism*, 491 U.S. 781, 791 (1989) (“Government regulation of the time, place, or manner of protected speech must be narrowly tailored to serve a significant governmental interest, but it need not be the least restrictive or least intrusive means of doing so.”). The First Circuit correctly applied this standard and upheld the law. *McCullen v. Coakley (McCullen I)*, 571 F.3d 167, 176-81 (1st Cir. 2009), *aff’d in part and rev’d in part on other grounds*, 134 S. Ct. 2518 (2014).

**A. The Law Is Content-Neutral and Viewpoint-Neutral.**

The Massachusetts statute is content-neutral because it “applies to all speech regardless of the topic discussed or the viewpoint expressed.” *Hill v. Colorado*, 530 U.S. 703, 720 (2000). It does not single out abortion-related speech or any other particular message; instead, it prohibits all individuals from entering a 35-foot buffer zone around clinic entrances, regardless of what they say or why they say it. *See id.* at 720-21 (upholding a similar Colorado law because it “impos[ed] no restrictions on—and thus draw[s] no distinctions based on—the content of any speech”). The law’s exemptions—covering patients, employees, law enforcement, and those merely passing by—further demonstrate its neutrality. *See McCullen I*, 571 F.3d at 177.

Petitioners’ reliance on *Sorrell v. IMS Health Inc.*, 564 U.S. 552 (2011), is misplaced. *Sorrell* struck down a content-based restriction targeting pharmaceutical marketing data, not a neutral, generally applicable time, place, and manner regulation. *Id.* at 566-67. Unlike the law in *Sorrell*, the Massachusetts statute does not “disfavor[] specific speakers or ideas.” *Id.* at 566.

**B. The Law Serves a Significant Governmental Interest.**

The Massachusetts legislature enacted the buffer zone law to address “serious problems of access to reproductive health care facilities,” including “congestion, harassment, and safety concerns.” *McCullen I*, 571 F.3d at 178 (quoting legislative findings). These interests are “sufficiently strong to justify” the law. *Ward*, 491 U.S. at 796. The First Circuit correctly recognized that the law advances the state’s interest in “ensur[ing] the safe passage of persons wishing to enter healthcare facilities.” *Id.* at 178.

Petitioners’ contention that the law is underinclusive because it does not address harassment outside the buffer zone ignores the legislature’s reasonable decision to target the most problematic areas. *See Hill*, 530 U.S. at 723 (“The unwilling listener’s interest in avoiding unwanted communication . . . is an adequate justification for protecting the unwilling listener within the 100-foot zone.”). The law is narrowly tailored to address the specific harms at clinic entrances and driveways, where congestion and confrontations are most acute. *See McCullen I*, 571 F.3d at 178-79.

**C. The Law Leaves Open Ample Alternative Channels for Communication.**

The First Circuit correctly held that the law leaves open “ample alternative channels for communication.” *Ward*, 491 U.S. at 791. Petitioners can still speak, distribute literature, and engage in counseling outside the buffer zone. *See McCullen I*, 571 F.3d at 180. The record shows that petitioners can communicate effectively from sidewalks adjacent to the buffer zones, using signs, spoken words, and literature. *See id.* at 180-81. The law does not prevent petitioners from conveying their message; it merely requires them to do so at a slightly greater distance from clinic entrances.

Petitioners’ claim that the law prevents “close personal contact” is overstated. *See id.* at 180. The buffer zone is only 35 feet—roughly the length of three parking spaces—leaving ample space for meaningful interaction. *See Hill*, 530 U.S. at 725 (upholding a 100-foot buffer zone as leaving open ample alternatives). The First Circuit’s factual findings, based on a bench trial, are entitled to deference. *See Anderson v. City of Bessemer City*, 470 U.S. 564, 573-74 (1985).

**II. HILL V. COLORADO REMAINS GOOD LAW AND SHOULD NOT BE LIMITED OR OVERRULED.**

Petitioners argue that *Hill v. Colorado*, 530 U.S. 703, should be limited or overruled. This argument fails. *Hill* upheld a content-neutral, viewpoint-neutral buffer zone law under intermediate scrutiny, applying the same framework this Court uses in *Ward*. *Hill*, 530 U.S. at 720-21. The Massachusetts law is indistinguishable in principle. *See McCullen I*, 571 F.3d at 181-82.

Petitioners’ reliance on *Snyder v. Phelps*, 562 U.S. 443 (2011), is misplaced. *Snyder* addressed a tort judgment against protesters for intentional infliction of emotional distress, not a content-neutral time, place, and manner restriction. *Id.* at 452. The buffer zone law does not target speech based on viewpoint or subject matter, and it does not impose liability for speech. *See Hill*, 530 U.S. at 720.

**CONCLUSION**

The First Circuit correctly upheld the Massachusetts buffer zone law as a constitutional time, place, and manner restriction. The law is content-neutral, serves a significant governmental interest, and leaves open ample alternative channels for communication. *Hill v. Colorado* remains good law and controls this case. The judgment below should be affirmed.
