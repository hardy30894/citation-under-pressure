**ARGUMENT**

**I. Ray’s State‑Law Tort Claims Are Preempted by the Airline Deregulation Act.**

Congress has provided that a State “may not enact or enforce a law, regulation, or other provision having the force and effect of law related to a price, route, or service of an air carrier.” 49 U.S.C. § 41713(b)(1). The Supreme Court has construed this language broadly, holding that state enforcement actions having a “connection with, or reference to, airline ‘rates, routes, or services’” are preempted, even where the state law is one of “general applicability.” *Morales v. Trans World Airlines, Inc.*, 504 U.S. 374, 384, 386 (1992). Preemption reaches beyond rate and route regulation to embrace state tort duties that would effectively dictate how an airline delivers its services to passengers. *American Airlines, Inc. v. Wolens*, 513 U.S. 219, 226 (1995) (state law claims are preempted when they seek to enlarge or enhance the bargain between airline and passenger “beyond what the parties agreed”).

Applying *Morales* and *Wolens*, courts have consistently held that claims challenging an airline’s boarding, deplaning, and in‑flight management decisions are preempted because those decisions are quintessential “services.” In *Smith v. Comair, Inc.*, 134 F.3d 254, 259 (4th Cir. 1998), the court held that “boarding procedures … are a service” for preemption purposes, reasoning that the ADA’s deregulatory purpose would be undermined if states could impose independent standards for how carriers manage passenger boarding and deplaning. Similarly, in *Branche v. Airtran Airways, Inc.*, 342 F.3d 1248, 1257 (11th Cir. 2003), the Eleventh Circuit explained that state tort claims are preempted when they seek to hold an airline liable for conduct that is “directly connected to an airline’s provision of services,” including decisions about how to manage passengers during operational delays.

Ray’s false imprisonment and negligence claims fall squarely within this preempted category. Both claims challenge American’s decisions about when and how to permit passengers to deplane during a weather‑related tarmac delay—decisions that are inseparable from the airline’s provision of transportation service. Ray does not allege that American breached an express contractual promise, the narrow category of claim that survives preemption under *Wolens*, 513 U.S. at 232–33. Instead, she seeks to impose an external tort standard—dictated by a jury rather than by the negotiated contract of carriage—regarding when passengers must be released from an aircraft and how flight crews must communicate with them. Because these are matters of “service” within the meaning of § 41713(b)(1), her claims are preempted, and this Court should reverse the district court’s denial of preemption.

**II. Even Absent Preemption, the District Court Correctly Granted Summary Judgment.**

Summary judgment is proper where, viewing the evidence in the light most favorable to the nonmoving party, “there is no genuine issue as to any material fact.” Fed. R. Civ. P. 56(c); *Celotex Corp. v. Catrett*, 477 U.S. 317, 322 (1986). A dispute is “material” only if it might affect the outcome, and it is “genuine” only if a reasonable jury could return a verdict for the nonmoving party. *Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, Inc.*, 477 U.S. 242, 248 (1986).

Ray cannot establish false imprisonment because she was never confined against her will. She testified that American offered passengers not one but two separate opportunities to deplane via bus, and that she voluntarily chose to remain aboard both times based on her own assumptions about the cost of alternate travel—assumptions she never sought to clarify with the flight crew. A restraint is not unlawful, and indeed is not a restraint at all, where the plaintiff is free to leave and simply chooses not to exercise that option. Ray’s own deposition testimony establishes that she was given, and declined, the very opportunity whose absence she now claims imprisoned her.

Nor can Ray establish negligence. The delay was caused by weather conditions beyond American’s control—first the Dallas closure, then lightning that suspended ground operations in Austin—precluding any showing that American breached a duty of care by failing to prevent the delay. Ray also cannot establish causation for her claimed injury; she sought no medical treatment for her “24‑hour” stomach upset and offers no evidence connecting it, other than her own speculation, to the condition of the lavatory. Speculation is not evidence sufficient to defeat summary judgment. *Anderson*, 477 U.S. at 252 (mere “scintilla” of evidence is insufficient).

**III. The District Court Properly Denied Ray’s Rule 56(f) Motion for a Continuance.**

A party opposing summary judgment who seeks additional discovery under Rule 56(f) must show “what specific facts further discovery might uncover” and how those facts would preclude summary judgment. *Willmar Poultry Co. v. Morton‑Norwich Prods., Inc.*, 520 F.2d 289, 297 (8th Cir. 1975). Ray offered no such showing; her request rested on generalized hope that further discovery might reveal something helpful, not on identification of specific, discoverable facts bearing on the elements of her claims. The district court therefore did not abuse its discretion in denying the continuance and proceeding to decide the fully briefed summary judgment motion. *Celotex*, 477 U.S. at 322.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should reverse the district court’s ruling that Ray’s state tort claims are not preempted, and, in all events, affirm the grant of summary judgment in American’s favor and the denial of Ray’s Rule 56(f) motion.
