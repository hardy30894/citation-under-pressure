# ARGUMENT

## I. THE TEMPORARY SCHEDULING PROVISION OF THE CONTROLLED SUBSTANCES ACT IS A CONSTITUTIONAL DELEGATION OF AUTHORITY

Article I vests "all legislative powers" in Congress. U.S. Const. art. I, § 1. But this Court has long recognized that this provision does not prevent Congress from enlisting the assistance of the coordinate branches, provided Congress lays "down by legislative act an intelligible principle to which the person or body authorized to [act] is directed to conform." *J.W. Hampton, Jr., & Co. v. United States*, 276 U.S. 394, 409 (1928). As this Court reaffirmed just two years before this case arose, application of the intelligible-principle test has "been driven by a practical understanding that in our increasingly complex society, replete with ever changing and more technical problems, Congress simply cannot do its job absent an ability to delegate power under broad general directives." *Mistretta v. United States*, 488 U.S. 361, 372 (1989).

Section 811(h) of the Controlled Substances Act satisfies this standard with ease. Congress did not hand the Attorney General an unconstrained license to criminalize conduct at will. Instead, Congress specified the precise trigger for temporary scheduling—that a substance pose "an imminent hazard to the public safety"—and required the Attorney General to consider enumerated statutory factors bearing on abuse potential before acting. 21 U.S.C. § 811(h)(1), (3). Congress further cabined the delegated authority by imposing procedural safeguards: publication of notice in the Federal Register, transmission of that notice to the Secretary of Health and Human Services for comment, and a strict durational limit of one year, extendable by only six months. *Id.* § 811(h)(2), (4).

These constraints track precisely the kind of statutory structure this Court approved in *Mistretta*. There, the Court upheld Congress's delegation to the Sentencing Commission because Congress specified the goals to be pursued, the factors to be considered, and outer limits on the delegated authority. 488 U.S. at 372–79. The same is true here: Congress specified the goal (preventing imminent hazards to public safety), specified the factors bearing on that determination (the abuse-history factors of § 811(c)(4)–(6)), and specified temporal limits confining the exercise of power to one-and-a-half years at most.

Petitioners' reliance on this Court's Depression-era decisions in *Panama Refining Co. v. Ryan*, 293 U.S. 388 (1935), and *A.L.A. Schechter Poultry Corp. v. United States*, 295 U.S. 495 (1935), is misplaced. In *Panama Refining*, the statute "did not declare any policy" regarding the delegated authority and provided no "criterion to govern the President's course." 293 U.S. at 415. In *Schechter Poultry*, the President was given essentially unconfined power to approve industry-drafted codes with no statutory standard to guide him. 295 U.S. at 521–22. Section 811(h) bears no resemblance to those statutes. It supplies a specific standard ("imminent hazard to the public safety"), enumerated factors, procedural checks, and a hard temporal ceiling—far more structure than this Court has required in any delegation case decided since 1935. Indeed, "[w]ith the exception of th[ose] two 1935 cases," this Court "has upheld every congressional delegation of power that has been presented to it, even where Congress has provided negligible standards for the delegatee to follow." *Mistretta*, 488 U.S. at 373.

That the delegated authority here concerns the definition of criminal conduct does not demand a more exacting test. This Court has never held that delegations touching criminal enforcement are subject to a heightened non-delegation standard distinct from the intelligible-principle inquiry. To the contrary, the same practical considerations that justify delegation generally—the need for "a quick and flexible law enforcement response," as the very concern animating Congress's 1984 amendment illustrates—apply with particular force in the context of rapidly evolving designer drugs. Congress recognized that traditional permanent scheduling, with its lengthy notice-and-comment and APA hearing requirements, created an "inevitable lag time" that clandestine chemists were exploiting to synthesize new analogs faster than the Attorney General could act. The temporary scheduling provision responds directly to that documented regulatory gap, and this Court has recognized that "the rapidity of changes in the matter being regulated" is itself a relevant factor supporting the propriety of delegation. *Yakus v. United States*, 321 U.S. 414, 425 (1944) (upholding delegation of price-control authority under wartime exigency).

Nor does the unavailability of judicial review over individual temporary scheduling orders, 21 U.S.C. § 811(h)(6), render the delegation infirm. The absence of a particular form of review affects, at most, the procedural protections available to a regulated party in a given case; it does not alter whether Congress articulated an intelligible principle in the statute itself. Moreover, the constitutional challenge to the delegation itself—the challenge petitioners raise here—remains fully available notwithstanding § 811(h)(6), because "the availability of judicial review is presumed" or "unless Congress' intent to [foreclose it is] manifested by clear and convincing evidence." *Califano v. Sanders*, 430 U.S. 99, 109 (1977). Petitioners' ability to bring the very facial challenge now before this Court confirms that the constitutional check remains intact.

## II. THE ATTORNEY GENERAL WAS AUTHORIZED TO SUBDELEGATE TEMPORARY SCHEDULING AUTHORITY TO THE DEA

Congress separately authorized the Attorney General to delegate his functions under the Act. 21 U.S.C. § 871(a). Pursuant to that express statutory grant, the Attorney General promulgated a regulation assigning to the DEA Administrator the "[f]unctions vested in the Attorney General by the Comprehensive Drug Abuse Prevention and Control Act of 1970, as amended." 28 C.F.R. § 0.100(b) (1989). This regulation was validly issued and squarely covers the temporary scheduling function at issue. Where, as here, Congress has expressly authorized subdelegation and the Attorney General has exercised that authority through a properly promulgated regulation encompassing the relevant statutory function, no separate constitutional or statutory infirmity arises from the Administrator's exercise of that delegated authority.

## CONCLUSION

The judgment of the Court of Appeals should be affirmed.
