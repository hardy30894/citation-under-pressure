ARGUMENT

The Sixth Amendment secures the right to jury trial as it existed at common law. See Thompson v. Utah, 170 U.S. 343, 349-350 (1898); Callan v. Wilson, 127 U.S. 540, 549 (1888). That right attached to the determination of guilt or innocence of the offense charged; it did not extend to the selection of punishment after conviction. See Williams v. New York, 337 U.S. 241, 246 (1949) (“both before and since the American colonies became a nation, courts in this country and in England practiced a policy under which a sentencing judge could exercise a wide discretion in the sources and types of evidence used to assist him in determining the kind and extent of punishment to be imposed within limits fixed by law”).

Florida’s scheme preserves that historical division. The jury returns a verdict of guilt on the capital offense; the trial judge then weighs statutory aggravating and mitigating circumstances to decide the sentence. Because those circumstances are not elements of the offense, their resolution by the court raises no Sixth Amendment question. See, e.g., Ex parte United States, 242 U.S. 27, 41-42 (1916) (distinguishing the jury’s role in fixing guilt from the court’s role in fixing penalty).

The same principle governs claims of mental retardation. At common law, insanity after conviction was a matter for executive or judicial inquiry, not a jury question at trial. See Nobles v. Georgia, 168 U.S. 398, 409 (1897). Florida’s requirement that the defendant prove retardation by clear and convincing evidence to the court is therefore consistent with historical practice.

The Eighth Amendment likewise imposes no requirement of jury unanimity. That Amendment forbids only those punishments regarded as cruel and unusual when the Bill of Rights was adopted. See Weems v. United States, 217 U.S. 349, 368-369 (1910); Wilkerson v. Utah, 99 U.S. 130, 134-135 (1879). No authority from that period suggests that a death sentence imposed by a judge after a non-unanimous jury recommendation would have been viewed as cruel and unusual.

Accordingly, the judgment of the Florida Supreme Court should be affirmed.
