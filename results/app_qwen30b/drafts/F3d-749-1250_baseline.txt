**ARGUMENT**

The district court erred in admitting the testimony of FBI Special Agent Charles Jones as an expert on deception, which constituted a fundamental violation of Federal Rule of Evidence 702 and the Sixth Amendment right to a fair trial. Agent Jones’s testimony improperly invaded the jury’s role as the sole arbiter of credibility, transformed the trial into a “trial within a trial” on the defendant’s truthfulness, and injected impermissible speculation and bias into the proceedings. Because the error was not harmless, reversal is required.

Under *Daubert v. Merrell Dow Pharmaceuticals, Inc.*, 509 U.S. 579, 593 (1993), expert testimony is admissible only if it is (1) based on sufficient facts or data, (2) the product of reliable principles and methods, and (3) the result of reliable application of those principles and methods to the facts of the case. The Supreme Court has emphasized that “the trial judge must ensure that any and all scientific testimony or evidence admitted is not only relevant, but reliable.” *Id.* at 592. Here, Agent Jones’s testimony failed all three prongs of the *Daubert* test.

First, Jones’s methodology was neither scientifically valid nor generally accepted. He claimed to be trained in “special tactics and ways to identify deception in statements and truths in statements,” but offered no peer-reviewed research, no standardized testing protocol, and no empirical basis for his assertions. The American Psychological Association has long cautioned that “no single behavioral indicator reliably distinguishes truth from deception,” and that “the vast majority of so-called ‘lie detection’ techniques have not been validated by scientific research.” *APA Presidential Task Force on Psychological Testing*, 2007. Jones’s reliance on subjective observations—such as “evasive behaviors common among the criminal element” or the invocation of faith as a sign of deception—amounts to no more than “folk psychology,” which is not admissible under Rule 702. *See United States v. Brawner*, 577 F.3d 1178, 1185 (10th Cir. 2009) (holding that “generalized assertions about human behavior” are not sufficient to qualify as expert testimony).

Second, Jones’s testimony was not reliably applied to the facts. He offered no objective criteria for determining whether a statement “makes sense” or whether a person’s behavior was “evasive.” His conclusion that Stanley “would rather die than face charges” was not based on any data or analysis but on a subjective, untested assertion: “Never in my career have I seen that with an innocent person.” This is not expert testimony—it is a prophetic judgment cloaked in the guise of expertise. *See United States v. Hines*, 585 F.3d 1318, 1325 (10th Cir. 2009) (holding that “an expert may not testify as to the ultimate issue of guilt or innocence” and that “testimony that amounts to a personal opinion on the defendant’s credibility is impermissible”).

Third, the testimony was not relevant to any issue in the case. The jury was already entitled to assess Stanley’s credibility based on the evidence and the demeanor of the witnesses. The government’s theory of the case was not that Stanley was lying about his identity or his involvement, but that he was a getaway driver. The jury was not asked to determine whether Stanley was truthful in his statements; it was asked to determine whether he participated in the robbery. Jones’s testimony, however, directly addressed the ultimate issue—whether Stanley was truthful—thereby usurping the jury’s constitutional role. *See United States v. Brawner*, 577 F.3d at 1185 (“The jury is the sole judge of credibility, and expert testimony that invades that function is improper.”).

Moreover, the testimony was highly prejudicial. By asserting that “when people start bringing faith into validating their statements, that they’re deceptive,” Jones injected religious bias into the trial. This statement, which equated religious expression with dishonesty, violated the principle that “a defendant’s religious beliefs must not be used as a basis for inferring guilt.” *See United States v. Hines*, 585 F.3d at 1325. The government’s use of such a statement to discredit a defendant’s invocation of God as a moral anchor was not only improper but potentially unconstitutional under the First Amendment’s Free Exercise Clause.

The district court’s failure to exclude this testimony was not harmless. The jury was instructed to consider the evidence in determining Stanley’s guilt, and the government’s case rested heavily on the credibility of the witnesses and the defendant’s statements. Jones’s testimony, which painted Stanley as a habitual deceiver and a man who would rather die than face charges, directly undermined Stanley’s credibility at a critical juncture. *See United States v. Mendoza*, 575 F.3d 1125, 1132 (10th Cir. 2009) (holding that “a single improper expert opinion may be so prejudicial as to require reversal”).

Even though Stanley did not object at trial, the error was plain under Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 52(b). The admission of Jones’s testimony was so fundamental to the fairness of the trial that it affected the outcome. The government’s case was built on circumstantial evidence, and the jury’s assessment of Stanley’s credibility was central. The improper expert testimony distorted that assessment, creating a substantial risk of a miscarriage of justice.

For these reasons, the judgment of the district court must be reversed and the case remanded for a new trial.
