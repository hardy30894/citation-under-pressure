ARGUMENT

I. The District Court's Order Is Not Appealable Under 28 U.S.C. § 1291 Because It Is Not a Final Order

The State's appeal should be dismissed for lack of jurisdiction because the district court's October 27 Order is not a final order under 28 U.S.C. § 1291. As this Court has held, a civil contempt order is ordinarily not appealable until the district court has adjudicated the contempt motion and applied sanctions. SEC v. Hickey, 322 F.3d 1123, 1127 (9th Cir. 2003). The October 27 Order clearly contemplates further proceedings, as it requires the State to show cause why it should not be held in contempt if it fails to transfer $250 million to the Receiver by November 5, 2008. This is not a final adjudication of the contempt motion, and therefore, the Order is not appealable.

The State argues that awaiting sanctions will not enable it to raise the challenges it seeks to raise here, but this is a consequence of its failure to follow more appropriate procedures to challenge the receivership and its plans. The district court's order may not confine the proposed proceedings to the issue of whether the State has delivered the $250 million, as the court has called for a witness list and a further scheduling order. The State's challenges can be raised on appeal when they are presented in appropriate form to the district court and ruled upon in a final order.

II. The District Court's Order Is Not Reviewable as a "Post-Judgment Order" Under United States v. State of Washington

The State's reliance on United States v. State of Washington, 761 F.2d 1404 (9th Cir. 1985), is misplaced. While orders that relate to fully adjudicated issues may be appealable, the October 27 Order is not a final adjudication of the contempt motion. See Stone v. City & County of San Francisco, 968 F.2d 850, 854-55 (9th Cir. 1992). The Order is still subject to modification by the district court, and therefore, it is not a final order.

III. The District Court's Order Is Not Reviewable as a "Collateral Order" Under Coopers & Lybrand v. Livesay

The October 27 Order is not a "collateral order" under Coopers & Lybrand v. Livesay, 437 U.S. 463 (1978), because it does not conclusively determine the disputed question, resolve an important issue completely separate from the merits of the action, and be effectively unreviewable on appeal from a final judgment. The State's challenges are fully intertwined with the central validity of the underlying litigation, the consent decrees, and the receivership and its plans. These challenges can be raised on appeal when they are presented in appropriate form to the district court and ruled upon in a final order.

IV. The Petition for Writ of Mandamus Should Be Denied

The State's petition for a writ of mandamus should be denied because it has not met the requirements set forth in Bauman v. United States Dist. Court, 557 F.2d 650 (9th Cir. 1977). The State has an adequate means to attain relief through a direct appeal after the district court has adjudicated the contempt motion and applied sanctions. The State will not be damaged or prejudiced in a way not correctable on appeal, as it can raise its challenges to the receivership and its plans in further proceedings before the district court. The district court's order is not clearly erroneous as a matter of law, and it does not manifest a persistent disregard of the federal rules.

The State's petition for mandamus is premature, as it seeks review of larger questions going beyond the order for which the writ is sought. The district court may deal with these issues in further proceedings that lead to appealable orders regarding the receivership and construction program. This Court should deny the petition for writ of mandamus and allow the district court to resolve the State's challenges in the first instance.
