**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Trial Court Properly Limited the Expert Testimony to the Insanity Defense**

The State respectfully submits that the trial court properly restricted the petitioner’s expert testimony to the insanity defense. Under *Jackson v. Indiana*, 406 U.S. 702 (1972), the insanity defense must be final. The trial court correctly ruled that the evidence was inadmissible to negate intent because the petitioner failed to meet his burden of proving insanity. As the Supreme Court has held, "the State has a legitimate interest in preventing the jury from being confused by irrelevant evidence." *Specht v. Patterson*, 386 U.S. 605, 610 (1967). Because the evidence was insufficient to establish insanity, it was also insufficient to establish a lack of intent.

**II. The Petitioner Lacks a Separate Fourteenth Amendment Right to Introduce Expert Evidence to Negate Intent**

The petitioner asserts a "separate" due process right to present expert evidence regarding his mental state to counter the prosecution’s evidence of intent. This argument is foreclosed by *Specht v. Patterson*, 386 U.S. 605 (1967). The Court held that the right to present a defense is not absolute and must be balanced against the State’s interest in preventing confusion. Here, the trial court’s restriction was a valid exercise of discretion to ensure the jury focused on the specific issue of criminal responsibility.

The petitioner’s belief that aliens had taken over his town was not a defense to the specific charge of first-degree murder, which requires proof of specific intent. *M'Naghten's Case*, 8 Eng. Rep. 718 (1843). The trial court did not violate due process by preventing the petitioner from introducing evidence that was legally insufficient to prove the elements of the crime. The petitioner’s mental state, while relevant to the insanity defense, was irrelevant to the specific intent required for the charged offense. Therefore, the trial court’s ruling was a proper application of the rules of evidence and did not infringe upon the petitioner’s Fourteenth Amendment rights.
