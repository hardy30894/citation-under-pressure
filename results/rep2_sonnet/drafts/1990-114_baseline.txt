# ARGUMENT

## I. MISSOURI'S MANDATORY RETIREMENT PROVISION FOR STATE JUDGES DOES NOT VIOLATE THE ADEA

### A. The Plain Statutory Command of the ADEA, as Informed by the Clear-Statement Rule, Excludes Missouri's Appointed Judges from Coverage

This case implicates one of the most sensitive areas of state sovereignty: a state's power to define who shall exercise its judicial authority. This Court has long recognized that "through the structure of its government, and the character of those who exercise government authority, a State defines itself as a sovereign." That power extends with particular force to the qualifications of judges, who alone among state officials bear responsibility for interpreting and enforcing the State's own constitution and laws.

Because Missouri's retirement rule is embedded in its Constitution—the most fundamental expression of state sovereignty—this Court should not construe the ADEA to override it absent a clear congressional statement. As this Court has held, when Congress intends to alter "the usual constitutional balance between the States and the Federal Government," it must make its intention "unmistakably clear in the language of the statute." *Atascadero State Hospital v. Scanlon*, 473 U.S. 234, 242 (1985). This clear-statement rule applies with special force here, where the state law in question governs the composition of the state judiciary itself—a decision "of the most fundamental sort for a sovereign entity."

The statutory text supplies no such clear statement. To the contrary, the ADEA expressly excludes from the definition of "employee" any "appointee on the policymaking level." 29 U.S.C. § 630(f). Missouri's appointed judges plainly fall within this exception. As the Eighth Circuit correctly recognized, "[t]he reality of judging is that it is not always—and perhaps not even usually—merely a matter of mechanically applying settled, unambiguous principles of law to specific fact patterns as they arise." 898 F.2d at 603. Judges make policy in the fullest sense: they resolve issues of first impression, articulate common-law rules binding on future litigants, and—in Missouri specifically—exercise "considerable policymaking responsibility in the supervision of the Missouri court system," including rulemaking authority over the conduct of the entire state judiciary and bar. *Id.* at 604.

Petitioners' contrary reading—that "policymaking" appointees exist only in the executive and legislative branches—would produce an implausible result: appointed judges would receive no exemption at all under any of the four statutory categories, while elected judges would be wholly exempt. Congress is not presumed to have intended such an anomalous distinction between States that elect their judiciaries and States that appoint them, particularly when the "powers and responsibilities of appointed and elected judges are identical." *Id.* at 606 n.5. Nothing in the statute suggests that Congress meant to penalize Missouri's choice of a merit-selection system by stripping its judges of an exemption that petitioners concede would apply to identically-functioning elected judges.

### B. Ambiguity Must Be Resolved in Favor of the State

Even if the term "policymaking level" appointee were less clear, ambiguity cannot substitute for the clear statement this Court's precedents require before finding that Congress has intruded upon a State's authority to structure its own judiciary. As Judge Bowman observed below, courts should not "interpret the ADEA as conflicting with the Missouri Constitution unless [they] find that Congress clearly has expressed its intention to bring state judges into the ambit of the federal statute." 898 F.2d at 601 (citing *United States v. Bass*, 404 U.S. 336, 349 (1971)). No such clear expression exists. The legislative history petitioners invoke speaks generally of "policymaking positions at the highest levels," language fully consistent with—indeed, descriptive of—state judgeships. This Court should resolve any doubt in favor of the States' traditional sovereign authority over the composition of their courts.

## II. MISSOURI'S MANDATORY RETIREMENT PROVISION SATISFIES RATIONAL-BASIS REVIEW UNDER THE EQUAL PROTECTION CLAUSE

Age is not a suspect classification, and this Court has repeatedly confirmed that state retirement provisions for public employees are subject only to rational-basis scrutiny. In *Massachusetts Board of Retirement v. Murgia*, 427 U.S. 307 (1976), this Court upheld a mandatory retirement age for state police, holding that "[t]his Court's decisions give no support to the proposition that a right of governmental employment per se is fundamental." *Id.* at 313. The same principle forecloses petitioners' claim here: there is no fundamental right to hold judicial office beyond a particular age.

Under rational-basis review, Missouri's rule need only be rationally related to a legitimate state interest—it need not be the wisest or most efficient means of achieving that interest. Missouri's mandatory retirement age advances multiple legitimate objectives. First, it responds to the reality that "the work of judges makes unique and exacting demands on faculties that age tends to erode." *Trafelet v. Thompson*, 594 F.2d 623, 627 (7th Cir. 1979). Second, it "reduces delays in the administration of justice caused by death or disabling illness of sitting judges," a risk that "increases as a judge's age increases." *Id.* at 629. Third, it "eliminates the unpleasantness of selectively removing aged... judges," *Malmed v. Thornburgh*, 621 F.2d 565, 572 (3d Cir. 1980), avoiding the "anguish, time, delay, expense, and embarrassment" of case-by-case competency review, *Apkin v. Treasurer and Receiver General*, 517 N.E.2d 141, 146 (Mass. 1988). Finally, mandatory retirement facilitates generational and demographic turnover on the bench, "mak[ing] it possible to increase the proportion of minority group members and women in the judiciary." *Id.*

Petitioners may disagree with these judgments as a matter of policy, but that disagreement does not establish a constitutional violation. As the Eighth Circuit correctly held, "[o]ne might disagree with some or all of these rationales on either empirical or philosophical grounds and yet be unable properly to conclude that a state which thinks otherwise lacks a rational basis for its mandatory retirement rule for judges." 898 F.2d at 605. Missouri's line-drawing at age seventy is precisely the sort of policy choice reserved to the States and to the people acting through their state constitutions—an authority this Court has repeatedly protected from judicial second-guessing under the rational-basis standard. *See Rodriguez*, 411 U.S. at 40-41.

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Court of Appeals should be affirmed.
