**Argument**

The Texas Supreme Court erred in holding that federal courts have exclusive jurisdiction whenever a state-law claim merely requires application of patent law. This Court has long rejected such an expansive view of “arising under” jurisdiction. Federal jurisdiction does not attach simply because a federal issue is embedded in a state cause of action; rather, the federal issue must appear on the face of the well-pleaded claim and be substantial in the jurisdictional sense. See *Louisville & Nashville R.R. Co. v. Mottley*, 211 U.S. 149 (1908); *Gully v. First Nat’l Bank in Meridian*, 299 U.S. 109 (1936); *Shulthis v. McDougal*, 225 U.S. 561 (1912).

1. **Section 1338 does not displace state jurisdiction merely because patent law is implicated.**  
This Court has repeatedly held that a case does not “arise under” federal law merely because a federal question may be raised in defense or as an ingredient of the controversy. In *Mottley*, the Court held that jurisdiction cannot rest on a federal issue anticipated in the complaint. 211 U.S. at 152. In *Shulthis*, the Court explained that a suit does not arise under federal law “unless it really and substantially involves a dispute or controversy” over federal law “upon the determination of which the result depends.” 225 U.S. at 569. And in *Gully*, the Court cautioned against turning “the ‘arising under’ door” into an open portal for any case with a federal element. 299 U.S. at 117-18. Those decisions foreclose the Texas Supreme Court’s rule that any malpractice case requiring patent analysis belongs exclusively in federal court.

2. **The presence of a patent issue as part of a state malpractice claim does not create exclusive federal jurisdiction.**  
The relevant inquiry is whether federal patent law creates the cause of action or whether the plaintiff’s right to relief necessarily depends on resolution of a substantial federal patent question. See *The Fair v. Kohler Die & Specialty Co.*, 228 U.S. 22 (1913); *Christianson v. Colt Indus. Operating Corp.*, 486 U.S. 800 (1988). But *Christianson* itself confirms that the “arising under” test is not satisfied by every claim that may require application of patent law in the background. 486 U.S. at 808-09. Earlier authority is in accord: federal jurisdiction exists only when the federal issue is an essential element of the plaintiff’s own claim, not when it is collateral or contingent. See *Smith v. Kansas City Title & Trust Co.*, 255 U.S. 180 (1921); *Shulthis*, 225 U.S. at 569.

3. **State courts routinely decide issues governed by federal law without surrendering jurisdiction.**  
This Court has never suggested that the mere need to interpret federal law divests state courts of power. To the contrary, state courts may adjudicate claims involving federal questions unless Congress has expressly or by necessary implication made jurisdiction exclusive. See *Claflin v. Houseman*, 93 U.S. 130 (1876); *Testa v. Katt*, 330 U.S. 386 (1947). Here, Congress made patent jurisdiction exclusive only for civil actions “arising under” the patent laws, 28 U.S.C. § 1338(a), not for every dispute in which patent doctrine may be consulted. *Claflin* teaches that state courts are competent to apply federal law when it arises in the course of adjudicating state-created rights. 93 U.S. at 136-37.

4. **Exclusive federal jurisdiction would distort the federal-state balance.**  
The Court has long cautioned that jurisdictional rules should not unduly transfer traditionally state-law disputes into federal court. In *Gully*, the Court emphasized restraint in reading federal jurisdiction broadly. 299 U.S. at 117-18. In *Mottley*, the Court refused to create jurisdiction from the anticipation of a federal defense. 211 U.S. at 152. And in *Osborn v. Bank of the United States*, 22 U.S. (9 Wheat.) 738 (1824), the Court recognized federal jurisdiction only because federal law was built into the very charter of the cause of action. That is not this case. Legal malpractice is a classic state-law tort, and the need to determine whether counsel should have raised experimental use as a patent defense does not convert the entire dispute into a federal patent suit.

Under these precedents, the Texas Supreme Court’s holding cannot stand. The petitioner's malpractice claim does not arise under the patent laws merely because patent law supplies one element of the causation inquiry. This Court should hold that federal courts do not have exclusive jurisdiction over state-law claims that only incidentally require patent analysis, and it should reverse.
