**ARGUMENT**

The Civil Rights Act of 1991 authorizes punitive damages only upon proof that the defendant acted “with malice or with reckless indifference to the federally protected rights of an aggrieved individual.” 42 U.S.C. § 1981a(b)(1). That statutory phrase incorporates the common-law requirement that punitive damages be reserved for conduct that is independently “egregious” or “outrageous.” Smith v. Wade, 461 U.S. 30, 46-47 (1983) (quoting Restatement (Second) of Torts § 908(2) (1979)). The court of appeals therefore erred in directing a new trial on punitive damages without that additional showing.

This Court has long held that punitive damages “are not a favorite of the law” and may be awarded only when the defendant’s conduct “is of the sort that calls for deterrence and punishment over and above that provided by compensatory awards.” BMW of North America, Inc. v. Gore, 517 U.S. 559, 568 (1996) (quoting Pacific Mut. Life Ins. Co. v. Haslip, 499 U.S. 1, 19 (1991)). The same principle governs federal civil-rights statutes. In Smith v. Wade the Court explained that § 1983 punitive damages require “evil motive or intent” or “reckless or callous indifference” that is “sufficiently egregious.” 461 U.S. at 56. Congress used identical language in § 1981a(b)(1), thereby importing the same limitation. Landgraf v. USI Film Prods., 511 U.S. 244, 252-55 (1994).

The legislative history confirms the point. The House Report stated that the 1991 Act demands “an even higher standard” than simple proof of intentional discrimination. H.R. Rep. No. 102-40, pt. 1, at 72 (1991). Senator Dole’s interpretive memorandum likewise limited recovery to “extraordinarily egregious cases.” 137 Cong. Rec. S15473 (daily ed. Oct. 30, 1991). These statements are consistent with the common-law tradition applied to § 1981 claims. Patterson v. McLean Credit Union, 491 U.S. 164, 182 (1989); see also St. Mary’s Honor Ctr. v. Hicks, 509 U.S. 502, 511 (1993) (rejecting liability on proof of pretext alone).

McDonnell Douglas Corp. v. Green, 411 U.S. 792 (1973), and its progeny supply the framework for liability but do not relax the separate punitive-damages threshold. A plaintiff who establishes a prima facie case and pretext may prevail on liability, Hicks, 509 U.S. at 511, yet still fail to show the “aggravating circumstances” required for punishment. TXO Prod. Corp. v. Alliance Res. Corp., 509 U.S. 443, 453 (1993). The district court correctly applied that distinction when it refused to instruct the jury on punitive damages.

Because the record contains no evidence of conduct that was outrageous apart from the employer’s state of mind, the judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed insofar as it orders a trial on punitive damages.
