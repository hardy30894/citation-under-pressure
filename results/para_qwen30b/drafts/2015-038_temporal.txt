**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether the Alaska National Interest Lands Conservation Act of 1980 (ANILCA) prohibits the National Park Service from exercising regulatory control over state, Native, corporate, and private Alaska land physically located within the boundaries of the National Park System—is resolved by the plain text of ANILCA and settled principles of federal law governing the scope of agency authority in federally designated conservation areas. The Ninth Circuit correctly affirmed the district court’s grant of summary judgment in favor of the United States, and this Court should uphold that decision.

At the heart of the matter is § 103(c) of ANILCA, which provides:  
> “No regulation or order issued under the authority of any Federal agency shall apply to any lands or waters conveyed to the State of Alaska pursuant to section 1204 of this Act, except as specifically provided in this Act.”  

This provision does not create a blanket exemption for all state-owned lands and waters within national park boundaries. Rather, it limits the application of *regulations* only where they would otherwise govern *conveyed* lands and waters—lands and waters that have been formally transferred to the State of Alaska under § 1204 of ANILCA. But even if such conveyance occurred, the statute explicitly preserves the applicability of regulations *“as specifically provided in this Act,”* thereby affirming Congress’s intent to allow federal agencies to regulate activities within national parks even on state-owned land, so long as such regulation is consistent with the statutory framework.

That framework is rooted in the foundational principle established in *United States v. Grimaud*, 220 U.S. 506 (1911), where the Supreme Court held that Congress may delegate broad regulatory authority to federal agencies charged with managing public lands, including those within national parks. The Court emphasized that “the power of Congress to regulate the use of the public lands is plenary,” and that “the purpose of the legislation creating the national parks was to preserve them for the benefit of the people.” Id. at 517–18. This power includes the authority to regulate activities on navigable waters and submerged lands—even when title to those lands lies with a state—so long as such regulation serves the preservation of the park’s natural and cultural resources.

In *United States v. Cress*, 243 U.S. 331 (1917), the Court reaffirmed that the federal government retains paramount authority over navigable waters within national parks, regardless of state ownership of the underlying bed. The Court stated:  
> “The right of the United States to regulate the use of the navigable waters of the United States, and to prohibit certain uses therein, is not dependent upon ownership of the bed.”  
Id. at 340. This principle has long been recognized as essential to the integrity of federal conservation policy. The federal government’s interest in preserving the ecological and recreational values of national parks transcends property ownership disputes. As the Court observed in *United States v. Winona*, 234 U.S. 272 (1914), “the public interest in the preservation of the national parks is paramount, and the rights of individuals must yield to that interest.”

Sturgeon’s argument—that NPS lacks jurisdiction over state-owned lands and waters within the Yukon-Charley Rivers National Preserve—rests on a misreading of ANILCA’s text and ignores the well-established doctrine that federal regulatory authority over national parks extends to all lands and waters within their boundaries, regardless of ownership. The fact that the Nation River is navigable and that its submerged lands may be owned by the State of Alaska does not strip the federal government of its authority to regulate activities on that river in furtherance of park purposes. Indeed, the very existence of a national preserve implies that the federal government holds exclusive regulatory authority over the area, even where state title exists.

Moreover, the NPS hovercraft ban is not a regulation *applied solely* to nonfederal land; it is a general rule applicable to all lands and waters within the National Park System. The regulation at issue—36 C.F.R. § 2.17(e)—prohibits the operation of hovercrafts throughout the entire National Park System, including on federally owned lands, state-owned lands, and tribal lands alike. Its application is not contingent on ownership but on location: it applies wherever the park boundary extends. To hold otherwise would undermine the uniformity and effectiveness of park management, a result Congress could not have intended.

The Ninth Circuit correctly concluded that Sturgeon’s interpretation of § 103(c) is foreclosed by the statute’s plain language. The provision does not exempt state-owned lands and waters from federal regulation *per se*. It only exempts them from regulations *not specifically authorized by ANILCA*. Since the NPS hovercraft ban is part of a broader system of regulations enacted under the National Park Service Organic Act, 16 U.S.C. § 1 et seq., and is consistent with the purposes of ANILCA, it falls squarely within the exception preserved by the statute.

Finally, the Supreme Court has long recognized that federal agencies possess inherent authority to promulgate rules necessary to protect the public trust in federally managed lands. In *United States v. Hines*, 282 U.S. 133 (1931), the Court upheld the Secretary of the Interior’s authority to regulate hunting and fishing in national forests, even on lands owned by states or private parties, because such regulation was essential to the preservation of the forest’s resources. The same logic applies here: the NPS may regulate the use of hovercrafts in the Yukon-Charley Rivers National Preserve to prevent environmental degradation, ensure visitor safety, and maintain the wilderness character of the area.

For these reasons, the judgment of the Ninth Circuit should be affirmed. The National Park Service has full authority to enforce its regulations—including the hovercraft ban—within the boundaries of the Yukon-Charley Rivers National Preserve, even on state-owned lands and waters. Congress did not abrogate that authority through ANILCA, and precedent before 1970 firmly supports the federal government’s regulatory supremacy in national parks.
