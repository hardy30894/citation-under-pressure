The National Labor Relations Board's (NLRB) "good-faith reasonable doubt" standard for polling employees on union support is arbitrary and inconsistent with the National Labor Relations Act (NLRA), as it fails to account for the employer's legitimate interest in ascertaining employee sentiment. The NLRB's factual determination that Allentown Mack Sales, Inc. lacked such doubt is not supported by substantial evidence in the record, and the court should therefore reverse the NLRB's order.

The NLRA does not explicitly govern employee polling, and the NLRB has developed a standard for permitting employers to conduct polls. However, the NLRB's standard, which requires an employer to have "objective indications raising a reasonable doubt about the union's majority status" before conducting a poll, is overly restrictive (Struksnes Constr. Co., 165 N.L.R.B. 1062 (1967)). This standard is not supported by the NLRA, which aims to promote stable collective-bargaining relationships (NLRB v. Jones & Laughlin Steel Corp., 301 U.S. 1, 45 (1937)). In fact, the NLRB's standard may actually destabilize collective-bargaining relationships by limiting the employer's ability to gauge employee sentiment.

Several courts of appeals have rejected the NLRB's standard, holding that a lesser showing should suffice (NLRB v. A.W. Thompson, Inc., 651 F.2d 1141 (5th Cir. 1961); Thomas Industries, Inc. v. NLRB, 687 F.2d 863 (6th Cir. 1962); Mingtree Restaurant, Inc. v. NLRB, 736 F.2d 1295 (9th Cir. 1964)). A more reasonable approach is to permit employers to conduct polls when they have substantial, objective evidence of a loss of union support, even if that evidence is not sufficient to justify withdrawal of recognition (NLRB v. John S. Swift Co., 302 F.2d 342, 346 (7th Cir. 1962)). This standard is consistent with the NLRA's goal of promoting fair labor practices and protecting employees' rights (NLRB v. Pittsburgh S.S. Co., 337 U.S. 656, 660 (1949)).

In this case, Allentown Mack had substantial, objective evidence of a loss of union support, including statements from several employees indicating their dissatisfaction with the union. For example, employees expressed concerns about the union's representation and voiced their opposition to continuing union membership. The NLRB's determination that Allentown Mack lacked a good-faith reasonable doubt about the union's majority status is not supported by substantial evidence in the record. The NLRB's decision to discount certain employee statements, such as those made during job interviews, is not justified by the record (Stoner Rubber Co., 123 N.L.R.B. 1440, 1445 (1959)).

The Supreme Court has held that the NLRB's decisions must be based on substantial evidence and that the court must review the NLRB's findings to ensure that they are not arbitrary or capricious (Universal Camera Corp. v. NLRB, 340 U.S. 474, 488 (1951)). In this case, the NLRB's decision is not supported by substantial evidence, and the court should therefore reverse the NLRB's order. By doing so, the court can ensure that the NLRB's decisions are consistent with the NLRA and promote fair labor practices.

In conclusion, the NLRB's "good-faith reasonable doubt" standard is arbitrary and inconsistent with the NLRA, and the NLRB's factual determination that Allentown Mack lacked such doubt is not supported by substantial evidence in the record. The court should therefore reverse the NLRB's order and hold that Allentown Mack's poll was lawful, as it was conducted in good faith and based on substantial, objective evidence of a loss of union support.
