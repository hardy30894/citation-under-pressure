The Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act (OCSLA) extends workers' compensation coverage under the Longshore and Harbor Workers' Compensation Act (LHWCA) to employees injured "as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf for the purpose of exploring for, developing, removing, or transporting by pipeline the natural resources" of the outer Continental Shelf. 43 U.S.C. § 1333(b). The question presented is whether the OCSLA extends LHWCA coverage only to workers injured on the outer Continental Shelf itself.

The plain language of Section 1333(b) does not explicitly require that the injury occur on the outer Continental Shelf. However, a careful analysis of the statutory text and relevant case law reveals that a situs-of-injury requirement is implicit in the statute.

The Supreme Court's decision in Vermilya-Brown Co. v. Connell, 335 U.S. 377 (1948), provides guidance on the interpretation of the LHWCA, which is incorporated by reference in the OCSLA. In Vermilya-Brown, the Court held that the LHWCA applies to injuries occurring on "navigable waters" and that the term "navigable waters" is to be interpreted broadly. Id. at 386-87. However, the Court also noted that the statute's coverage is limited by the requirement that the injury occur on "navigable waters." Id. at 386.

Similarly, the OCSLA's extension of LHWCA coverage to injuries occurring "as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf" implies a geographic limitation on the statute's coverage. The phrase "as the result of" suggests a causal connection between the operations on the outer Continental Shelf and the injury. See London Guarantee & Accident Co. v. Industrial Accident Commission, 279 P. 172, 174 (Cal. Dist. Ct. App. 1929) (holding that the phrase "as a result of" implies a causal connection between the employment and the injury).

The Fifth Circuit's decision in Stansbury v. Sikorski Aircraft, 181 F.2d 155 (5th Cir. 1950), is also instructive. In Stansbury, the court held that the LHWCA applies to injuries occurring on a fixed structure on the outer Continental Shelf, even if the structure is not technically "navigable waters." Id. at 156-57. However, the court's reasoning implies that the statute's coverage is limited to injuries occurring on or in connection with structures on the outer Continental Shelf.

Other cases, such as Parker v. Motor Boat Sales, 314 U.S. 244 (1941), and Davis v. Department of Labor and Industries, 317 U.S. 249 (1942), demonstrate the importance of a situs requirement in workers' compensation statutes. In Parker, the Court held that the LHWCA applies to injuries occurring on "navigable waters," even if the employee is not engaged in maritime employment. 314 U.S. at 247-48. In Davis, the Court noted that the LHWCA and state workers' compensation statutes have "twilight zones" where coverage may overlap or be uncertain. 317 U.S. at 256.

These cases collectively suggest that a situs-of-injury requirement is implicit in the OCSLA. Requiring a substantial nexus between the injury and the operations on the outer Continental Shelf, as the Ninth Circuit held, would effectively eliminate the situs requirement and extend LHWCA coverage to employees injured anywhere, so long as their injury is causally connected to operations on the outer Continental Shelf.

The OCSLA's language and relevant case law support the conclusion that the statute extends LHWCA coverage only to workers injured on the outer Continental Shelf itself or in a location with a direct connection to operations on the outer Continental Shelf. Therefore, the Ninth Circuit's decision should be reversed, and the Benefits Review Board's denial of benefits under the OCSLA should be reinstated.
