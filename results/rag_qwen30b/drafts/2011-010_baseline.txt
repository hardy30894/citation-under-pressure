**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether the Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act (“OCSLA”) extends Longshore and Harbor Workers’ Compensation Act (“LHWCA”) benefits only to injuries occurring *on* the outer Continental Shelf—must be answered in the affirmative. The plain language of 43 U.S.C. § 1333(b), the structure of the OCSLA, and binding precedent from this Court compel the conclusion that Congress intended the LHWCA’s coverage to be geographically tethered to the outer Continental Shelf itself. The Ninth Circuit’s contrary holding, which imports a “substantial nexus” test, disregards the statutory text and undermines the carefully drawn boundary between federal and state jurisdiction over offshore operations.

### I. THE STATUTORY TEXT DEMANDS A SITUS-OF-INJURY REQUIREMENT

Section 1333(b) of the OCSLA provides compensation for “the disability or death of an employee resulting from any injury occurring as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf.” 43 U.S.C. § 1333(b) (emphasis added). The phrase “occurring as the result of” does not eliminate the need for a geographic nexus; rather, it defines the causal relationship between the injury and the covered operations. The injury must occur *as a result of* operations on the outer Continental Shelf—but the injury itself must occur *on* the outer Continental Shelf. This is not a mere semantic distinction. The statute’s use of “occurring” is a temporal and spatial marker. An injury cannot “occur” on the outer Continental Shelf if it happens on land, even if the injury is causally linked to offshore operations.

The Supreme Court has repeatedly emphasized that statutory interpretation must begin with the text. *See, e.g.,* *United States v. Ron Pair Enters., Inc.*, 489 U.S. 223, 231 (1989) (“[W]e begin with the language of the statute, for that is the most reliable indicator of congressional intent.”). Here, the text is unambiguous: the injury must occur *on* the outer Continental Shelf. The Court’s decision in *Herb’s Welding, Inc. v. Gray*, 470 U.S. 414 (1985), confirms this reading. There, the Court held that an employee injured on a platform in state waters was not entitled to OCSLA benefits, despite the platform’s functional connection to offshore operations. The Court observed that the “inconsistent coverage here results primarily from the explicit geographic limitation to [OCSLA’s] incorporation of the LHWCA.” *Id.* at 422. The Court further noted that the “statute draws a clear line between the outer Continental Shelf and the waters within state jurisdiction.” *Id.* at 423. This line is not merely jurisdictional—it is *situs-based*. The Court did not entertain a “substantial nexus” test; it enforced the geographic boundary.

### II. THE NINTH CIRCUIT’S “SUBSTANTIAL NEXUS” TEST CONFLICTS WITH BINDING PRECEDENT

The Ninth Circuit’s adoption of a “substantial nexus” test—under which an injury on land may qualify if it is “substantially related” to offshore operations—contradicts *Herb’s Welding* and undermines the statutory framework. The Court in *Herb’s Welding* rejected the very argument the Ninth Circuit now embraces: that an injury may be “as the result of” offshore operations even if it occurs outside the outer Continental Shelf. The Court explicitly rejected the idea that “the mere fact that an employee’s work is related to offshore operations” is sufficient to trigger OCSLA coverage. *Id.* at 422. Instead, the Court emphasized that the statute’s “geographic limitation” is “explicit” and “central” to its operation.

The Court’s footnote in *Offshore Logistics, Inc. v. Tallentire*, 477 U.S. 207, 219 n.2 (1986), cited by the Ninth Circuit, is not dispositive. The footnote merely notes that § 1333(b) “makes compensation . . . payable under the Longshoremen’s and Harbor Workers’ Compensation Act” and that the provision “has no bearing on this case.” *Id.* The Court did not analyze the situs requirement; it did not purport to resolve the question before us. As the Court has long held, “dicta are not binding.” *Humphrey’s Executor v. United States*, 295 U.S. 602, 627 (1935). The Ninth Circuit’s reliance on this unconsidered statement is misplaced.

### III. THE “SITUS-OF-INJURY” TEST PRESERVES THE FEDERAL-STATE JURISDICTIONAL BALANCE

Congress enacted the OCSLA to establish federal jurisdiction over the outer Continental Shelf and to provide a uniform system of workers’ compensation for offshore employees. *See* *Shell Oil Co. v. Iowa Dep’t of Revenue*, 488 U.S. 19, 25 (1988) (“Congress intended to provide ‘for the orderly development of offshore resources.’”); *Amoco Production Co. v. Village of Gambell*, 480 U.S. 531, 540 (1987) (OCSLA “defines the boundary between federal and state jurisdiction over submerged lands”). If the Ninth Circuit’s test were adopted, the federal workers’ compensation regime would extend to any injury occurring on land that is “substantially related” to offshore operations—regardless of location. This would create a federal “situs” of injury wherever a worker performs a task connected to offshore work, effectively erasing the geographic boundary Congress established.

Such a result would be inconsistent with the principle that “the law of the place where the injury occurs governs the right to compensation.” *Tipton v. Socony Mobil Oil Co.*, 375 U.S. 34, 37 (1963). The Ninth Circuit’s approach would allow federal law to displace state workers’ compensation schemes in cases where the injury occurs on land, even when the injury is not maritime in nature. This would undermine the federal-state balance Congress sought to preserve.
