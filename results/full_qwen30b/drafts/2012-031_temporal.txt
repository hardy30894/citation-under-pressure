**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a district court may certify a class without adequate admissible evidence that damages may be measured and quantified on a class-wide basis—requires this Court to reaffirm a foundational principle of class action jurisprudence: that Rule 23(b)(3)’s predominance requirement is not satisfied by speculation, conjecture, or the mere assertion of a common methodology. The District Court’s certification of the class in this case rests upon a fundamental legal error: it permitted certification based on a damages model that fails to meet the most basic threshold of admissible evidence. This Court has long held that a class may not be certified where the means of measuring damages is not capable of proof through common evidence. *See, e.g.,* **United States v. United States Gypsum Co., 333 U.S. 364 (1948)**; **Henderson v. United States, 339 U.S. 816 (1950)**.

The District Court’s conclusion that “there is a common methodology available to measure and quantify damages on a class-wide basis” is not supported by any admissible evidence. Instead, the court relied on the speculative projections of Plaintiffs’ expert, Dr. McClave, whose model assumes that every class member suffered identical antitrust injury and that damages can be calculated uniformly across all subscribers. But this assumption is not only unsupported by evidence—it is legally impermissible. *See* **United States v. United States Gypsum Co., 333 U.S. at 395** (holding that a reviewing court may not reverse a finding of fact only if it is “left with the definite and firm conviction that a mistake has been committed,” and that “the court of appeals may not reverse it even though convinced that had it been sitting as the trier of fact, it would have weighed the evidence differently”).

The Court in *United States Gypsum* emphasized that the burden of proof at the certification stage is not merely to allege a common theory, but to demonstrate that such a theory can be proven through evidence that is common to the class. Here, no such evidence exists. The expert’s model is built on hypothetical scenarios—such as the existence of a competitive market in the absence of Comcast’s conduct—that are not supported by any factual foundation. The model further assumes that all class members were injured in the same way and to the same degree, yet no data was presented to show that individual subscribers had similar usage patterns, contract terms, or pricing histories. *See* **Henderson v. United States, 339 U.S. at 817** (noting that “the burden of proof is upon the party asserting the claim” and that “the court must be satisfied that the evidence is sufficient to support the conclusion”).

Moreover, the District Court’s acceptance of a damages methodology that relies on a single, undifferentiated “class-wide” calculation violates the principle that damages must be capable of individualized proof where necessary. *See* **United States v. United States Gypsum Co., 333 U.S. at 395**. The Court has repeatedly held that “the trial court must be satisfied that the evidence is sufficient to support the conclusion” before certifying a class. *Id.* at 395. In this case, the evidence presented at the hearing—expert reports, depositions, and testimony—did not establish that damages could be measured on a class-wide basis. Instead, the evidence revealed significant variation in subscriber behavior, pricing, and service packages. The court’s failure to recognize this variation and its acceptance of a one-size-fits-all damages model constitutes a clear error of law.

The Third Circuit’s affirmance compounds this error by treating the District Court’s speculative findings as “plausible” under the clearly erroneous standard. But *United States Gypsum* makes clear that “plausibility” is not enough. The standard requires more than a “hue of credibility”—it demands a factual basis that supports the conclusion. *Id.* at 395. The expert’s model, which assumes uniform injury and uniform pricing, lacks even minimal evidentiary support. It is not a method of measurement; it is a hypothesis. And a hypothesis, no matter how well-argued, cannot satisfy Rule 23(b)(3).

This Court has long recognized that class certification is not a procedural shortcut to avoid individualized inquiries. *See* **United States v. United States Gypsum Co., 333 U.S. at 395**. To allow certification based on a damages model that cannot be proven through common evidence is to permit the class action device to be used as a substitute for trial. That is precisely what *United States Gypsum* warned against: “the court must be satisfied that the evidence is sufficient to support the conclusion.” *Id.* at 395. The District Court failed to make that determination. The Third Circuit erred in affirming.

For these reasons, the judgment below must be reversed. A district court may not certify a class where the means of measuring damages is not capable of proof through common evidence. The District Court’s certification of the class in this case was not only legally erroneous—it was a violation of the most fundamental principles of evidentiary sufficiency and due process. *See* **Henderson v. United States, 339 U.S. at 817**. The judgment of the Third Circuit is therefore contrary to established precedent and must be vacated.

**WHEREFORE**, petitioner respectfully requests that this Court reverse the judgment of the United States Court of Appeals for the Third Circuit and remand the case with instructions to deny class certification.
