The judgment should be affirmed. This Court’s fee-shifting cases do not establish a categorical rule forbidding any enhancement based on quality of performance or results obtained. To the contrary, the Court has repeatedly recognized that, in the “rare” and “exceptional” case, an enhancement may be appropriate when the lodestar does not fully reflect superior service and exceptional success. *Blum v. Stenson*, 465 U.S. 886, 898-901 (1984); *Pennsylvania v. Delaware Valley Citizens’ Council for Clean Air*, 478 U.S. 546, 565-68 (1986) (“Delaware Valley I”).

In *Blum*, the Court rejected the argument that quality and results can never matter under 42 U.S.C. § 1988. The Court stated that where a plaintiff has obtained “excellent results,” counsel should receive a “fully compensatory fee,” and that “in some cases of exceptional success an enhancement award may be justified.” 465 U.S. at 901. The Court explained that an enhancement may be warranted in the “rare case” where the applicant offers “specific evidence” that the quality of service rendered was superior to what reasonably should be expected in light of the hourly rates charged. Id. at 899. That is not a prohibition; it is a limitation demanding record support.

*Delaware Valley I* confirmed that understanding. The Court reiterated that “upward adjustments of the lodestar figure are still permissible” in “rare” and “exceptional” cases supported by “specific evidence” and “detailed findings.” 478 U.S. at 565. Although the Court also observed that the lodestar “includes most, if not all, of the relevant factors constituting a ‘reasonable’ attorney’s fee,” it did not hold that superior performance or exceptional results are irrelevant as a matter of law. Id. at 566. Instead, it rejected an across-the-board enhancement on the record before it because the lodestar ordinarily accounts for the relevant considerations. Id. at 566-68. That is a far cry from saying an enhancement is never allowed.

The Eleventh Circuit’s decision is consistent with those precedents. It did not authorize a routine bonus for winning; it held only that, on this record, the district court did not abuse its discretion in finding that counsel’s “skill, commitment, dedication, and professionalism” and the “exceptional nature of results” justified a modest enhancement. *Kenny A. v. Perdue*, 532 F.3d 1209, 1242 (11th Cir. 2008). That conclusion tracks the Supreme Court’s instruction that fee awards under fee-shifting statutes are entrusted to the district court’s informed discretion, subject to the requirement of specific evidentiary support. *Blum*, 465 U.S. at 899; *Delaware Valley I*, 478 U.S. at 565.

Petitioners’ contrary rule would improperly read the lodestar cases as imposing a categorical ban that the Court has never adopted. The Court has instead recognized that the lodestar is presumptively reasonable, not irrebuttably so. *Delaware Valley I*, 478 U.S. at 564-66. It has likewise acknowledged that “in some cases of exceptional success an enhancement award may be justified.” *Blum*, 465 U.S. at 901. Those decisions leave room for the uncommon case where superior advocacy and exceptional results are not adequately captured by hours and market rates alone. Id. at 899-901; 478 U.S. at 565-68.

Nor is there any sound administrability reason to impose petitioners’ absolute rule. Fee-shifting statutes are designed to ensure that prevailing parties can secure effective representation. *Hensley v. Eckerhart*, 461 U.S. 424, 429 (1983). When a district court makes detailed findings that counsel’s work was substantially above the norm and that the results were extraordinary, a limited enhancement is a permissible way to make the fee “reasonable.” *Blum*, 465 U.S. at 897-901; *Delaware Valley I*, 478 U.S. at 565-68.

This Court should therefore hold that reasonable fees under a federal fee-shifting statute may, in an appropriate and rare case, be enhanced based on quality of performance and results obtained, provided the enhancement is supported by specific evidence and detailed findings. Because the district court here made precisely that determination, and the court of appeals correctly sustained it for abuse of discretion, the judgment should be affirmed.
