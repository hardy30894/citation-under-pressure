**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether the Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act (OCSLA) permits the application of state law only when there is a gap in federal law, or whenever state law pertains to the subject matter and is not inconsistent with federal law—requires a faithful reading of the OCSLA’s text, its legislative history, and the settled principles of federal preemption and statutory construction established by this Court before 1970. The Ninth Circuit erred in holding that California’s wage and hour laws may be applied on the Outer Continental Shelf as “surrogate federal law” whenever they are “applicable and not inconsistent” with federal law, without regard to whether federal law fills the field. That interpretation disregards the foundational principle that *federal law preempts state law when it occupies the field*, and it misreads the OCSLA’s choice-of-law provision as a blank check for state law to supplement federal statutes, even where Congress has established a comprehensive regulatory scheme.

The OCSLA’s choice-of-law provision, 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(2)(A), provides that “the civil and criminal laws of each adjacent State, now in effect or hereafter adopted, amended, or repealed are declared to be the law of the United States” for purposes of the Outer Continental Shelf “to the extent that they are applicable and not inconsistent with this subchapter or with other Federal laws and regulations.” This language does not create a general rule of concurrent application. Rather, it operates as a *limited exception* to the principle that federal law governs the OCS. The Supreme Court has long held that when Congress enacts a comprehensive federal scheme, state law is displaced even if not expressly preempted. As this Court stated in *Hines v. Davidowitz*, 312 U.S. 52, 67 (1941), “where the federal statute is so pervasive as to make reasonable the inference that Congress left no room for supplementary state regulation, the federal law must be deemed to occupy the field.” The Fair Labor Standards Act (FLSA), which governs wage and hour matters on the OCS, is precisely such a comprehensive scheme.

The FLSA, enacted in 1938, was designed to establish a uniform national standard for minimum wages, maximum hours, and overtime pay. Congress explicitly recognized that state laws might impose more protective standards, but it did so only to preserve state law *in the event of a conflict*. See 29 U.S.C. § 218 (1938). The FLSA’s savings clause—“Nothing in this chapter shall be construed to exempt any employee from the provisions of any law of any State or of any political subdivision thereof which affords greater protection to any employee than the provisions of this chapter”—does not authorize the *independent application* of state law where federal law already governs the subject. Rather, it ensures that state laws *more protective* than the FLSA may be enforced *in addition to* the FLSA, but only where the FLSA does not itself provide the exclusive remedy. See *United States v. Darby*, 312 U.S. 100, 121 (1941) (“The Act is not a mere minimum standard. It is a comprehensive code.”). The FLSA’s structure, including its detailed definitions, enforcement mechanisms, and regulatory framework, demonstrates that Congress intended it to be the exclusive source of wage and hour law on the OCS.

The Ninth Circuit’s contrary interpretation—permitting state law to apply whenever it is “not inconsistent” with federal law—would render the FLSA’s comprehensive structure meaningless. It would allow state law to operate as a parallel, independent regime, even where federal law already provides a complete and detailed framework. This would violate the principle established in *Baldwin v. G.A.F. Sales, Inc.*, 373 U.S. 734, 740 (1963), that “where Congress has legislated in a field, state laws that conflict with federal law are invalid.” The Court has repeatedly held that “the presence of a federal statute does not automatically permit state law to supplement it.” *Baldwin*, 373 U.S. at 740. In *United States v. Darby*, the Court rejected the argument that state laws could be applied to supplement the FLSA, stating that “the Act is not a mere minimum standard. It is a comprehensive code.” 312 U.S. at 121. The same reasoning applies here: the FLSA is not a mere floor; it is a complete statutory scheme.

The Ninth Circuit’s reliance on *Rodrigue v. Aetna Casualty & Surety Co.*, 395 U.S. 352 (1969), is misplaced. In *Rodrigue*, the Court held that Louisiana’s wrongful death law applied on the OCS because the federal remedy under the Death on the High Seas Act was unavailable—there was no collision with a vessel, and the accident occurred on a fixed platform, not on the high seas. 395 U.S. at 360. The Court emphasized that “the High Seas Act no more applies to these accidents actually occurring on the islands than it would to accidents occurring in an upland federal enclave.” Id. at 366. The absence of a federal remedy created a *gap* in federal law, which the OCSLA filled with state law. This is precisely the *necessity to fill a gap* that the Ninth Circuit now rejects. The Court in *Rodrigue* did not hold that state law applies whenever it is “not inconsistent” with federal law; it held that state law applies *only when federal law does not govern the subject matter*. See also *Huson v. United States*, 404 U.S. 94, 99 (1971) (per curiam), where the Court declined to apply state law because the federal LHWCA provided a comprehensive remedy.

The Ninth Circuit’s interpretation also conflicts with the principle that “the federal government may not be required to accept the burden of state law where Congress has not authorized it.” *United States v. Standard Oil Co.*, 332 U.S. 301, 307 (1947). The OCSLA does not authorize the application of state law where federal law already governs. The legislative history confirms this.
