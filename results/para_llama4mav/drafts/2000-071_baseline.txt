The exclusion of the Good News Club from meeting after hours at Milford Central School did not violate the Club's First Amendment free speech rights. The school's community use policy, which prohibits use of its facilities for "religious purposes," is a permissible restriction on speech in a limited public forum. As the Second Circuit correctly held, the Club's activities, which included singing songs, hearing Bible lessons, memorizing scripture, and praying, were "quintessentially religious" and thus fell outside the scope of the school's limited public forum. See Good News Club v. Milford Central School, 202 F.3d 502 (2d Cir. 2000).

The Supreme Court has recognized that a limited public forum is subject to certain restrictions on speech, as long as those restrictions are viewpoint-neutral and reasonable in light of the purpose served by the forum. See Rosenberger v. Rector & Visitors of Univ. of Va., 515 U.S. 819, 829 (1995). Here, Milford's policy prohibiting use for "religious purposes" is a reasonable restriction, as it is designed to avoid the appearance of endorsing or promoting religious activities. See Lamb's Chapel v. Center Moriches Union Free School Dist., 508 U.S. 384, 395 (1993) (upholding a similar policy as "reasonable" and "viewpoint-neutral").

Furthermore, the Club's activities cannot be characterized as merely presenting a "religious viewpoint" on an otherwise permissible subject, such as morals and character development. As the Second Circuit observed, the Club's activities "fall outside the bounds of pure 'moral and character development'" and are instead "the equivalent of religious instruction and prayer." Good News Club, 202 F.3d at 502. The Club's own materials and lesson plans demonstrate that its primary purpose is to instruct children in Christian doctrine and promote evangelism, which is a distinct subject matter that falls outside the scope of the school's limited public forum.

The dissenting opinion in the Second Circuit suggests that the Club's activities may be seen as presenting a "religious viewpoint" on morals and character, but this interpretation is not supported by the record. The Club's activities are not merely a "viewpoint" on a secular subject, but rather a comprehensive program of religious instruction and devotion. As such, they are properly excluded from the school's limited public forum under Milford's community use policy.

Moreover, permitting the Club's activities would have raised Establishment Clause concerns, as it would have created the appearance of official endorsement or promotion of religious activities. See Lee v. Weisman, 505 U.S. 577, 592-93 (1992) (noting that "subtle coercive pressure" can be exerted on students when a school appears to endorse or promote religious activities). Milford's exclusion of the Club from its facilities was thus justified by its concern for avoiding an Establishment Clause violation.

In sum, Milford Central School did not violate the Good News Club's First Amendment free speech rights by excluding it from meeting after hours at the school. The school's community use policy is a reasonable and viewpoint-neutral restriction on speech in a limited public forum, and the Club's activities fall outside the scope of that forum.
