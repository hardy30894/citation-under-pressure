## ARGUMENT

A state prisoner may establish cause to excuse a procedural default of a federal habeas claim by demonstrating constitutionally ineffective assistance of counsel that caused the default, even if the ineffective-assistance claim itself is procedurally defaulted.

**I. Cause for Procedural Default Includes Ineffective Assistance of Counsel**

A federal habeas petitioner who has procedurally defaulted a claim in state court must demonstrate “cause” for the default and “actual prejudice” to obtain review. *Wainwright v. Sykes*, 433 U.S. 72, 84 (1977); *United States v. Frady*, 456 U.S. 152, 167-68 (1982). Cause exists where “some objective factor external to the defense impeded counsel’s efforts to comply with the State’s procedural rule.” *Murray v. Carrier*, 477 U.S. 478, 488 (1986). Ineffective assistance of counsel that violates the Sixth Amendment qualifies as such an external factor. *Id.* at 488-89.

The right to effective assistance extends to a first appeal as of right. *Evitts v. Lucey*, 469 U.S. 387, 396-97 (1985); *see also Strickland v. Washington*, 466 U.S. 668, 687 (1984) (establishing performance-and-prejudice standard). When appellate counsel unreasonably fails to raise a meritorious claim, that failure constitutes cause for the default of that claim.

**II. A Procedurally Defaulted Ineffective-Assistance Claim May Serve as Cause**

Here, the Ohio courts dismissed Carpenter’s ineffective-assistance-of-appellate-counsel claim as untimely. The court of appeals correctly held that this procedural default does not bar Carpenter from using that claim to excuse the default of his sufficiency-of-the-evidence claim. The state procedural bar on the ineffective-assistance claim does not destroy its ability to serve as cause.

*First*, *Carrier* requires only that an ineffective-assistance claim be “presented to the state courts as an independent claim before it may be used to establish cause.” 477 U.S. at 489. Carpenter presented his claim; Ohio rejected it as untimely. The requirement of presentation ensures the state an opportunity to correct its own error. *See Harris v. Reed*, 489 U.S. 255, 264 (1989) (procedural default respects state interests). That opportunity was provided. *Carrier* does not demand that the claim survive procedural scrutiny. The very purpose of the cause-excuse doctrine is to allow federal review when a state procedural bar would otherwise foreclose it. Requiring the claim to be free of any procedural flaw would render the cause doctrine circular.

*Second*, *Coleman v. Thompson*, 501 U.S. 722 (1991), is distinguishable and supports respondent. *Coleman* held that ineffective assistance in a state post-conviction proceeding cannot constitute cause because there is no constitutional right to counsel in such proceedings. *Id.* at 752. By negative implication, where a constitutional right to counsel exists—as on direct appeal—ineffective assistance can constitute cause. *See id.* at 755. *Coleman* further stated that if an ineffective-assistance claim is procedurally defaulted, “the prisoner must demonstrate cause for the default and prejudice from the asserted error.” *Id.* at 753. This dictum should not be extended to a case like this one. Requiring separate cause for the claim that itself serves as cause would create an infinite regress, turning habeas into an “inextricable procedural conundra.” *Fay v. Noia*, 372 U.S. 391, 438 (1963) (recognizing habeas as an equitable remedy). This Court has never imposed such a chain of cause upon cause.

*Third*, the fundamental nature of an ineffective-assistance claim reinforces this conclusion. Carpenter’s claim alleges that his appellate counsel violated the Sixth Amendment, stripping him of a critical safeguard on direct appeal. *See Evitts*, 469 U.S. at 396. That constitutional violation directly caused the default of his sufficiency claim: counsel failed to raise it. The procedural default of the ineffective-assistance claim—often resulting from the same deficient performance or a related procedural oversight—does not sever the causal connection. As *Carrier* holds, cause exists if a “violation of the Sixth Amendment occurred.” 477 U.S. at 488. The violation occurred here. To insist that Carpenter also show cause for the default of the claim asserting that violation would insulate a constitutional error from federal habeas review, undermining the writ’s historic function. *See Fay*, 372 U.S. at 401-02.

The judgment of the Court of Appeals should be affirmed.
