### Argument

The judgment should be affirmed because a district court’s failure to give the Rule 32(a)(2) appeal advisement does not itself entitle a defendant to habeas relief when the defendant already knew of the right to appeal and chose not to pursue it. Habeas corpus is not a mechanism for setting aside a conviction or sentence for every procedural misstep; it lies only for violations that work a constitutional or fundamental miscarriage of justice. See *Hill v. United States*, 368 U.S. 424, 428 (1962) (collateral relief is not available for “failure to comply with the formal requirements” of a rule); *Sunal v. Large*, 332 U.S. 174, 178 (1947) (habeas is not a substitute for appeal); *Sunal v. Large*, 332 U.S. 174, 181 (1947) (“The writ of habeas corpus is not to be used as a substitute for the regular appellate process.”); *Glasser v. United States*, 315 U.S. 60, 70 (1942); *Wiborg v. United States*, 163 U.S. 632, 658 (1896).

Rule 32(a)(2) was designed to protect a defendant who might otherwise lose the opportunity to appeal because he did not know the right existed. Where that concern is absent because the defendant was actually aware of the right, the omission is harmless. This Court has repeatedly held that not every violation of a criminal rule requires vacatur. In *United States v. Arena*, 180 F.2d 139, 145 (2d Cir. 1950), the court rejected automatic reversal for a procedural defect absent prejudice. The same principle applies here: once the district court found that Peguero knew of his right to appeal at sentencing, the omitted advisement caused no prejudice and therefore affords no basis for collateral relief.

That conclusion is fully consistent with the longstanding harmless-error principle. In *Fahy v. Connecticut*, 375 U.S. 85, 86–87 (1963), the Court asked whether the error had any impact on the result. A defendant who already knows of the right to appeal is in the same position whether or not the judge recites the advisement on the record; the omission does not affect the defendant’s choice, the availability of review, or the fairness of the proceeding.

The Court also should reject petitioner’s invitation to treat every Rule 32 violation as automatically voiding the sentence. This Court has long refused to equate all procedural irregularities with jurisdictional defects. *Hopt v. Utah*, 110 U.S. 574, 579 (1884), recognizes that only errors affecting the substantial rights of the accused warrant relief. *Hill v. United States*, 368 U.S. 424, 428 (1962), confirms that collateral relief does not lie for a mere formal omission.

Petitioner’s position would also create an unwarranted collateral remedy for a purely formal omission. This Court has repeatedly emphasized finality in criminal judgments. *Ex parte Watkins*, 28 U.S. (3 Pet.) 193, 202–03 (1830), limits collateral review to defects of jurisdiction or fundamental legality. *Sunal v. Large*, 332 U.S. 174, 336–37 (1940), makes clear that habeas is not a substitute for appeal. Where the record shows the defendant already knew of the appeal right and deliberately elected not to appeal, there is no miscarriage of justice to correct.

Finally, the district court’s factual finding that Peguero knew of his right to appeal is controlling unless clearly erroneous. See *United States v. United States Gypsum Co.*, 333 U.S. 364, 395 (1948). On that finding, the Rule 32 omission was harmless and nonprejudicial. Because habeas relief is reserved for substantial violations, not empty formalities, the court of appeals correctly affirmed.

For these reasons, the judgment below should be affirmed.
