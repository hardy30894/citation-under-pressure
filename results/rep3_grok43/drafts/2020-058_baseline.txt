ARGUMENT

I. A Grant of TPS Satisfies § 1255(a)’s Admission Requirement.

Section 1254a(f)(4) provides that an alien granted TPS “shall be considered as being in, and maintaining, lawful status as a nonimmigrant” for purposes of adjustment under § 1255. This language supplies the functional equivalent of admission. Ramirez v. Brown, 852 F.3d 954, 960 (9th Cir. 2017). Because obtaining nonimmigrant status ordinarily requires inspection and authorization, the statutory directive that TPS recipients be treated as nonimmigrants necessarily includes the requisite admission. Id.

The INA’s definitions confirm the point. Although “admission” is defined as “the lawful entry of the alien into the United States after inspection and authorization,” 8 U.S.C. § 1101(a)(13)(A), Congress may and does create legal fictions that equate certain grants of status with admission. See, e.g., 8 U.S.C. § 1255(h), (i) (express exceptions to the admission prerequisite). Section 1254a(f)(4) operates in the same manner. Flores v. U.S. Citizenship & Immigration Servs., 718 F.3d 548, 553 (6th Cir. 2013).

The structure of § 1255 reinforces this reading. Subsection (a) conditions adjustment on inspection and admission or parole; subsection (c)(2) separately bars aliens who have failed to maintain lawful status. Treating TPS as satisfying the former while excusing lapses in the latter gives independent effect to each provision. Ramirez, 852 F.3d at 961. The contrary view would render § 1254a(f)(4) largely superfluous.

Finally, the TPS statute’s purpose—to permit eligible aliens to remain in the United States without fear of removal—would be undermined if recipients who entered without inspection were required to depart and reenter before adjusting status. Flores, 718 F.3d at 555. Congress did not impose that obstacle.

For these reasons, the judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
