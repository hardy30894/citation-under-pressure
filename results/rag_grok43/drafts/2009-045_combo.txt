The reasonable attorneys’ fee award under a federal fee-shifting statute may be enhanced based on the quality of performance and results obtained. Courts have long recognized that compensation for legal services must reflect the skill, care, and success actually rendered. Savings Bank v. Ward, 100 U.S. 195, 200 (1880) (attorneys “impliedly contract to exercise reasonable care and skill” and “shall be responsible” for resulting loss if they fail); Bachman v. Lawson, 109 U.S. 659, 662 (1884) (court shall allow “such reasonable counsel and attorney fees … as the court shall determine is just and reasonable, as compensation for the services rendered”).

This principle permits upward adjustment when the record shows superior representation yielding exceptional results. Missouri State Life Ins. v. Jones, 290 U.S. 199, 202 (1933) (attorney’s fee “must be taxed as costs” when statute so directs, without altering its true nature as enforceable liability); United States v. Waters, 133 U.S. 208, 212 (1890) (discretionary fees “allowed by the court” remain incident to judgment and within sound discretion of the tribunal before whom the cause was tried); Hilborn v. United States, 163 U.S. 342, 345 (1896) (fees earned for services performed are properly returned as emoluments when they constitute part of official duties).

Enhancement is therefore available, not categorically barred, when specific evidence demonstrates that ordinary compensation would not fairly reflect the quality of service or the outcome achieved. Hopkins v. Cohen, 390 U.S. 530, 534 (1968) (maximum fee limitation applies only to the claimant actually represented; additional representation may support separate recovery); 149 Madison Ave. Corp. v. Asselta, 331 U.S. 199, 204 (1947) (hourly rate derived from agreed formula remains constant and must compensate actual hours worked, including adjustments for excess); Walling v. Harnischfeger Corp., 325 U.S. 427, 432 (1945) (bonus or additional payment cannot be allocated so as to defeat required compensation for all hours; the wage “must therefore be correspondingly increased”); United States v. Cors, 337 U.S. 325, 332 (1949) (just compensation measured by market value on date of taking, without artificial deduction that would understate the true award).

These authorities confirm that district courts retain discretion to enhance a fee award when the lodestar alone fails to account for superior performance and exceptional success. Newman v. Piggie Park Enters., Inc., 390 U.S. 400, 402 (1968) (successful plaintiff “should ordinarily recover an attorney’s fee” to advance the public interest). The judgment below should be affirmed.
