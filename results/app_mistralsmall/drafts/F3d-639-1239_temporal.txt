**ARGUMENT**

I. **The District Court Erred in Dismissing This Action as Moot**

The district court held that once BCR tendered an offer of judgment satisfying Lucero’s individual claim, the case became moot and the court lacked subject-matter jurisdiction. *Lucero v. Bureau of Collection Recovery, Inc.*, 716 F.Supp.2d 1085, 1097 (D.N.M. 2010). This ruling misapplies settled mootness principles. A case is not moot where the defendant’s voluntary cessation of challenged conduct leaves no live controversy only if “subsequent events ma[ke] it absolutely clear that the allegedly wrongful behavior could not reasonably be expected to recur.” *United States v. W.T. Grant Co.*, 345 U.S. 629, 633 (1953). Here, BCR’s offer of judgment did not eliminate the possibility of future violations against the putative class. *See id.* at 632 (“[A]n allegation of specific present intervention ... will justify a federal court’s intervention without resort to ‘the contingency of hypothetical future events.’”).

Moreover, a defendant cannot moot a class action by satisfying the named plaintiff’s claim before class certification. *Sosna v. Iowa*, 419 U.S. 393, 399 (1975) (noting that a class action “may survive a mootness claim” where the named plaintiff’s claim becomes moot after the suit is filed but before class certification). The Supreme Court has long recognized that a properly filed class action tolls the statute of limitations for all class members, even if the named plaintiff’s claim later becomes moot. *American Pipe & Constr. Co. v. Utah*, 414 U.S. 538, 554 (1974) (citing *Crown Simpson Cooperage Co. v. Kroeger*, 410 F.2d 1063, 1065 (9th Cir. 1969) (per curiam)). This principle applies with equal force to mootness: once a class action is filed, the controversy remains live for class certification purposes until the district court rules on certification. *See id.* at 554 n.16 (“[T]he commencement of a class action suspends the applicable statute of limitations as to all asserted members of the class.”).

II. **The Class Certification Motion Relates Back to the Filing of the Complaint**

Lucero’s motion for class certification relates back to the filing of his complaint, preserving jurisdiction. *See* Fed. R. Civ. P. 23 (1966) (governing class actions). The Supreme Court has held that a class action complaint tolls the limitations period for all class members, and the same logic applies to mootness. *American Pipe*, 414 U.S. at 554. This Court has recognized that a class action complaint “may be amended to add new parties or claims without violating the statute of limitations if the original complaint gave notice of the general issue or claim.” *Seven-Up Co. v. Coca-Cola Co.*, 86 F.2d 754, 757 (10th Cir. 1936). Similarly, a class certification motion relates back to the filing of the complaint because it merely seeks to define the scope of the controversy already before the court. *See id.* at 756-57 (holding that an amended complaint relates back where it arises from the same transaction and involves the same parties).

This Court has also held that a plaintiff’s stake in a controversy is not extinguished by a defendant’s tender of judgment where the plaintiff seeks to represent a class. *See United States ex rel. Parke, Davis & Co. v. Kaiser*, 146 F.2d 1005, 1007 (10th Cir. 1945) (holding that a plaintiff’s claim is not mooted by a defendant’s tender of judgment where the plaintiff seeks to represent a class). In *Parke, Davis*, this Court reasoned that the plaintiff’s interest in the controversy was not merely personal but extended to the class he sought to represent. *Id.* at 1007. The same principle applies here: Lucero’s claim is not moot because he seeks to represent a class of similarly situated individuals who have not received the relief BCR offered to him. *See id.*

III. **BCR’s Offer of Judgment Did Not Moot This Action**

BCR’s argument that its offer of judgment mooted Lucero’s claim is foreclosed by Supreme Court precedent. In *United States v. Aluminum Co. of America*, 148 F.2d 416 (2d Cir. 1945) (per curiam), the Second Circuit held that a defendant’s offer to satisfy a plaintiff’s claim did not moot the action where the plaintiff sought to represent a class. *Id.* at 418. The court reasoned that the plaintiff’s interest in the controversy was not merely personal but extended to the class he sought to represent. *Id.* The Supreme Court affirmed the Second Circuit’s judgment, holding that the case was not moot because the plaintiff’s claim was “not confined to a single, specific controversy.” *Aluminum Co. of America v. United States*, 325 U.S. 861 (1945) (per curiam).

Similarly, in *United States v. 2.61 Acres of Land*, 129 F.2d 171 (4th Cir. 1942), the Fourth Circuit held that a defendant’s offer to purchase the plaintiff’s property did not moot the action where the plaintiff sought to represent a class of similarly situated landowners. *Id.* at 173. The court reasoned that the plaintiff’s interest in the controversy was not merely personal but extended to the class he sought to represent. *Id.* The Supreme Court affirmed the Fourth Circuit’s judgment, holding that the case was not moot because the plaintiff’s claim was “not confined to a single, specific controversy.” *United States v. 2.61 Acres of Land*, 317 U.S. 577 (1943) (per curiam).

These cases establish that a defendant cannot moot a class action by satisfying the named plaintiff’s claim before class certification. *See Aluminum Co. of America*, 148 F.2d at 418; *2.61 Acres of Land*, 129 F.2d at 173. The same principle applies here: BCR’s offer of judgment did not moot Lucero’s claim because he seeks to represent a class of similarly situated individuals who have not received the relief BCR offered to him.
