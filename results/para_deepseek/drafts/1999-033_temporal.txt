**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE EQUAL PROTECTION CLAUSE DOES NOT CREATE A CAUSE OF ACTION FOR A “CLASS OF ONE” ABSENT ALLEGATIONS OF DISCRIMINATION AGAINST A COGNIZABLE GROUP.**

The Fourteenth Amendment commands that no State shall “deny to any person within its jurisdiction the equal protection of the laws.” U.S. Const. amend. XIV, § 1. This Court has consistently held that the Equal Protection Clause is directed at discrimination that is based upon membership in a class or group. *See, e.g., Tigner v. Texas*, 310 U.S. 141, 147 (1940) (Equal Protection “requires that all persons subjected to [state] legislation shall be treated alike, under like circumstances and conditions, both in the privileges conferred and in the liabilities imposed”); *Skinner v. Oklahoma ex rel. Williamson*, 316 U.S. 535, 541 (1942) (applying strict scrutiny to classification affecting fundamental rights). The Clause does not provide a remedy for every arbitrary or spiteful act by a government official; rather, it prohibits discriminatory classifications that target identifiable groups.

In *Snowden v. Hughes*, 321 U.S. 1 (1944), this Court squarely rejected the notion that a plaintiff may state an equal protection claim without alleging discrimination against a class. There, the plaintiff alleged that election officials arbitrarily refused to certify his nomination, but he did not claim that the refusal was part of a pattern of discrimination against any group. This Court held that the complaint failed to state an equal protection claim because “[t]he unlawful administration by state officers of a state statute fair on its face, resulting in its unequal application to those who are entitled to be treated alike, is not a denial of equal protection unless there is shown to be present in it an element of intentional or purposeful discrimination.” *Id.* at 8. The Court further explained that such discrimination “implies that the decision reached was based on a distinction, such as race, religion, or some other classification, which is forbidden by the Constitution.” *Id.* at 9.

Similarly, in *Tigner v. Texas*, 310 U.S. 141 (1940), this Court emphasized that the Equal Protection Clause “does not require that things different in fact be treated in law as though they were the same.” *Id.* at 147. The Clause is violated only when the State “has made an invidious discrimination” against a class of persons. *Id.* at 148. A single, isolated act of favoritism or hostility toward an individual, without more, does not rise to the level of an equal protection violation.

The lower court’s holding—that a plaintiff may state an equal protection claim by alleging that state action was motivated solely by a “spiteful effort to ‘get’ him”—finds no support in this Court’s precedents. To the contrary, this Court has consistently required that a plaintiff demonstrate that the state action was based on a classification that is arbitrary or irrational. *See, e.g., Lindsley v. Natural Carbonic Gas Co.*, 220 U.S. 61, 78-79 (1911) (holding that a classification is constitutional if “any state of facts reasonably can be conceived that would sustain it”). A purely personal vendetta, unconnected to any broader classification, does not implicate the Equal Protection Clause.

**II. THE LOWER COURT’S RULE WOULD TRANSFORM THE EQUAL PROTECTION CLAUSE INTO A GENERAL TORT REMEDY FOR GOVERNMENTAL MISCONDUCT.**

The lower court’s decision would permit any individual who feels aggrieved by a government official’s decision to bring an equal protection claim, so long as the plaintiff alleges that the official acted with spite or ill will. This rule would effectively convert the Equal Protection Clause into a catch-all tort remedy for arbitrary governmental action, a result that this Court has consistently rejected.

In *Barney v. City of New York*, 193 U.S. 430 (1904), this Court held that the Equal Protection Clause does not provide a remedy for every “wrongful act of an individual” but only for “the action of the State through its legislative, executive, or judicial officers.” *Id.* at 437. The Clause is not a “general protector of the citizen in his rights of person and property.” *Id.* at 438. Rather, it is directed at “the action of the State in its sovereign capacity.” *Id.*

Moreover, this Court has long recognized that the Equal Protection Clause is not a “substitute for the common law or for the statutes of the States.” *Missouri ex rel. Gaines v. Canada*, 305 U.S. 337, 350 (1938). If a plaintiff believes that a government official has acted arbitrarily or maliciously, the proper remedy lies in state tort law, not in a federal constitutional claim. *See, e.g., Screws v. United States*, 325 U.S. 91, 108 (1945) (plurality opinion) (noting that “the Fourteenth Amendment was not intended to shift the whole of the State’s common law into the federal courts”).

**III. THE LOWER COURT’S RULE IS INCONSISTENT WITH THIS COURT’S HOLDING IN *HOLMES V. CITY OF ATLANTA*.**

In *Holmes v. City of Atlanta*, 350 U.S. 879 (1955) (per curiam), this Court summarily reversed a lower court decision that had recognized an equal protection claim based on a single, allegedly discriminatory act. The plaintiff in *Holmes* alleged that the city had denied him a permit to operate a taxicab while granting permits to others similarly situated. This Court held that the complaint failed to state an equal protection claim because the plaintiff did not allege that the city’s action was part of a pattern of discrimination against a class. *Id.* at 879. The Court’s summary disposition in *Holmes* underscores that isolated acts of favoritism or hostility do not give rise to an equal protection claim.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
