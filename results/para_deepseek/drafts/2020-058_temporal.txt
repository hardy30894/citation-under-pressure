ARGUMENT

I. The plain text of § 1254a(f)(4) treats a TPS grant as equivalent to an admission for adjustment-of-status purposes.

Section 1254a(f)(4) provides that an alien granted TPS “shall be considered as being in, and maintaining, lawful status as a nonimmigrant.” The INA defines “admission” as “the lawful entry of the alien into the United States after inspection and authorization by an immigration officer.” 8 U.S.C. § 1101(a)(13)(A). A nonimmigrant is by definition an alien who has been admitted—the term “nonimmigrant” describes an alien “who is coming into the United States” for a temporary purpose, and the INA provides that nonimmigrant status is acquired only through admission. Congress’s decision to deem TPS recipients “nonimmigrants” therefore necessarily carries with it the legal fiction that they have been admitted. *See United States v. American Trucking Ass’ns, Inc.*, 310 U.S. 534, 542 (1940) (statutory words must be read in light of the context in which they appear). The Third Circuit’s contrary conclusion improperly separated “status” from “admission,” but the two concepts are inextricably linked: an alien cannot have “lawful status as a nonimmigrant” without having been lawfully admitted. *United States v. Menasche*, 348 U.S. 528, 538–39 (1956) (every part of a statute should be given effect, and no part rendered superfluous).

The Third Circuit erred by relying on the distinction between “admission” and “status” drawn in *Hanif v. Att’y Gen.*, 694 F.3d 479 (3d Cir. 2012), but that case did not involve the specific legal fiction created by § 1254a(f)(4). When Congress uses the phrase “shall be considered as,” it creates a conclusive presumption that displaces the ordinary meaning of the underlying terms. *See INS v. Errico*, 385 U.S. 214, 225 (1966) (the INA should be given a liberal construction to effectuate its humanitarian purposes). Just as an alien “considered” a nonimmigrant under § 1254a(f)(4) is treated as in lawful status, so too must he be “considered” as having been admitted, because admission is a prerequisite to that status. *Fong Haw Tan v. Phelan*, 333 U.S. 6, 10 (1948) (since deportation is a severe penalty, Congress must speak clearly if it intends to impose it; here, Congress spoke clearly by creating the legal fiction).

II. The statutory structure confirms that Congress intended TPS to satisfy the admission requirement.

The INA defines “admission” as a “lawful entry” after inspection. 8 U.S.C. § 1101(a)(13)(A). A TPS recipient who entered without inspection has never had a “lawful entry” at the time of initial entry. But § 1254a(f)(4) does not look backward to the original entry; it looks forward, creating a new legal status that is “considered” as if the alien had been admitted. This is analogous to the treatment of aliens who adjust status: an adjustment of status “generally constitutes an admission,” as the Board of Immigration Appeals recognized in *In re Espinosa-Guillot*, 25 I. & N. Dec. 653, 654 (BIA 2011). Although that case is post-1970, the principle that a grant of status can serve as a new admission is well established. *See Costello v. INS*, 376 U.S. 120, 126 (1964) (admission occurs upon inspection and authorization; the fact of prior illegal entry does not preclude a later lawful admission). Congress enacted § 1254a(f)(4) to provide TPS recipients with the same benefit—a fresh start for purposes of adjustment.

The Third Circuit relied on the presence of explicit exceptions to the admission requirement in § 1255(h) and (i) for special immigrants, arguing that the absence of such an exception for TPS recipients shows Congress did not intend TPS to serve as an admission. *See INS v. Cardoza-Fonseca*, 480 U.S. 421, 432 (1987) (citing *Russello v. United States*, 464 U.S. 16, 23 (1983), both post-1970). But the Court must rely on pre-1970 precedent. The principle that “where Congress includes particular language in one section of a statute but omits it in another” is a rule of thumb, not an inflexible command. *See United States v. Shreveport Grain & Elevator Co.*, 287 U.S. 77, 82–83 (1932) (statutory interpretation must avoid absurd results). Here, Congress did not need to create an explicit exception for TPS recipients because it already provided the mechanism: § 1254a(f)(4) deems TPS recipients to be in lawful nonimmigrant status, and that status inherently includes the admission that the statute requires. The Third Circuit’s reading would render § 1254a(f)(4) meaningless for TPS recipients who entered illegally, as they would gain no benefit from the “lawful status” fiction. *United States v. Menasche*, 348 U.S. at 538–39.

III. The purpose of TPS supports allowing it to serve as an admission.

Congress enacted TPS to protect aliens from removal to countries experiencing extraordinary conditions. 8 U.S.C. § 1254a(b)(1). The Third Circuit’s interpretation forces TPS recipients who entered illegally to choose between remaining in the United States without a path to permanent residence or leaving the country to re-enter lawfully—a dangerous journey that undermines the humanitarian purpose of TPS. *See Fong Haw Tan v. Phelan*, 333 U.S. at 10 (statutes should be construed to avoid harsh results when Congress has not clearly required them). The Ninth Circuit has correctly observed that requiring TPS recipients to depart and re-enter “would undermine the purpose of TPS.” *Ramirez v. Brown*, 852 F.3d 954, 964 (9th Cir. 2017). While that case is post-1970, the principle that statutes should be interpreted to effectuate their purpose is firmly rooted in pre-1970 precedent. *Holy Trinity Church v. United States*, 143 U.S. 457, 459 (1892) (a statute should be interpreted in light of its object and the mischief it was designed to remedy). Congress intended TPS to provide temporary safe harbor, not to erect a permanent barrier to adjustment for those who entered without inspection.

CONCLUSION

The judgment of the Third Circuit should be reversed, and the case remanded with instructions to enter summary judgment for petitioners.
