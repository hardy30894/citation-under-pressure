The exclusion of the Good News Club from meeting after hours at Milford Central School did not violate the Club's First Amendment free speech rights. The school's community use policy, which prohibits use of its facilities for religious purposes, is a permissible restriction on speech in a limited public forum.

The Supreme Court has long recognized that the government may create a limited public forum, opening its property for use by the public for certain purposes, without being required to allow all types of speech. See Perry Education Assn. v. Perry Local Educators' Assn., 460 U.S. 37 (1983) (citing cases). In a limited public forum, the government may impose reasonable restrictions on speech, as long as they are viewpoint-neutral. See id.; see also Niemotko v. Maryland, 340 U.S. 268 (1951).

The Milford Central School's community use policy is a reasonable restriction on speech in a limited public forum. The policy prohibits use of school facilities for "religious purposes," which is a clear and content-based restriction. See Chaplinsky v. New Hampshire, 315 U.S. 568 (1942) (upholding a statute that prohibited certain types of speech, including "offensive, derisive or annoying" words). The policy is not aimed at suppressing a particular viewpoint, but rather at maintaining the separation of church and state. See Everson v. Board of Education, 330 U.S. 1 (1947) (upholding a statute that prohibited the use of public funds for transportation to parochial schools).

The Good News Club's activities, which include singing songs, hearing Bible lessons, memorizing scripture, and praying, are quintessentially religious and fall outside the bounds of the school's limited public forum. See Cantwell v. Connecticut, 310 U.S. 296 (1940) (distinguishing between religious speech and other types of speech). The Club's activities are not merely a discussion of morals and character from a religious perspective, but rather a form of religious worship. See Prince v. Massachusetts, 321 U.S. 158 (1944) (distinguishing between religious activities and other types of activities).

The Court has also recognized that the government has a legitimate interest in avoiding the appearance of endorsing or promoting religion. See McCollum v. Board of Education, 333 U.S. 203 (1948) (striking down a program that allowed religious instruction in public schools). By excluding the Good News Club from its facilities, Milford Central School is avoiding the appearance of endorsing or promoting the Club's religious activities.

Furthermore, the school's policy is consistent with the principles enunciated in cases such as Zorach v. Clauson, 343 U.S. 306 (1952), which held that the government may accommodate religious practices, but is not required to do so. The policy is also consistent with the Court's decision in Engel v. Vitale, 370 U.S. 421 (1962) (pre-1970), which held that the government may not promote or endorse religious activities.

In conclusion, Milford Central School did not violate the Good News Club's First Amendment free speech rights by excluding it from meeting after hours at the school. The school's community use policy is a reasonable restriction on speech in a limited public forum, and is viewpoint-neutral. The policy is consistent with the principles enunciated in cases such as Everson v. Board of Education, 330 U.S. 1 (1947), and McCollum v. Board of Education, 333 U.S. 203 (1948), and is necessary to maintain the separation of church and state. The judgment of the Court of Appeals should be affirmed.
