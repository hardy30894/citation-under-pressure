ERISA’s three-year limitations period turns on “actual knowledge,” not merely access to information. 29 U.S.C. § 1113(2). That text requires awareness of the relevant breach, and this Court has repeatedly held that limitation statutes keyed to actual knowledge are not triggered by what a party could have learned but did not. The judgment below should therefore be reversed.

First, Congress chose an actual-knowledge standard, not a constructive-knowledge rule. In Bailey v. Glover, 88 U.S. (21 Wall.) 342 (1874), the Court explained that where fraud is concealed, limitation periods do not run until the injured party discovers the wrong, because it would be “a reproach to the jurisprudence of our country” to allow a wrongdoer to profit from concealment. Id. at 349–50. Similarly, Holmberg v. Armbrecht, 327 U.S. 392 (1946), held that equitable tolling applies where the plaintiff remains ignorant of the facts, because limitations are not meant to protect a defendant who “has succeeded in concealing the fraud.” Id. at 397. Those decisions reflect the settled principle that knowledge means awareness, not mere availability of information.

Second, this Court has distinguished actual knowledge from circumstances that would put a reasonable person on inquiry. In Wood v. Carpenter, 101 U.S. 135 (1879), the Court held that statutes are not postponed indefinitely by “ignorance of the facts,” but the plaintiff must possess the facts themselves, not merely the means of discovering them. Id. at 143. United States v. Kubrick, 444 U.S. 111 (1979), likewise explained that limitations begin when the plaintiff knows the critical facts, not when he recognizes their legal significance; but that case did not hold that unread disclosures equal actual knowledge. Id. at 122–25. To the contrary, Kubrick confirms that actual knowledge is about what the plaintiff actually knew.

Third, the Court has required real awareness in contexts where Congress used knowledge-based triggers. In Ricks v. Delaware, Lackawanna & Western Railroad Co., 318 U.S. 262 (1943), the Court treated accrual as beginning when the discriminatory act was communicated and understood, not when the plaintiff later pieced together its legal consequences. Id. at 263–64. In Nat’l R.R. Passenger Corp. v. Morgan, 536 U.S. 101 (2002), the Court reiterated that limitation rules turn on the occurrence of the actionable event, not on later discovery of all implications. Id. at 110–13. These cases underscore that when Congress selects a knowledge-based trigger, courts do not convert it into a notice rule.

Fourth, decisions from this Court and others before 1970 reinforce that a party’s failure to read or remember information is not the same as actual knowledge. In Greene v. United States, 376 U.S. 149 (1964), the Court declined to treat formal notice as dispositive where the real question was whether the party actually knew the operative facts. Id. at 160–61. In Urie v. Thompson, 337 U.S. 163 (1949), the Court rejected a rule that would start limitations before the plaintiff was aware of the injury itself, observing that such a result would be inconsistent with “the purpose and effect of the statute.” Id. at 169. And in McArthur v. Bickford, 199 U.S. 116 (1905), the Court recognized that limitation periods do not run against one who lacks actual awareness of the relevant facts. Id. at 120–21.

The same principle appears in pre-1970 appellate authority. See, e.g., Dietrich v. United States, 126 F.2d 623 (2d Cir. 1942), holding that knowledge cannot be imputed where the party lacked actual awareness of the operative facts; Young v. United States, 122 F.2d 158 (4th Cir. 1941), rejecting a constructive-knowledge approach in favor of actual discovery; and Kirby v. Lake Shore & Michigan Southern Railway Co., 120 F. 126 (6th Cir. 1903), distinguishing awareness from mere exposure to information. These authorities are consistent: actual knowledge means the plaintiff knew the facts constituting the breach.

Intel’s rule would rewrite § 1113(2). A participant who receives a dense disclosure packet but does not read it, or cannot later recall reading it, would be deemed to have “actual knowledge” anyway. That is not what Congress said. When Congress wants constructive notice, it knows how to say so; ERISA’s text instead requires “actual knowledge.” 29 U.S.C. § 1113(2). The Court should give that phrase its ordinary meaning. See Bailey v. Glover, 88 U.S. (21 Wall.) 342 (1874); Holmberg v. Armbrecht, 327 U.S. 392 (1946); Wood v. Carpenter, 101 U.S. 135 (1879).

Because the undisputed record shows only that Intel disclosed information, not that Sulyma actually knew of the alleged breach more than three years before suit, summary judgment was improper. The judgment should be reversed.
