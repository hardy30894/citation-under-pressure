# ARGUMENT

## I. Penry v. Lynaugh Forecloses A Categorical Eighth Amendment Bar On Executing Mentally Retarded Offenders.

This Court squarely addressed and rejected the claim petitioner now renews. In *Penry v. Lynaugh*, 492 U.S. 302 (1989), the Court held that the Eighth Amendment does not categorically prohibit the execution of a mentally retarded capital defendant. The Court reasoned that mental retardation, like youth, is a condition of "diverse capacities and intentions" that bears on culpability but does not, standing alone, remove an offender from the class of persons who may constitutionally be sentenced to death. Instead, *Penry* concluded that the appropriate vehicle for accounting for mental retardation is the individualized sentencing process itself, in which the defendant may offer, and the jury must be permitted to consider, evidence of retardation as a mitigating circumstance. That framework tracks this Court's longstanding requirement, announced in *Lockett v. Ohio*, 438 U.S. 586, 604 (1978) (plurality opinion), that a capital sentencer not "be precluded from considering, as a mitigating factor, any aspect of a defendant's character or record" that the defendant offers as a reason for a sentence less than death.

Virginia's capital sentencing scheme did precisely what *Penry* and *Lockett* require. Petitioner was permitted to present—and did present—expert testimony regarding his intellectual functioning at both sentencing hearings. The jury heard that evidence, weighed it against the State's rebuttal evidence and the aggravating circumstances of an execution-style abduction and murder, and twice concluded that death was the appropriate sentence. Nothing in the Eighth Amendment, as construed in *Penry*, required a different result. Absent a controlling basis to overrule *Penry*—and petitioner has offered none—stare decisis counsels adherence to that precedent.

## II. There Is No National Consensus Sufficient To Establish A New Categorical Rule.

The Eighth Amendment's prohibition on cruel and unusual punishments "must draw its meaning from the evolving standards of decency that mark the progress of a maturing society." *Trop v. Dulles*, 356 U.S. 86, 101 (1958). But this Court has consistently instructed that the existence of such an evolving standard is measured by objective indicia—chiefly the judgments of state legislatures and the sentencing decisions of juries—not by the subjective views of the Justices or of interested professional organizations. *See Coker v. Georgia*, 433 U.S. 584, 592–93 (1977) (looking to "the current judgment," as reflected in "legislative decisions" and "jury determinations"); *Gregg v. Georgia*, 428 U.S. 153, 173–74 (1976) (plurality opinion).

Measured by that objective yardstick, no such consensus exists here. At the time *Penry* was decided, only two States and the federal government barred the execution of the mentally retarded. Although additional States have since acted, a substantial number of death-penalty jurisdictions—including Virginia—continue to permit execution of mentally retarded offenders subject to individualized mitigation review. This pattern is a far cry from the near-uniform legislative abolition this Court has demanded before recognizing a new categorical Eighth Amendment rule. In *Stanford v. Kentucky*, 492 U.S. 361, 373 (1989), the Court declined to find a national consensus against executing sixteen- and seventeen-year-old offenders even though a comparable minority of States prohibited the practice, explaining that "it is not a societal consensus" of that character, but rather one approaching unanimity, that this Court has required. The same principle governs here. A trend among some state legislatures, without more, does not establish the kind of settled societal judgment that would justify displacing the individualized sentencing regime approved in *Penry* with a categorical constitutional rule.

Nor can petitioner substitute the views of amici, professional associations, or foreign practice for the judgments of the People acting through their elected representatives. This Court has repeatedly emphasized that legislative enactments are "the clearest and most reliable objective evidence of contemporary values." *Penry*, 492 U.S. at 331. Where, as here, the legislatures of a substantial number of capital-punishment States have considered and declined to adopt a categorical exemption, this Court should not impose one under the guise of the Eighth Amendment.

## III. Individualized Capital Sentencing Adequately Protects The Interests Petitioner Invokes.

Petitioner's true complaint is not that Virginia's system is incapable of accounting for mental retardation, but that a jury, having heard the evidence, disagreed with his assessment of its mitigating weight. That is not an Eighth Amendment violation; it is the system working as designed. This Court has long recognized that capital sentencing determinations—informed by full presentation of aggravating and mitigating evidence—are peculiarly within the province of the jury, and that deference to those individualized judgments is a cornerstone of a constitutional death-penalty regime. *See McCleskey v. Kemp*, 481 U.S. 279, 311 (1987) (recognizing the substantial discretion afforded capital sentencers to weigh mitigating and aggravating evidence). A rule categorically removing an entire class of offenders from capital sentencing—based not on evidentiary insufficiency in a given case but on a diagnostic label alone—would displace the individualized weighing this Court has consistently required and would treat as legally incompetent a class of citizens whom Virginia's own juries, applying legislatively prescribed procedures, have found fit to stand trial, to be convicted, and to bear moral responsibility for their crimes.

Because Virginia's capital sentencing procedures satisfy *Penry*, and because no national consensus supports displacing that precedent, the judgment of the Supreme Court of Virginia should be affirmed.
