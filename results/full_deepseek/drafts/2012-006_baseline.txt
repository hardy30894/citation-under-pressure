**ARGUMENT**

**WITHDRAWAL FROM A CONSPIRACY BEFORE THE STATUTE OF LIMITATIONS PERIOD DOES NOT RELIEVE THE GOVERNMENT OF ITS BURDEN TO PROVE THE ACCUSED WAS A MEMBER OF THE CONSPIRACY DURING THE LIMITATIONS PERIOD.**

The government bears the burden of proving beyond a reasonable doubt every element of the offense charged, including that the conspiracy existed within the applicable statute of limitations period. *See Grunewald v. United States*, 353 U.S. 391, 396 (1957). This burden requires the government to demonstrate that the defendant was a member of the conspiracy during that period. *United States v. Lash*, 937 F.2d 1077, 1081 (6th Cir. 1991).

Where a defendant presents evidence of withdrawal from the conspiracy prior to the limitations period, the government must prove beyond a reasonable doubt that the defendant remained a member of the conspiracy during the limitations period. *United States v. Read*, 658 F.2d 1225, 1236 (7th Cir. 1981). Withdrawal is an affirmative defense only when the defendant relies on it to defeat liability for acts committed before withdrawal; it does not alter the government's burden to prove membership during the limitations period. *United States v. Sinito*, 723 F.2d 1250, 1262 (6th Cir. 1983).

The government mistakenly contends that the burden shifts to defendants to disprove membership during the limitations period. This contradicts the fundamental principle that the prosecution must prove each element of the offense beyond a reasonable doubt. *In re Winship*, 397 U.S. 358, 364 (1970). The statute of limitations is not an affirmative defense but a limitation on the government's power to prosecute. *United States v. Walsh*, 700 F.2d 846, 855 (2d Cir. 1983).

Here, petitioners presented evidence that the conspiracy's leaders—Rodney Moore and Kevin Gray—split up before the limitations period. This evidence raised a genuine issue regarding the conspiracy's continuity. Once petitioners produced such evidence, the government bore the burden of proving beyond a reasonable doubt that the conspiracy continued into the limitations period and that petitioners were members. *United States v. Sperling*, 179 F.2d 338, 341 (2d Cir. 1950).

The court below erred by suggesting that petitioners bore the burden of persuasion. That approach effectively relieved the government of its constitutional obligation. Accordingly, the judgment should be reversed.
