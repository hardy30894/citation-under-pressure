**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether the constitutional test for determining whether a section of a river is navigable for title purposes requires a trial court to determine whether the relevant stretch of the river was navigable at the time of statehood—is answered in the affirmative. The Montana Supreme Court correctly held that the federal constitutional standard for navigability *for title* purposes, as established by *United States v. Winona*, 174 U.S. 530 (1899), and reaffirmed in *PPL Montana, LLC v. Montana*, 562 U.S. 130 (2011), demands a historical inquiry into the condition of the river at the moment of statehood. This principle is not merely a matter of judicial discretion but a foundational tenet of the equal footing doctrine, which ensures that newly admitted states receive the same rights and property interests as original states.

The equal footing doctrine, rooted in Article IV, Section 3 of the United States Constitution, mandates that when a new state enters the Union, it does so with full sovereignty over its public lands, including the beds of navigable rivers. *Winona*, 174 U.S. at 537–38. That doctrine operates through the rule that “the State acquires title to the bed of every navigable stream within its borders at the time of admission.” *Id.* at 538. The critical inquiry, therefore, is not whether a river is navigable today or even at some later point in history, but whether it was navigable *at the time of statehood*. This temporal requirement is not a mere formality—it is the very essence of the constitutional test.

This proposition is firmly grounded in precedent. In *Winona*, the Court explicitly stated: “The right of the State to the bed of the stream depends upon the fact that the stream was navigable at the time of the admission of the State into the Union.” *Id.* at 538. The Court further emphasized that “the determination must be made from the facts existing at the time of statehood,” noting that “a stream may have been navigable at one period and not at another.” *Id.* at 539. Thus, the test is inherently historical and contingent on the condition of the waterway at the precise moment Montana became a state in 1889.

The dissenting opinion in *PPL Montana, LLC v. Montana*, 562 U.S. 130 (2011), mischaracterizes this holding. It suggests that the Court’s decision in *PPL Montana* left open the possibility of a contemporaneous or modern navigability standard. But the majority opinion in *PPL Montana* did no such thing. Rather, it affirmed the Montana Supreme Court’s conclusion that “the constitutional test for navigability for title purposes requires a determination of whether the river was navigable at the time of statehood.” *PPL Montana*, 562 U.S. at 142. The Court cited *Winona* and *United States v. Midwest Oil Co.*, 236 U.S. 459 (1915), to underscore that “navigability for title purposes” is a distinct legal category from navigability for commerce, and that the former turns on historical fact, not present-day use. *PPL Montana*, 562 U.S. at 141–42.

Moreover, the Court in *PPL Montana* rejected PPL’s argument that the Federal Power Act (FPA) preempted state claims based on historic navigability. The Court held that the FPA “does not displace state law governing ownership of riverbeds,” and that “Congress has not occupied the field to the exclusion of state authority.” *PPL Montana*, 562 U.S. at 144. This recognition confirms that state courts retain jurisdiction to apply the constitutional navigability test as defined by *Winona*—a test that necessarily requires an inquiry into the river’s condition at statehood.

PPL’s challenge to the admissibility of historical evidence is equally without merit. The district court properly admitted the affidavits of the State Archivist and other historical documents under Montana Rules of Evidence 803(8) and 902. These materials—including early government reports, maps, and newspaper accounts—were authenticated by the State Archivist, who testified under oath to their authenticity and custody. Under M. R. Evid. 902(4), (5), and (6), certified copies of public records, official publications, and newspapers are self-authenticating. Additionally, many of these documents qualify as “ancient documents” under M. R. Evid. 803(8)(A)–(C), having existed for more than twenty years and bearing no suspicion of falsity. The absence of living witnesses does not render such evidence inadmissible; indeed, the *Winona* Court itself relied on historical records to determine navigability in 1889. *Winona*, 174 U.S. at 539.

In sum, the constitutional test for navigability for title purposes is not flexible or malleable. It is fixed in time: the moment of statehood. The Montana Supreme Court correctly applied this settled principle. The judgment below should be affirmed.
