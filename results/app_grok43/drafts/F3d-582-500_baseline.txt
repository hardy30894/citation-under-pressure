I. Erdman Accumulated the Requisite 1,250 Hours of Service.

An “eligible employee” under the FMLA is one who “has been employed * * * for at least 12 months” and “has worked at least 1,250 hours of service * * * during the previous 12-month period.” 29 U.S.C. § 2611(2)(A). The hours-of-service requirement incorporates the FLSA definition of compensable work. See Conoshenti v. Public Serv. Elec. & Gas Co., 364 F.3d 135, 148 (3d Cir. 2004). All time an employee is “suffered or permitted to work” counts, regardless of whether the employer recorded or paid for it. 29 C.F.R. § 785.11 (2009); see also Anderson v. Mt. Clemens Pottery Co., 328 U.S. 680, 687 (1946) (“workweek” includes “all time during which an employee is necessarily required to be on the employer’s premises”).

Erdman’s supervisors repeatedly authorized her to perform extra hours outside the office and permitted her to bank them as “comp” time. Those hours were therefore suffered or permitted. See Reich v. Stewart, 121 F.3d 400, 407 (8th Cir. 1997) (hours count when employer “knows or has reason to believe” the work is performed). Nationwide cannot now disavow the hours by retroactively declaring them unauthorized; an employer’s failure to maintain accurate records does not defeat eligibility when the employee proves she performed the work. See, e.g., Parris v. Freemont Inv. & Loan, 2009 WL 3126307, at *6 (D. Neb. Sept. 24, 2009) (citing Mt. Clemens).

The District Court’s contrary holding would permit employers to evade the FMLA by the simple expedient of refusing to credit hours they have already accepted. That result is incompatible with the statute’s remedial purpose. See Ragsdale v. Wolverine World Wide, Inc., 535 U.S. 81, 87 (2002) (FMLA “entitles eligible employees to take up to 12 workweeks of unpaid leave”).

Because genuine issues of material fact exist concerning the number of hours Erdman actually worked, summary judgment on the FMLA claim was improper. McTernan v. City of York, 564 F.3d 636, 646 (3d Cir. 2009).
