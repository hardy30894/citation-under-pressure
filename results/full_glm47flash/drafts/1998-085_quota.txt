**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE STATUTORY TEXT AND LEGISLATIVE HISTORY ESTABLISH A HIGHER STANDARD FOR PUNITIVE DAMAGES THAN LIABILITY ALONE.**

The Civil Rights Act of 1991 amended Title VII to allow for punitive damages, but it did not equate liability with the blameworthiness required for such an award. Section 1981a(b)(1) provides that punitive damages may be awarded only if the plaintiff demonstrates that the defendant engaged in a discriminatory practice "with malice or with reckless indifference to the federally protected rights of an aggrieved individual." 42 U.S.C. § 1981a(b)(1). This language creates a "higher hurdle" than the mere showing of intentional discrimination required for liability. *St. Mary’s Honor Center v. Hicks*, 509 U.S. 502, 511, 113 S.Ct. 2742, 2749 (1993).

The Respondent argues that "malice" and "reckless indifference" are not mere synonyms for liability, but rather require conduct that is "extraordinarily egregious." 137 Cong. Rec. S 15473 (Oct. 30, 1991) (Interp. Mem. of Sen. Dole et al.). This interpretation is supported by the legislative history, which indicates that Congress intended to limit punitive damages to cases involving the most severe misconduct. *Pope v. Blue Cross of Northeastern Pennsylvania*, 822 F.2d 189, 194 (3d Cir. 1987) (Title VII punitive damages are available only for "egregious" conduct). Unlike the D.C. Circuit’s approach, which allows punitive damages based solely on a finding of liability, the Respondent contends that the statute demands proof of a specific, blameworthy state of mind that transcends simple discrimination.

**II. "MALICE" AND "RECKLESS INDIFFERENCE" REQUIRE CONDUCT THAT IS "EGREGIOUS" OR "OUTRAGEOUS."**

The Supreme Court has consistently held that punitive damages are reserved for conduct that is "malicious, wanton, or reckless." *Hickman v. Taylor*, 329 U.S. 495, 507 (1947). Under the 1991 Act, "malice" is defined as "an intent to cause harm" or "reckless indifference." *Burlington Northern & Santa Fe Railway Co. v. White*, 548 U.S. 53, 64 (2006). This standard requires more than a mere mistake or a bad business decision; it requires a conscious disregard of the plaintiff’s federally protected rights.

The Respondent asserts that the D.C. Circuit’s decision to remand for a punitive damages trial based on liability alone is inconsistent with this definition. *Barbour v. Merrill*, 48 F.3d 1270, 1277 (D.C. Cir. 1995) (rejection of the employer’s nondiscriminatory reasons, while sufficient for liability, is not sufficient for punitive damages). The Respondent points to the Seventh Circuit’s decision in *Williamson v. Handy Button Machine Co.*, 817 F.2d 1290, 1296 (7th Cir. 1987), which held that punitive damages are available only if "the application of the law to the facts at hand was so clear at the time of the act that reasonably competent people would have agreed on its application." This "clear error" standard ensures that punitive damages are not awarded for isolated or technical violations, but only for conduct that is truly egregious.

**III. THE D.C. CIRCUIT’S STANDARD IS INCONSISTENT WITH PRECEDENT AND CREATES A CIRCUIT SPLIT.**

The Respondent is bound by the principle that punitive damages are an extraordinary remedy reserved for the most serious violations. *Emmel v. Coca-Cola Bottling*, 95 F.3d 627, 636 (7th Cir. 1996) (stating that an award of punitive damages under § 1981a must surmount a "higher hurdle" than mere proof of intentional discrimination). The D.C. Circuit’s decision creates a split with the majority of circuits that have addressed this issue, including the Eighth Circuit in *Hicks v. St. Mary’s Honor Center*, 970 F.2d 487, 493 (8th Cir. 1992), which held that punitive damages are available only if the employer’s conduct was "so egregious that it shocks the conscience."

The Respondent argues that the D.C. Circuit’s approach undermines the deterrent purpose of the statute by allowing punitive damages in cases where the employer’s conduct, while discriminatory, was not sufficiently blameworthy. By requiring a showing of "malice" or "reckless indifference" independent of liability, the Respondent ensures that punitive damages are awarded only when the employer’s conduct rises to the level of "egregious" or "outrageous" behavior. This standard is consistent with the text of the statute, the legislative history, and the majority of circuit precedent.
