ARGUMENT

Under Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 12(b)(6), a complaint survives dismissal if it states “a claim to relief that is plausible on its face.” Bell Atl. Corp. v. Twombly, 550 U.S. 544, 570 (2007); Ashcroft v. Iqbal, 556 U.S. 662, 678 (2009). This Court reviews dismissal de novo, accepting the well-pleaded facts as true and drawing all reasonable inferences in Foley’s favor. Medina-Velázquez v. Hernández-Gregorat, 767 F.3d 103, 108 (1st Cir. 2014); Watterson v. Page, 987 F.2d 1, 3 (1st Cir. 1993). Measured against that standard, the district court erred in dismissing Foley’s contract and Chapter 93A claims.

I. Foley Adequately Alleged a Breach of Contract Claim Because He Is an Intended Beneficiary of the Settlement Agreement.

Foley is a member of Settlement Class B, and the settlement agreement’s express terms obligate Wells Fargo to consider him “for a HAMP modification” and, failing that, “for a MAP2R modification” following the mandatory “waterfall” process. Foley alleged that Wells Fargo never completed that process—indeed, that it strung him along for over a year, lost his application, and pushed him toward foreclosure alternatives rather than the modification review the agreement guaranteed. Those allegations, taken as true, plausibly state a breach.

Massachusetts law recognizes that a non-signatory may enforce a contract when the language and circumstances “clearly and definitely” show that the contracting parties intended to give him the benefit of the promised performance. Cumis Ins. Soc’y, Inc. v. BJ’s Wholesale Club, Inc., 455 Mass. 458, 466 (2009). The settlement agreement here does exactly that: it identifies discrete classes of Pick-a-Payment borrowers, including Class B, and imposes specific, individually enforceable “servicing commitments”—clear explanations of denials, notice during foreclosure proceedings, and completion of the waterfall analysis—“[i]n order to ensure that Borrowers are appropriately considered for a MAP2R Modification in a timely manner.” Nothing about that language limits enforcement to the class representatives or the settling parties; it is designed, on its face, to run to the individual borrowers who make up the class. The district court’s contrary conclusion improperly resolved a contract-interpretation question against Foley at the pleading stage, when all inferences must run in his favor. Medina-Velázquez, 767 F.3d at 108.

II. The Home Owners’ Loan Act Does Not Preempt Foley’s State-Law Claims.

Wells Fargo’s preemption argument fails because Foley’s claims do not regulate “lending” in the sense HOLA and its implementing regulations address; they merely seek to hold Wells Fargo to a bargain it voluntarily struck to resolve federal Truth-in-Lending Act litigation. HOLA preemption reaches state laws that impose requirements on the “processing, origination, servicing, sale or purchase of ... mortgages,” 12 C.F.R. § 560.2(b), but it does not immunize a lender from its own contractual commitments. See Silvas v. E*Trade Mortg. Corp., 514 F.3d 1001, 1006 (9th Cir. 2008) (distinguishing state laws that regulate lending itself from state-law claims arising from a lender’s independent obligations). The settlement agreement did not arise from any generally applicable state lending regulation; it was privately negotiated by Wells Fargo to settle disclosure claims under a federal statute. Enforcing that private bargain does not “regulate” mortgage lending any more than enforcing any other commercial contract a federally chartered bank chooses to sign. Because the obligations Foley seeks to enforce are of Wells Fargo’s own making, not creatures of Massachusetts lending law, preemption does not apply, and the same reasoning defeats Wells Fargo’s preemption argument against the Chapter 93A claim, which is likewise grounded in the bank’s failure to honor the settlement’s servicing commitments rather than in any state-imposed lending requirement.

III. Foley Stated a Claim Under Chapter 93A for Unfair and Deceptive Conduct.

Chapter 93A prohibits “unfair or deceptive acts or practices in the conduct of any trade or commerce,” Mass. Gen. Laws ch. 93A, § 2(a), and Massachusetts courts have long held that a breach of contract, when accompanied by conduct that is unscrupulous or exceeds the bounds of acceptable commercial behavior, may violate the statute. Rae v. Air-Speed, Inc., 386 Mass. 187, 194 (1982) (the conduct “must attain a level of rascality that would raise an eyebrow of someone inured to the rough and tumble of the world of commerce”). Foley’s complaint alleges precisely that kind of rascality: Wells Fargo repeatedly promised him a HAMP application, delayed seven months in sending it, then claimed never to have received his completed submission; its designated “Home Preservation Specialists” ignored his calls; and rather than process the modification review the settlement required, the bank pressed him toward a short sale or deed in lieu of foreclosure—outcomes that would strip him of his home rather than preserve it, all while it moved forward with foreclosure. These are not merely technical contract breaches; they are the kind of dilatory, misleading loan-servicing practices that Massachusetts courts and this Court have recognized as actionable unfair practices in the mortgage-servicing context. At the pleading stage, these allegations plausibly establish both a breach of the settlement’s servicing commitments and a pattern of unfair dealing sufficient to sustain a Chapter 93A claim.

CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the district court dismissing Foley’s breach of contract and Chapter 93A claims should be reversed, and the case remanded for further proceedings.
