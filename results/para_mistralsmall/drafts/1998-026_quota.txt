**ARGUMENT**

The District Court correctly concluded that Petitioner is not entitled to habeas relief because the court’s failure to advise him of his right to appeal under Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 32(a)(2) was harmless. The Supreme Court has long held that a Rule violation does not warrant collateral relief where the defendant’s substantial rights were not affected. *United States v. Timmreck*, 441 U.S. 780, 784 (1979) (“[A] collateral attack on a final judgment in a criminal case does not lie where the alleged error did not affect the substantial rights of the defendant.”). Here, Petitioner concedes—and the District Court found—that he was aware of his right to appeal at the time of sentencing. Thus, the Rule 32(a)(2) error caused no prejudice and is subject to harmless-error review. *See United States v. Vonn*, 535 U.S. 55, 62–63 (2002) (holding that Rule 11 violations are subject to harmless-error analysis where the defendant’s substantial rights were not affected).

The Third Circuit properly applied harmless-error review in affirming the District Court’s denial of habeas relief. *See United States v. Olano*, 507 U.S. 725, 734 (1993) (“[A]n error must be more than ‘silly’ or ‘harmless’ to justify reversal.”). The error here was harmless because Petitioner’s awareness of his right to appeal rendered the Rule 32(a)(2) violation inconsequential. *See Neder v. United States*, 527 U.S. 1, 9–10 (1999) (holding that a jury instruction error was harmless where the omitted element was undisputed and the jury necessarily found it). Similarly, the District Court’s failure to advise Petitioner of his right to appeal did not affect the fairness or reliability of his sentence. *See United States v. Vontsteen*, 950 F.2d 1086, 1090 (5th Cir. 1992) (en banc) (holding that a Rule 32(a)(2) violation is harmless where the defendant was aware of his right to appeal).

The purpose of Rule 32(a)(2) is to ensure that defendants are informed of their right to appeal. *See United States v. Smith*, 601 F.2d 108, 111 (5th Cir. 1979) (explaining that Rule 32(a)(2) is designed to apprise defendants of their appellate rights). Where, as here, the defendant was already aware of that right, the Rule’s purpose was satisfied. *See United States v. Dominguez Benitez*, 542 U.S. 74, 83 (2004) (holding that a Rule 11 error is harmless where the defendant’s substantial rights were not affected). The Third Circuit correctly concluded that the District Court’s error did not prejudice Petitioner and thus did not warrant habeas relief.

Moreover, the Supreme Court has repeatedly emphasized that collateral review is not a substitute for direct appeal. *See Stone v. Powell*, 428 U.S. 465, 494 n.31 (1976) (“[C]ollateral review is not intended to create a super-appeal.”). Petitioner’s claim is precisely the type of issue that should have been raised on direct appeal. *See United States v. Frady*, 456 U.S. 152, 167 (1982) (holding that a defendant must raise a claim on direct appeal or risk forfeiting it). Here, Petitioner waited four years to raise his Rule 32(a)(2) claim, further underscoring that the error was harmless and did not prejudice him.

Finally, the District Court’s finding that Petitioner was aware of his right to appeal is entitled to deference under the Antiterrorism and Effective Death Penalty Act (AEDPA). *See 28 U.S.C. § 2254(d)(2)* (providing that a state court’s factual findings are entitled to deference unless they are unreasonable). The Third Circuit correctly deferred to the District Court’s factual finding and concluded that the error was harmless. *See Wright v. West*, 505 U.S. 277, 296 (1992) (holding that federal courts must defer to state court factual findings under AEDPA).

In sum, the District Court’s failure to advise Petitioner of his right to appeal under Rule 32(a)(2) was harmless because Petitioner was aware of that right. The Third Circuit properly applied harmless-error review and correctly concluded that Petitioner is not entitled to habeas relief.
