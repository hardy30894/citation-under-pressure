**ARGUMENT**

**I. The District Court Did Not Abuse Its Discretion in Enhancing the Lodestar Based on Exceptional Performance and Results.**

The Eleventh Circuit correctly affirmed the district court’s enhancement of the lodestar figure, as the Supreme Court has repeatedly recognized that such enhancements are permissible in “rare” and “exceptional” cases where the record supports a finding of superior performance and exceptional results. *See Blum v. Stenson*, 465 U.S. 886, 899 (1984) (“[A]n upward adjustment may be justified in the rare case where the fee applicant offers specific evidence to show that the quality of service rendered was superior to that one reasonably should expect in light of the hourly rates charged”); *Pennsylvania v. Del. Valley Citizens’ Council for Clean Air*, 478 U.S. 546, 565 (1986) (plurality opinion) (“upward adjustments of the lodestar figure are still permissible ... in certain ‘rare’ and ‘exceptional’ cases, supported by both ‘specific evidence’ on the record and detailed findings by the lower courts”).

The district court’s findings here were more than sufficient to justify the enhancement. It expressly found that plaintiffs’ counsel provided “stellar performance throughout this long and difficult case,” including “substantial experience and familiarity with the prevailing rates in Atlanta,” and that the results obtained were “exceptional.” *Kenny A. ex rel. Winn v. Perdue*, 454 F.Supp.2d 1260, 1286, 1290 (N.D. Ga. 2006). Such detailed, case-specific findings are precisely what the Supreme Court required in *Delaware Valley I* and *Blum*. *See Del. Valley I*, 478 U.S. at 565; *Blum*, 465 U.S. at 899. The Eleventh Circuit correctly deferred to these findings, as they were supported by the record and not clearly erroneous. *See Kenny A. v. Perdue*, 532 F.3d 1209, 1242 (11th Cir. 2008).

**II. The Supreme Court Has Rejected the Notion That Enhancements Are Categorically Prohibited.**

Petitioners’ argument that enhancements are never permissible rests on a misreading of *Delaware Valley I* and *Blum*. In *Delaware Valley I*, the Supreme Court declined to overrule *Blum* and instead reaffirmed that enhancements may be appropriate in “rare” cases. 478 U.S. at 564-68. The Court explicitly stated that the lodestar “is presumed to be the reasonable fee,” but left open the possibility of an upward adjustment where the record supports it. *Id.* at 564-65. *Blum* likewise did not foreclose enhancements; it merely held that the applicants had failed to offer specific evidence of superior performance or exceptional results. 465 U.S. at 899. Here, by contrast, the district court made detailed findings supported by the record, and the Eleventh Circuit properly upheld them.

Petitioners’ reliance on *City of Burlington v. Dague*, 505 U.S. 557 (1992), is misplaced. *Dague* addressed enhancements for contingency, not performance or results. *Id.* at 562-63. The Supreme Court has never held that enhancements for superior performance or exceptional results are categorically prohibited. To the contrary, *Delaware Valley I* and *Blum* expressly permit them in appropriate cases.

**III. The Eleventh Circuit Correctly Followed Binding Precedent.**

The Eleventh Circuit’s decision is consistent with every other circuit to have considered this issue. *See, e.g., Geier v. Sundquist*, 372 F.3d 784, 794-95 (6th Cir. 2004) (“Delaware Valley I permits enhancements based on quality of representation and results obtained in rare and exceptional cases”); *Van Gerwen v. Guarantee Mut. Life Co.*, 214 F.3d 1041, 1046 (9th Cir. 2000) (enhancement justified where “quality of service was superior in light of the hourly rates charged and that the success was exceptional”); *Quaratino v. Tiffany & Co.*, 166 F.3d 422, 425 (2d Cir. 1999) (“The lodestar may be adjusted based on several factors, including in particular the results obtained”). Petitioners’ suggestion of a circuit split is unsupported.

**IV. The District Court’s Findings Were Sufficiently Specific and Supported by the Record.**

Petitioners argue that the district court’s findings were insufficient, but this mischaracterizes the record. The district court did not rely on “testimony” or unspecified comparisons; it made detailed findings based on its own observations of counsel’s performance and the results achieved. *Kenny A.*, 454 F.Supp.2d at 1286, 1290. Such findings are entitled to deference, and the Eleventh Circuit properly upheld them. *See Kenny A.*, 532 F.3d at 1242.

**CONCLUSION**

The district court did not abuse its discretion in enhancing the lodestar based on exceptional performance and results. The Eleventh Circuit correctly affirmed its decision, and this Court should do the same.
