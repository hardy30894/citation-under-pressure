ARGUMENT

The National Park Service possesses authority to regulate activities on navigable waters within the boundaries of units of the National Park System. Congress holds plenary power over the public lands, derived from the Property Clause. Gibson v. Chouteau, 80 U.S. 92, 99 (1872). That authority extends to the adoption of regulations necessary for the protection of federal reservations. United States v. Grimaud, 220 U.S. 506, 516 (1911). Regulations promulgated under this power “have the force of law.” Id. at 521.

Navigable waters within federal reservations remain subject to federal control even when title to the bed has passed to a State. The Daniel Ball, 77 U.S. 557, 563 (1870). The United States may exercise its dominant navigational servitude to protect the waters for public purposes. United States v. Chandler-Dunbar Water Power Co., 229 U.S. 53, 62 (1913). This servitude permits the imposition of reasonable restrictions on the use of such waters. Id. at 66.

The establishment of a national preserve constitutes a valid exercise of congressional authority to reserve lands and waters for specific public uses. Light v. United States, 220 U.S. 523, 536 (1911). Once reserved, the lands and waters are withdrawn from entry or appropriation under state law to the extent necessary to accomplish the federal purpose. United States v. Midwest Oil Co., 236 U.S. 459, 474 (1915). Federal regulations governing conduct within the reservation therefore apply uniformly to all persons present there. Camfield v. United States, 167 U.S. 518, 525 (1897).

States cannot interpose their own laws to defeat federal regulation of navigable waters inside federal boundaries. The State’s title to submerged lands is held subject to the paramount authority of the United States. Shively v. Bowlby, 152 U.S. 1, 48 (1894). That authority includes the power to prohibit particular modes of navigation that threaten the values the reservation was created to preserve. Union Bridge Co. v. United States, 204 U.S. 364, 400 (1907).

The hovercraft prohibition is a reasonable exercise of this regulatory power. It applies to all persons operating within the preserve and advances the statutory mandate to conserve wildlife and natural resources. The regulation therefore binds operators on state-owned waters lying inside the preserve boundaries. See Ashwander v. Tennessee Valley Authority, 297 U.S. 288, 338 (1936) (recognizing federal power to control navigable streams for conservation purposes).

Because the challenged regulation rests on longstanding federal authority over public lands and navigable waters, ANILCA does not divest the Service of its power to enforce the hovercraft ban within the Yukon-Charley Rivers National Preserve.
