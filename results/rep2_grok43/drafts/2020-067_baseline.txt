ARGUMENT

A federal court of appeals conducting plain-error review under Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 52(b) may consult the entire record to determine whether an unpreserved error affected substantial rights or the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of the proceedings.

Rule 52(b) authorizes correction of an error that is plain and affects substantial rights. United States v. Olano, 507 U.S. 725, 732 (1993). The substantial-rights inquiry asks whether the error was “prejudicial,” meaning it “affected the outcome of the district court proceedings.” Id. at 734. Because that inquiry is outcome-oriented, the reviewing court must examine “the record” as a whole rather than a truncated portion of it. Id. at 736.

This Court has repeatedly confirmed that the relevant record includes evidence not presented to the jury. In Johnson v. United States, 520 U.S. 461 (1997), the Court looked to the entire trial transcript and the defendant’s own admissions to conclude that an unobjected-to omission of an element did not affect substantial rights. Id. at 469-70. Likewise, in United States v. Vonn, 535 U.S. 55 (2002), the Court held that, when assessing the effect of a Rule 11 error, “the record” encompasses “the whole record,” including materials outside the plea colloquy itself. Id. at 59, 74-76. The same principle governs Rehaif errors. See Henderson v. United States, 568 U.S. 266, 276 (2013) (plain-error analysis “is not a hypothetical inquiry” limited to the evidence the jury heard).

The fairness, integrity, and public-reputation prong likewise permits consideration of the full record. Olano, 507 U.S. at 736. An appellate court cannot sensibly decide whether an error “seriously affect[ed] the fairness, integrity or public reputation of judicial proceedings” without knowing what the record actually shows about the defendant’s knowledge. Id. (internal quotation marks omitted). Limiting review to the trial evidence would produce the anomalous result that a defendant with an extensive criminal history could obtain reversal even though the record conclusively establishes the omitted element.

The Eleventh Circuit therefore acted well within established precedent when it examined Greer’s prior convictions and the remainder of the record to conclude that the Rehaif error neither affected his substantial rights nor seriously undermined the fairness or integrity of the proceedings.
