**ARGUMENT**

I. THE EQUAL PROTECTION CLAUSE RECOGNIZES A "CLASS OF ONE" CLAIM WHERE STATE ACTION IS MOTIVATED BY ILLEGITIMATE, ARBITRARY, OR SPITEFUL REASONS.

The Seventh Circuit correctly held that a plaintiff may bring an equal protection claim as a "class of one" when state action is taken for reasons wholly unrelated to any legitimate governmental objective. *Village of Willowbrook v. Olech*, 160 F.3d 386, 387 (7th Cir. 1998) ("A plaintiff can allege an equal protection violation by asserting that state action was motivated solely by a 'spiteful effort to "get" him for reasons wholly unrelated to any legitimate state objective.'") (quoting *Yick Wo v. Hopkins*, 118 U.S. 356, 373–74 (1886)). This Court has long recognized that the Equal Protection Clause prohibits arbitrary and irrational government action, regardless of whether the plaintiff belongs to a suspect class. *See Romer v. Evans*, 517 U.S. 620, 633 (1996) ("The Constitution’s guarantee of equal protection is not confined to discrimination between individuals and groups of different races, sexes, or alienage."); *City of Cleburne v. Cleburne Living Ctr.*, 473 U.S. 432, 439 (1985) ("The Equal Protection Clause is not offended by a law that is rational and non-invidious.").

The Seventh Circuit’s reasoning aligns with this Court’s precedents, which condemn government action taken for purely personal or spiteful motives. *See Yick Wo*, 118 U.S. at 373–74 (invalidating a facially neutral ordinance enforced discriminatorily against Chinese laundry operators); *see also Allegheny Pittsburgh Coal Co. v. Cty. Comm’n of Webster Cty.*, 488 U.S. 336, 344 (1989) (striking down a tax assessment scheme that arbitrarily and irrationally discriminated against a single property owner). The Court has repeatedly emphasized that the Equal Protection Clause "protects the individual against arbitrary action of government," *Village of Arlington Heights v. Metro. Hous. Dev. Corp.*, 429 U.S. 252, 262 (1977), and the Seventh Circuit’s "class of one" theory is a logical extension of this principle.

II. THE VILLAGE’S CONDITIONAL DEMAND FOR AN 18-FOOT EASEMENT WAS NOT RATIONALLY RELATED TO ANY LEGITIMATE GOVERNMENTAL OBJECTIVE.

The Village’s demand for an 18-foot easement—nearly double the 15-foot requirement imposed on similarly situated property owners—lacks any rational basis. This Court has made clear that a classification lacking any rational relationship to a legitimate state interest violates the Equal Protection Clause. *See FCC v. Beach Commc’ns, Inc.*, 508 U.S. 307, 313 (1993) ("[T]he Equal Protection Clause allows [legislatures] to differentiate among persons or entities when they do so in a rational manner."); *United States R.R. Ret. Bd. v. Fritz*, 449 U.S. 166, 174 (1980) ("[T]he Equal Protection Clause is satisfied so long as there is a plausible policy reason for the classification.").

Here, the Village has offered no plausible justification for treating Olech differently from other property owners. The Seventh Circuit correctly observed that the Village’s demand was "motivated solely by a spiteful effort to 'get' [Olech] for reasons wholly unrelated to any legitimate state objective." *Olech*, 160 F.3d at 387. Such arbitrary and irrational action falls squarely within the ambit of the Equal Protection Clause. *See Romer*, 517 U.S. at 632–33 (invalidating a law motivated by "animus" toward a politically unpopular group); *Yick Wo*, 118 U.S. at 373–74 (striking down a law enforced discriminatorily against a disfavored group).

III. THE SEVENTH CIRCUIT’S DECISION DOES NOT CONFLICT WITH THIS COURT’S PRECEDENTS.

The Village erroneously contends that the Seventh Circuit’s decision conflicts with this Court’s precedents. To the contrary, the Seventh Circuit’s "class of one" theory is consistent with this Court’s recognition that the Equal Protection Clause prohibits arbitrary and irrational government action, regardless of whether the plaintiff belongs to a suspect class. *See Cleburne*, 473 U.S. at 439; *Romer*, 517 U.S. at 633. The Village’s reliance on *Washington v. Davis*, 426 U.S. 229 (1976), is misplaced, as that case addressed discriminatory intent in the context of a facially neutral law, not the arbitrary treatment of a single individual. *See id.* at 240 ("Our cases have not embraced the proposition that a law or other official act, without regard to whether it reflects a racially discriminatory purpose, is unconstitutional solely because it has a racially disproportionate impact."). The Village’s demand for an 18-foot easement, imposed solely to burden Olech, is not merely disproportionate—it is arbitrary and irrational.

**CONCLUSION**

The Seventh Circuit correctly held that the Equal Protection Clause recognizes a "class of one" claim where state action is motivated by illegitimate, arbitrary, or spiteful reasons. The Village’s conditional demand for an 18-foot easement lacks any rational basis and violates the Equal Protection Clause. The petition for certiorari should be denied.
