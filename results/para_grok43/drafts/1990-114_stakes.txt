**ARGUMENT**

**I. The ADEA Excludes Appointed State Judges.**

The Age Discrimination in Employment Act defines “employee” to exclude “any person elected to public office in any State or political subdivision of any State by the qualified voters thereof, or any person chosen by such officer to be on such officer’s personal staff, or an appointee on the policymaking level or an immediate advisor with respect to the exercise of the constitutional or legal powers of the office.” 29 U.S.C. § 630(f). Missouri judges appointed under the Non-Partisan Court Plan and retained by election perform core policymaking functions. “Policymaking is indisputably a part of the function of judging to the extent that judging involves lawmaking to fill the interstices of authority found in constitutions, statutes, and precedents.” EEOC v. Massachusetts, 858 F.2d 52, 55 (1st Cir. 1988) (quoting 680 F.Supp. 455, 462 (D. Mass. 1988)). Missouri Supreme Court decisions establish common law for the entire state, Gustafson v. Benda, 661 S.W.2d 11 (Mo. 1983) (en banc), and resolve questions with far-reaching public-policy implications, Cruzan v. Harmon, 760 S.W.2d 408 (Mo. 1983) (en banc), cert. granted, 492 U.S. 917 (1989). Administrative supervision of the judicial system likewise constitutes policymaking. Mo. Const. art. V, § 4.

Because Congress did not “explicitly and unequivocally manifest[] its intent to preempt state law” in this traditionally state-regulated domain, United States v. Bass, 404 U.S. 336, 349 (1971), the statutory exception applies. The judges therefore are not “employees” protected by the ADEA.

**II. The Mandatory Retirement Provision Satisfies Rational-Basis Review.**

Article V, § 26(1) of the Missouri Constitution draws an age classification that neither burdens a fundamental right nor targets a suspect class. Massachusetts Bd. of Retirement v. Murgia, 427 U.S. 307, 313 (1976) (per curiam). It therefore “carries with it a presumption of rationality that can only be overcome by a clear showing of arbitrariness and irrationality.” Hodel v. Indiana, 452 U.S. 314, 331–32 (1981). Missouri’s rule “eliminates the unpleasantness of selectively removing aged . . . judges,” Malmed v. Thornburgh, 621 F.2d 565, 572 (3d Cir.), cert. denied, 449 U.S. 955 (1980), “reduces delays in the administration of justice caused by death or disabling illness of sitting judges,” Trafelet v. Thompson, 594 F.2d 623, 629 (7th Cir.), cert. denied, 444 U.S. 906 (1979), and “makes it possible to increase the proportion of minority group members and women in the judiciary,” Apkin v. Treasurer & Receiver Gen., 401 Mass. 427, 436, 517 N.E.2d 141, 146 (1988). These considerations supply a rational basis. Vance v. Bradley, 440 U.S. 93, 97 (1979). The classification therefore comports with the Equal Protection Clause.

The judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
