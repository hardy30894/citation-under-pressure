## Argument

The equal footing doctrine provides that Montana acquired title to the beds of navigable waterways upon its admission to the Union in 1889. *Pollard’s Lessee v. Hagan*, 44 U.S. 212, 224 (1845). The controlling question is whether a particular stretch of river was navigable in its natural state at the time of statehood. *United States v. Utah*, 283 U.S. 64, 75 (1931). This Court has consistently held that navigability for title purposes must be assessed as of the moment of statehood, without regard to subsequent improvements or changes. *Oklahoma v. Texas*, 258 U.S. 574, 591 (1922); *Brewer-Elliott Oil & Gas Co. v. United States*, 260 U.S. 77, 87 (1922).

The test for navigability is whether the river, in its ordinary natural condition, was “used, or is susceptible of being used, as a highway for commerce, over which trade and travel are or may be conducted in the customary modes.” *The Daniel Ball*, 77 U.S. 557, 563 (1870); *United States v. Holt State Bank*, 270 U.S. 49, 56 (1926). This definition governs title determinations under the equal footing doctrine. *The Montello*, 87 U.S. 430, 439 (1874). The susceptibility to use for commerce, not actual past use, is the key inquiry. *United States v. Utah*, 283 U.S. at 76.

The trial court correctly applied these principles. It examined historical evidence—including government surveys, contemporary newspaper accounts, and accounts of early explorers—to determine whether the Missouri, Madison, and Clark Fork Rivers were navigable in their natural state at statehood. The court found that each of these rivers had been used for commercial transportation by steamboat and other vessels before and at the time of Montana’s admission. These factual findings were based on admissible evidence and are owed deference.

PPL’s argument that the trial court must conduct a stretch-by-stretch analysis with modern precision contravenes settled law. This Court has long recognized that navigability need not be continuous throughout a river’s entire length; it is sufficient if a particular reach is navigable in fact. *The Montello*, 87 U.S. at 441; *Oklahoma v. Texas*, 258 U.S. at 588. The trial court evaluated each relevant stretch separately, relying on evidence of actual commercial use at the time of statehood. That approach satisfies the constitutional test.

Moreover, the equal footing doctrine vests title to the beds of navigable waters in the State upon admission, regardless of whether the State had previously asserted ownership. *Shively v. Bowlby*, 152 U.S. 1, 47 (1894). Montana’s claim to the riverbeds of these three rivers arose automatically in 1889; no subsequent legislation was required to perfect that title. *Packer v. Bird*, 137 U.S. 661, 669 (1891).

PPL’s reliance on later changes to the rivers—such as dam construction in the early twentieth century—is irrelevant. Navigability for title is fixed at statehood. *United States v. Utah*, 283 U.S. at 87; *Holt State Bank*, 270 U.S. at 56. Improvements that later enhance navigability cannot defeat the State’s title. *McGillvra v. Ross*, 215 U.S. 70, 79 (1909). Conversely, the fact that portions of a river may have been non-navigable in 1889 does not deprive the State of title to those specific segments if the State did not claim them. Here, the trial court properly limited its navigability findings to the stretches actually underlying PPL’s dams—the very segments at issue.

The Montana Supreme Court’s decision thus faithfully applied this Court’s precedents. The constitutional test for navigability for title requires a determination of whether the relevant stretch of river was navigable in its natural state at the time of statehood. The trial court made that determination based on competent historical evidence, and the Montana Supreme Court affirmed. That judgment should stand.
