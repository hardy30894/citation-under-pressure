**Argument**

**I. The Supreme Court’s decision in *Johnson* announced a watershed rule of criminal procedure that implicates the fundamental fairness of the criminal proceeding.**

*Schriro v. Landrigan*, 550 U.S. 465, 473 (2007) (holding that a "watershed" rule is one that "implicates the fundamental fairness and accuracy of the criminal proceeding"). In *Johnson*, the Supreme Court held that the residual clause of the Armed Career Criminal Act, which defined a "violent felony" as an offense that "otherwise involves conduct that presents a serious potential risk of physical injury to another," was unconstitutionally vague. 130 S. Ct. 1265, 1271 (2010). This decision struck down a provision that allowed courts to categorize crimes as "violent felonies" based on a "hypothetical risk posed by an abstract generic version" of the crime. *Id.* at 1271. The *Johnson* decision is a watershed rule because it fundamentally alters the definition of a "violent felony" and ensures that a defendant is not sentenced to a mandatory minimum based on a conviction for a crime that does not actually involve the use of physical force. By invalidating the residual clause, the Court prevented the government from relying on a "garden-variety" criminal statute to trigger a fifteen-year mandatory minimum sentence. *Id.* at 1271-72. This protection of the defendant's right to a clear, non-vague statute is essential to the integrity of the judicial process.

**II. The *Teague* framework does not bar retroactive application of *Johnson* because it falls within the watershed exception.**

*Teague v. Lane*, 489 U.S. 288, 310 (1989) (recognizing an exception to the general rule against retroactivity for "watershed" rules of criminal procedure). Under *Teague*, new rules of criminal procedure generally do not apply retroactively to cases on collateral review. However, the Court has recognized an exception for "watershed" rules. *Id.* at 310. The *Johnson* decision is a watershed rule because it implicates the fundamental fairness of the sentencing phase of a criminal prosecution. *Schriro*, 550 U.S. at 473. The *Johnson* decision ensures that a defendant is not subjected to a mandatory minimum sentence based on a conviction for a crime that, under the new standard, does not actually involve the use of physical force. This protection is critical to the integrity of the judicial process and the defendant's right to a fair sentencing determination. Therefore, the rule announced in *Johnson* must apply retroactively to Welch's sentence.
