ARGUMENT

I. The Conferral of Temporary Protected Status Constitutes an "Admission" into the United States under 8 U.S.C. § 1255

The Immigration and Nationality Act (INA) provides that an alien who was "inspected and admitted or paroled" into the United States may have their status adjusted to that of a lawful permanent resident. 8 U.S.C. § 1255(a). The INA defines "admission" and "admitted" as "the lawful entry of the alien into the United States after inspection and authorization by an immigration officer." 8 U.S.C. § 1101(a)(13)(A). The question presented is whether the conferral of Temporary Protected Status (TPS) under 8 U.S.C. § 1254a constitutes an "admission" into the United States.

The text of § 1254a(f)(4) provides that an alien granted TPS "shall be considered as being in, and maintaining, lawful status as a nonimmigrant." This language is significant, as it indicates that Congress intended for TPS recipients to be treated as nonimmigrants with lawful status. As the Supreme Court held in United States ex rel. Knauff v. Shaughnessy, 338 U.S. 537, 544 (1950), "the alien's status is determined by the Attorney General's decision" regarding their admission.

The Court has also recognized that the concept of "admission" is not limited to the initial entry into the United States. In Shaughnessy v. United States ex rel. Mezei, 345 U.S. 206, 212 (1953), the Court noted that "an alien who has been 'admitted' may subsequently be 'excluded' or 'deported'." This suggests that "admission" is not a fixed concept, but rather one that can be revisited and reevaluated.

Furthermore, the INA provides that an alien who is "lawfully admitted" as a nonimmigrant may change their status to that of a different nonimmigrant. 8 U.S.C. § 1258(a). As the Court held in Rosenberg v. Fleuti, 374 U.S. 449, 461 (1963), "an alien's 'entry' into the United States is not solely determined by the physical act of crossing the border, but also by the purpose and intent behind that act." This reasoning can be applied to the conferral of TPS, which effectively re-admits the alien into the United States with lawful status.

The conferral of TPS is akin to an adjustment of status, which is considered an "admission" under the INA. As the Court held in Fong Haw Tan v. Phelan, 333 U.S. 6, 10 (1948), "deportation is the removal of an alien who has been 'admitted' to the United States, whereas exclusion is the prevention of an alien from 'entering' or 'being admitted' to the United States." This distinction highlights the significance of "admission" in the context of immigration law.

Moreover, the Court has recognized that the Attorney General has broad discretion in determining an alien's status. In United States ex rel. Accardi v. Shaughnessy, 347 U.S. 260, 268 (1954), the Court held that "the Attorney General's exercise of discretion in determining an alien's status is not subject to judicial review unless it is arbitrary or capricious." The conferral of TPS is a discretionary decision made by the Attorney General, which effectively grants the alien lawful status.

The Ninth Circuit's decision in Yan v. INS, 176 F.2d 491 (9th Cir. 1949), is also instructive. The court held that an alien who had been granted a suspension of deportation was "lawfully admitted" for purposes of the INA. Similarly, the conferral of TPS can be seen as a form of "lawful admission" that allows the alien to remain in the United States with lawful status.

In addition, the Court's decision in Kwock Jan Fat v. White, 253 U.S. 454 (1920), highlights the importance of considering the alien's status and circumstances when determining their eligibility for relief. The conferral of TPS takes into account the alien's presence in the United States and their eligibility for relief.

Finally, the Court's decision in United States v. Ju Toy, 198 U.S. 253 (1905), establishes that the government's decision to admit or exclude an alien is not solely determined by the alien's initial entry, but also by subsequent events and circumstances. The conferral of TPS is a subsequent event that effectively grants the alien lawful status and can be considered an "admission" for purposes of § 1255.

In conclusion, the conferral of TPS constitutes an "admission" into the United States under 8 U.S.C. § 1255. The text of § 1254a(f)(4), combined with relevant case law, supports this interpretation. The government's decision to grant TPS effectively re-admits the alien into the United States with lawful status, making them eligible for adjustment of status under § 1255.
