ARGUMENT

The U.S. Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit lacks jurisdiction to review the Merit Systems Protection Board's dismissal of Anthony Perry's mixed case for lack of jurisdiction. Instead, judicial review of the Board's decision lies in a federal district court.

The Civil Service Reform Act of 1978, 5 U.S.C. § 1101 et seq., governs judicial review of MSPB decisions. As a general matter, review of Board decisions lies in the Federal Circuit. 5 U.S.C. § 7703(b)(1)(A). However, an exception to that rule applies to "[c]ases of discrimination," as to which review lies in federal district court. Id. § 7703(b)(2). The statute defines those cases as ones in which an employee "(A) has been affected by an action which [she] may appeal to the Merit Systems Protection Board, and (B) alleges that a basis for the action was discrimination prohibited by" enumerated federal laws. Id. § 7702(a)(1).

In Kloeckner v. Solis, 568 U.S. 41 (2012), the Supreme Court held that when the Board dismisses a mixed-case appeal on procedural grounds, judicial review resides in district court, not the Federal Circuit. The Court's decision in Kloeckner effectively overruled the D.C. Circuit's decision in Powell v. Department of Defense, 158 F.3d 597 (D.C. Cir. 1998), to the extent that Powell drew a distinction between procedural and jurisdictional dismissals.

The Kloeckner Court's analysis focused on the statutory language defining mixed cases, which requires that the employee have "been affected by an action which [she] may appeal to the" MSPB. 5 U.S.C. § 7702(a)(1)(A). The Court interpreted this language to mean that the action itself must be appealable to the Board, not that the employee's appeal must be successful. Kloeckner, 133 S.Ct. at 604. This interpretation applies equally to jurisdictional dismissals, where the Board determines that the action is not appealable to it.

The Federal Circuit's decision in Conforto v. Merit Systems Protection Board, 713 F.3d 1111 (Fed. Cir. 2013), attempted to distinguish between procedural and jurisdictional dismissals. However, the reasoning in Kloeckner undermines this distinction. The Supreme Court's awareness of the potential distinction between procedural and jurisdictional dismissals, as evidenced by the Justices' questions during oral argument, suggests that the Court deliberately chose not to draw such a distinction.

Furthermore, the statute's language and structure support the conclusion that judicial review of jurisdictional dismissals lies in district court. The same deadlines and uncertainty concerns that Perry raised apply to both procedural and jurisdictional dismissals. See, e.g., 5 U.S.C. § 7702(a)(2), (e)(1)(B), (e)(2). It would be inconsistent with the statutory scheme to have different forums for judicial review based on the Board's eventual decision about its jurisdiction.

In light of Kloeckner, the D.C. Circuit's decision in Powell is no longer good law. The Federal Circuit lacks jurisdiction to review the Board's dismissal of Perry's mixed case for lack of jurisdiction. Instead, judicial review lies in a federal district court. This Court should reverse the D.C. Circuit's decision transferring Perry's case to the Federal Circuit and hold that the proper forum for judicial review is a federal district court.

See Kloeckner v. Solis, 568 U.S. 41 (2012); Conforto v. Merit Systems Protection Board, 713 F.3d 1111 (Fed. Cir. 2013); Powell v. Department of Defense, 158 F.3d 597 (D.C. Cir. 1998).
