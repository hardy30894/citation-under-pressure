# ARGUMENT

## A CLAIM FOR NOMINAL DAMAGES CANNOT, BY ITSELF, KEEP AN OTHERWISE MOOT CASE ALIVE

Article III confines federal courts to deciding "Cases" and "Controversies." *Spokeo, Inc. v. Robins*, 578 U.S. 330, 337 (2016). This requirement "subsists through all stages of federal judicial proceedings," and a case becomes moot when it "no longer present[s] a case or controversy." *Genesis Healthcare Corp. v. Symczyk*, 569 U.S. 66, 71 (2013) (quoting *Already, LLC v. Nike, Inc.*, 568 U.S. 85, 90–91 (2013)). Petitioners' claims for declaratory and injunctive relief became moot when GGC revised the challenged policies and Uzuegbunam graduated—a conclusion Petitioners do not contest. The only question is whether a bare request for nominal damages, unmoored from any compensable injury, can independently sustain federal jurisdiction. It cannot.

### I. Nominal Damages Require a Continuing Case or Controversy Just Like Any Other Claim for Relief

This Court has long recognized that mootness doctrine ensures "a plaintiff must demonstrate a 'personal stake in the outcome'" that persists throughout litigation. *Genesis Healthcare*, 569 U.S. at 71 (quoting *Baker v. Carr*, 369 U.S. 186, 204 (1962)). A live claim for damages ordinarily satisfies this requirement because "a case is not moot as long as the parties have a concrete interest, however small, in the outcome." *Chafin v. Chafin*, 568 U.S. 165, 172 (2013). But that principle presupposes a *compensable* damages claim tethered to actual injury—not a symbolic request severed from any remedial function.

Nominal damages, historically, serve only to mark the fact of a legal wrong where no actual damages are proved. See *Carey v. Piphus*, 435 U.S. 247, 266 (1978) (recognizing that "the denial of procedural due process should be actionable for nominal damages without proof of actual injury"). *Carey* held that nominal damages may be awarded in a § 1983 suit *at judgment*, once liability is established, precisely because the plaintiff proved no compensable harm. *Id.* at 266–67. *Carey* did not hold—nor has any decision of this Court held—that the mere *request* for nominal damages in a complaint creates an ongoing case or controversy sufficient to defeat mootness once every other basis for relief has evaporated.

The Eleventh Circuit's holding in *Flanigan's Enterprises, Inc. of Georgia v. City of Sandy Springs*, 868 F.3d 1248 (11th Cir. 2017) (en banc), correctly applied these principles. There, as here, the plaintiffs' claims for declaratory and injunctive relief were mooted by a change in law, and the court held that "a claim for nominal damages, by itself, [is] insufficient to save an otherwise moot case." *Id.* at 1264. The court reasoned that permitting a technical, unquantifiable damages request to override mootness would allow parties to manufacture perpetual live controversies through pleading formalities, contrary to Article III's insistence on a genuine, ongoing dispute. *Id.* at 1262–65.

### II. Petitioners' First Amended Complaint Sought Only Nominal, Not Compensatory, Damages

The district court and the Eleventh Circuit both correctly determined, after examining the complaint "as a whole," that Petitioners requested only nominal damages. Pet. App. 9–11. This finding is entitled to deference and is amply supported by the record. The prayer for relief specifically requested nominal damages, and nowhere did Petitioners allege the kind of "actual injury" that compensatory damages require. As this Court has explained, "compensatory damages" under § 1983 must be grounded in "actual injury caused by the defendant rather than . . . the 'abstract value' of the constitutional rights that may have been violated." *Memphis Community School District v. Stachura*, 477 U.S. 299, 308 (1986); accord *Carey*, 435 U.S. at 264. Petitioners' complaint never alleged monetary loss, physical harm, emotional distress, or reputational injury—the categories of actual injury *Carey* contemplated. *Carey*, 435 U.S. at 263–64. Instead, they rested entirely on the "abstract" constitutional violation, the precise theory *Stachura* forecloses as a basis for compensatory recovery.

Petitioners' post hoc assertions that they intended to seek compensatory damages, raised only in response to a motion to dismiss, cannot rewrite the operative pleading. The Eleventh Circuit correctly declined to indulge this "after-the-fact" recharacterization. Pet. App. 11. Because no compensable injury was ever pled, this case squarely fits within *Flanigan's* rule: once the requests for injunctive and declaratory relief became moot, nothing remained but a symbolic damages request incapable of sustaining jurisdiction.

### III. Permitting Nominal Damages Alone to Preserve Jurisdiction Would Undermine Mootness Doctrine

Petitioners' rule would effectively eliminate mootness in constitutional challenges to government policies, since virtually any plaintiff could append a one-dollar nominal damages request to insulate a case from intervening policy changes. This would deprive governmental defendants of the settled benefit of *voluntary compliance*—an outcome this Court has consistently encouraged. See *Already, LLC*, 568 U.S. at 91 (explaining that voluntary cessation moots a case where "subsequent events ma[ke] it absolutely clear that the allegedly wrongful behavior could not reasonably be expected to recur"). GGC's decision to revise its speech policies mid-litigation—removing precisely the restrictions Petitioners challenged—is exactly the kind of responsive, corrective action that mootness doctrine is designed to credit, not penalize. Allowing a naked nominal-damages request to override that voluntary correction would perversely discourage institutions from promptly fixing unconstitutional policies, lest doing so expose them to perpetual litigation risk untethered to any actual controversy.

### CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
