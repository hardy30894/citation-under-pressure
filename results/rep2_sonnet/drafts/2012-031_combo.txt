# ARGUMENT

A district court may not certify a class under Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 23(b)(3) on the basis of a damages methodology that has not been shown, by admissible and competent evidence, to measure the injury actually caused by the conduct on which liability is premised. The decision below permits certification on exactly the opposite basis: a model that quantifies harm from four theories of anticompetitive conduct, three of which the District Court rejected, was nonetheless accepted as adequate proof of damages flowing from the single surviving theory—deterrence of overbuilder entry. That result cannot be reconciled with settled principles governing proof of damages, the burden borne by a plaintiff seeking classwide treatment, or the due-process limits on binding absent parties to a judgment.

**I. Damages Must Be Proved by a Method Tied to the Specific Unlawful Conduct, Not by Speculation.**

This Court has long insisted that damages—especially in antitrust cases—rest on more than speculation and must bear a demonstrable relationship to the wrong alleged. In *Keogh v. Chicago & Northwestern Railway Co.*, 260 U.S. 156 (1922), the Court denied recovery where the asserted injury could not be traced with any reliability to the challenged rate-fixing conduct, recognizing that a plaintiff cannot recover for harm it cannot causally connect to the defendant's unlawful acts. Similarly, in *Story Parchment Co. v. Paterson Parchment Paper Co.*, 282 U.S. 555 (1931), the Court distinguished between uncertainty as to the *fact* of damage—which may be tolerated—and uncertainty as to whether the damage is properly attributable to the unlawful conduct at all, which may not. And in *Eastman Kodak Co. v. Southern Photo Materials Co.*, 273 U.S. 359 (1927), the Court permitted a jury to approximate damages only because the evidence connected the harm to the very monopolistic practices found unlawful.

*Bigelow v. RKO Radio Pictures, Inc.*, 327 U.S. 251 (1946), confirms that a relaxed standard for the *amount* of damages does not relax the requirement that the plaintiff first show the damages flow from the specific unlawful conduct proven. And in *Zenith Radio Corp. v. Hazeltine Research, Inc.*, 395 U.S. 100 (1969), this Court reiterated that an antitrust plaintiff must show injury "of the type the antitrust laws were intended to prevent and that flows from that which makes defendants' acts unlawful," rejecting recovery untethered to the specific anticompetitive mechanism at issue. These cases establish a single, unbroken principle: a damages methodology divorced from the theory of liability actually before the court is not proof of anything, let alone proof sufficient to satisfy Rule 23(b)(3)'s predominance requirement.

Dr. McClave's model violates this principle. It calculates damages by comparing actual prices to a but-for benchmark generated from all four originally alleged theories of anticompetitive impact. When the District Court winnowed the case to a single theory—deterred overbuilding—it left the model wholly unadjusted. The model therefore cannot show that common evidence predominates as to damages resulting from the *only* theory of liability in the case, because it was never designed to isolate that theory's effects. Under *Story Parchment* and *Zenith Radio*, that is not permissible proof; it is speculation dressed as econometrics.

**II. The Proponent of Certification Bears the Burden of Producing Admissible Evidence, and a Court's Contrary Finding Cannot Stand Without Evidentiary Support.**

It is axiomatic that the party invoking a rule in its favor bears the burden of establishing the facts necessary to satisfy it. *Palmer v. Hoffman*, 318 U.S. 109 (1943). Plaintiffs bore that burden here, and the record does not support the District Court's finding that Dr. McClave's model matched the certified theory. Where a factual finding lacks evidentiary mooring, it cannot survive review. *United States v. U.S. Gypsum Co.*, 333 U.S. 364, 395 (1948) (a finding is clearly erroneous when the reviewing court is "left with the definite and firm conviction that a mistake has been committed"). Because the model measured harm from theories no longer in the case, the finding that it offered common proof of damages under the surviving theory is not merely debatable—it is unsupported.

That the district court may "look behind the pleadings" to resolve Rule 23 disputes does not excuse this deficiency. *Eisen v. Carlisle & Jacquelin*, 391 F.2d 555 (2d Cir. 1968), confirms that courts may probe beneath allegations to determine whether Rule 23's prerequisites are actually met—but probing beneath the pleadings means testing them against real evidence, not accepting a mismatched model on faith.

**III. Due Process Requires That Common Proof Actually Exist Before Absent Class Members Are Bound.**

Finally, because a class judgment binds absent members who never appear, this Court has insisted that classwide treatment be reserved for cases where the interests of class members are genuinely aligned by common questions of fact. *Hansberry v. Lee*, 311 U.S. 32, 40 (1940) ("[O]ne is not bound by a judgment in personam in a litigation in which he is not designated as a party."). Certifying a class on a model that does not correspond to the theory of liability risks binding thousands of absent Comcast subscribers to a damages determination that was never actually common to them. That risk is precisely what Rule 23(b)(3)'s predominance requirement, read consistently with *Hansberry*, *Palmer*, and *Gypsum*, is designed to prevent.

The judgment below should be reversed.
