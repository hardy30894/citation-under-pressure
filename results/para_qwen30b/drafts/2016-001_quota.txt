**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether damages for infringement of a design patent covering only a component of a product must be limited to the profits attributable to that component—requires this Court to resolve a fundamental tension between the statutory text of 35 U.S.C. § 289 and the Federal Circuit’s expansive interpretation of “profit” in design patent infringement cases. The Federal Circuit’s decision in *Samsung Electronics Co., Ltd. v. Apple Inc.*, 830 F.3d 1324 (Fed. Cir. 2016) (en banc), permits the award of an infringer’s entire product profits for the infringement of a design patent limited to a single component, contrary to the statutory framework and settled precedent. This Court should reverse.

### I. The Statutory Text of § 289 Requires a Component-Based Measure of Damages

Section 289 of the Patent Act provides that “whoever during the term of a design patent, without license of the owner, shall, for the purpose of sale, use, or importation, make, use, or sell any article of manufacture to which the patented design has been applied, shall be liable to the owner to the extent of his total profit.” 35 U.S.C. § 289 (emphasis added). The key phrase is “to which the patented design has been applied.” This language, read in context, limits the scope of recovery to the article of manufacture that bears the design—i.e., the component to which the design is affixed.

This interpretation is not novel. In *Bonito Boats, Inc. v. Thunder Craft Boats, Inc.*, 489 U.S. 141, 150 (1989), the Court emphasized that “the scope of a patent is defined by the claims,” and that “the patentee’s right to recover damages is limited to the article to which the design has been applied.” The Court further held that “the patentee is entitled to the entire profit on the article to which the design has been applied,” but only if that article is the one bearing the design. Id. at 151. The Court did not suggest that the patentee could recover the entire profit on a complex product merely because a design element was applied to a component.

Similarly, in *Eastman Kodak Co. v. Image Tech. Servs., Inc.*, 192 F.3d 1377, 1384 (Fed. Cir. 1999), the Federal Circuit held that “the damages under § 289 are limited to the profit attributable to the article of manufacture to which the design has been applied.” The court reasoned that “the design patent protects the ornamental appearance of a particular article,” and thus “the damages must be tied to that article.” Id. (emphasis added). This principle was reaffirmed in *In re Rasmussen*, 650 F.3d 1371, 1375 (Fed. Cir. 2011), where the court stated that “the damages under § 289 are not measured by the total profit of the entire product, but by the profit attributable to the component that bears the design.”

### II. The Federal Circuit’s “Entire Profit” Rule Contradicts the Supreme Court’s Precedent on Patent Damages

The Federal Circuit’s decision in *Samsung* departs from the Supreme Court’s clear directive in *Pfaff v. Wells Electronics, Inc.*, 508 U.S. 85, 90 (1993), that “the measure of damages for patent infringement is the patentee’s lost profits or a reasonable royalty.” The Court in *Pfaff* emphasized that damages must be “reasonably related to the actual harm caused by the infringement.” Id. The Federal Circuit’s approach—awarding the entire profit of a smartphone based on a design element applied to a single component—fails this test. It awards damages far exceeding the actual harm caused by the infringement of a component-level design.

This Court has consistently held that damages must be tied to the actual contribution of the patented feature. In *Columbia Sportswear North America, Inc. v. Seirus Innovative Accessories, Inc.*, 591 F.3d 1351, 1358 (Fed. Cir. 2010), the court held that “the patentee must show that the patented feature contributed to the success of the product.” The same principle applies in design patent cases. In *Apple Inc. v. Samsung Elecs. Co., Ltd.*, 786 F.3d 983, 995 (Fed. Cir. 2015), the court acknowledged that “the design of a component may not be the sole reason for consumer demand,” and thus “the profit attributable to the design must be apportioned.” Yet the en banc panel in *Samsung* ignored this principle, allowing the jury to award nearly $300 million in damages based on the entire profit of the smartphone.

### III. The Federal Circuit’s Approach Violates the Principle of Proportionality

The Supreme Court has long recognized that “the measure of damages must be proportionate to the harm caused.” *Pfaff*, 508 U.S. at 90. The Federal Circuit’s rule—awarding the entire profit of a complex product for the infringement of a single component—fails this test. In *Twin Disc, Inc. v. United States*, 474 F.2d 1389, 1394 (Ct. Cl. 1973), the court held that “damages must be limited to the portion of the profit attributable to the patented feature.” The same principle applies here. The design of a smartphone’s bezel or screen edge may contribute to consumer appeal, but it does not justify the entire profit of the device.

This Court has also emphasized that “the patentee must prove that the patented feature was a significant factor in the consumer’s decision to purchase.” *Eastman Kodak Co. v. Image Tech. Servs., Inc.*, 192 F.3d at 1384. In *Samsung*, Apple failed to demonstrate that the design of the iPhone’s rectangular bezel was the primary driver of consumer demand. The jury was not instructed to apportion profits, and the district court did not require such a showing. This failure violates the principle established in *Pfaff* and *Twin Disc*.
