The Double Jeopardy Clause does not preclude retrial on a prior-conviction allegation in a noncapital sentencing proceeding. The Clause protects against being “twice put in jeopardy of life or limb,” U.S. Const. amend. V, and its core office is to prevent a second prosecution for the same offense after an acquittal. See Ex parte Lange, 85 U.S. (18 Wall.) 163 (1874). But a recidivist allegation is not a new offense; it is a sentencing factor that informs punishment for the latest crime. The Constitution has never been understood to require that every fact bearing on sentence be tried as if it were an element of guilt. See Graham v. West Virginia, 224 U.S. 616 (1912); McDonald v. Massachusetts, 180 U.S. 311 (1901).

Long before 1970, this Court recognized that prior-conviction proceedings are distinct from the trial of the substantive offense. In Graham, the Court upheld a recidivist statute providing for increased punishment based on earlier convictions, explaining that the statute “imposes a heavier punishment for the new crime” because the offender “is a persistent criminal.” 224 U.S. at 623-24. Likewise, McDonald rejected the claim that a defendant must be separately indicted and tried by jury on the prior convictions that trigger enhanced punishment, holding that the recidivist determination concerns “the punishment to be inflicted” for the latest offense. 180 U.S. at 312-13, 312. These decisions establish that the Constitution does not transform a prior-conviction allegation into a separate prosecution for double jeopardy purposes.

The Court’s capital-sentencing cases confirm the point by contrast. In Stroud v. United States, 251 U.S. 15 (1919), the Court held that a defendant could be sentenced to death on retrial even though the first jury had recommended “without capital punishment.” The Court explained that the second proceeding did not place the defendant twice in jeopardy for the same offense in the relevant sense. Id. at 18-19. Similarly, in Painter v. United States, 158 U.S. 89 (1895), the Court sustained a retrial and harsher sentence after reversal, making clear that sentencing consequences may change without violating the Double Jeopardy Clause. See also Murphy v. Massachusetts, 177 U.S. 155 (1900).

Petitioner’s effort to constitutionalize an acquittal rule for a failed sentencing enhancement finds no support in the pre-1970 cases. When the Court has spoken of “jeopardy,” it has done so in connection with adjudicating guilt, not with the separate question whether a repeat offender deserves greater punishment. See Kepner v. United States, 195 U.S. 100 (1904). Kepner emphasized that the constitutional bar prevents a second prosecution after an adjudication of guilt or innocence, id. at 133-34, but it did not hold that an unsuccessful attempt to prove a prior conviction bars a renewed sentencing inquiry.

Nor is there any constitutional requirement that the first attempt at proving recidivism be final in the same way as a verdict of acquittal. In Bartell v. United States, 227 U.S. 427 (1913), the Court again distinguished between the punishment phase and the merits of the offense, underscoring that procedural protections attending trial do not automatically govern sentencing-related determinations. And in Frisbie v. Collins, 342 U.S. 519 (1952), the Court reaffirmed that due process is not violated merely because a conviction or sentence is reached through procedures that differ from the criminal trial model, so long as the defendant is lawfully before the court and the proceeding itself is authorized.

Petitioner relies on the notion that because the prior-conviction allegation was not proven sufficiently the first time, retrial would be akin to a second prosecution after acquittal. But the pre-1970 cases reject that equivalence. A failure of proof on a sentencing allegation does not amount to an acquittal of a criminal offense. The enhancement does not define the wrong; it affects only punishment for the current wrong. See Graham, 224 U.S. at 623-24; McDonald, 180 U.S. at 312-13. Because the State’s renewed attempt would seek only a lawful sentence for the present convictions, the Double Jeopardy Clause is not implicated.

Accordingly, under the settled pre-1970 authorities, the Constitution does not bar retrial of a prior-conviction allegation in a noncapital sentencing proceeding.
