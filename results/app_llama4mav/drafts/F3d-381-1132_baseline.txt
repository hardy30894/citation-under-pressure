ARGUMENT

I. The Court of Federal Claims Erred in Holding that the Government's Actions Did Not Constitute a Per Se Physical Taking.

The Court of Federal Claims' decision granting summary judgment in favor of the government was based on an erroneous understanding of the law regarding physical takings. The court held that the scope of the easement was irrelevant and that the physical presence of the fiber optic cable over the easement was not a taking. However, this reasoning ignores the fact that the government's actions exceeded the scope of the easement, resulting in a physical occupation of Tuthill's property.

The Supreme Court has long held that a physical occupation of property constitutes a per se taking, requiring just compensation under the Fifth Amendment. See Loretto v. Teleprompter Manhattan CATV Corp., 458 U.S. 419 (1982). In Loretto, the Court held that a state law requiring landlords to permit cable television companies to install cable facilities on their property constituted a taking, as it resulted in a permanent physical occupation of the landlord's property. Id. at 441. The Court emphasized that "when the 'character of the governmental action' is a permanent physical occupation of property, our cases uniformly have found a taking." Id. at 426 (quoting Penn Central Transp. Co. v. New York City, 438 U.S. 104, 124 (1978)).

Similarly, in this case, the government's installation of fiber optic cables with excess capacity and its subsequent leasing of that capacity to third parties constitutes a physical occupation of Tuthill's property. The government's actions are not merely an exercise of its rights under the easement, but rather an overreaching that burdens Tuthill's property beyond what was contemplated by the original easement agreement. See United States v. Causby, 328 U.S. 256, 265-66 (1946) (holding that the government's use of airspace above a landowner's property constituted a taking, as it interfered with the landowner's use and enjoyment of the property).

The Court of Federal Claims' reliance on the fact that the physical presence of the fiber optic cable was not a new intrusion is misplaced. The government's actions have resulted in a new and additional burden on Tuthill's property, namely, the leasing of excess capacity to third parties. This is not simply a matter of the government exercising its existing rights under the easement, but rather a new use that was not contemplated by the original agreement.

The government's argument that the excess capacity was installed to accommodate future growth is not a justification for its actions. The fact remains that the government is currently leasing that excess capacity to third parties, resulting in a physical occupation of Tuthill's property that is not authorized by the easement. See Hendler v. United States, 952 F.2d 1364, 1376 (Fed. Cir. 1991) (holding that the government's installation of monitoring wells on a landowner's property constituted a taking, as it was not authorized by any existing easement or agreement).

In sum, the Court of Federal Claims erred in holding that the government's actions did not constitute a per se physical taking. The government's installation of fiber optic cables with excess capacity and its leasing of that capacity to third parties constitutes a physical occupation of Tuthill's property, requiring just compensation under the Fifth Amendment.

II. The Court of Federal Claims Should Have Considered the Scope of the Easement in Determining Whether a Taking Occurred.

The Court of Federal Claims' decision to disregard the scope of the easement was erroneous. The scope of the easement is directly relevant to determining whether the government's actions constituted a taking. As the Supreme Court has held, the government's actions must be evaluated in light of the property rights that were acquired through the easement. See United States v. General Motors Corp., 323 U.S. 373, 378 (1945).

In this case, the easement granted the government the right to install "signal lines... necessary in connection therewith." The government's installation of fiber optic cables with excess capacity and its leasing of that capacity to third parties exceeds the scope of this easement. The Court of Federal Claims should have considered the scope of the easement in determining whether a taking occurred, rather than dismissing it as irrelevant.

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Court of Federal Claims should be reversed, and the case remanded for further proceedings to determine the just compensation owed to Tuthill for the government's physical taking of its property.
