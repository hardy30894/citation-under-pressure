**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Releases Are Void Because the WARN Act’s Protective Rights Cannot Be Waived.**

The Worker Adjustment and Retraining Notification Act, 29 U.S.C. §§ 2101–2109, was enacted to shield employees from the devastating effects of sudden plant closings and mass layoffs. Its notice requirement is a mandatory, non-waivable public protection. Pre-1970 Supreme Court precedent firmly establishes that when a federal statute confers a right for the benefit of a vulnerable class, that right may not be bargained away. In *Brooklyn Savings Bank v. O’Neil*, 324 U.S. 697, 704–05 (1945), the Court held that a statutory right to liquidated damages under the Fair Labor Standards Act “cannot be waived or released” because the statute “is a remedial measure designed to protect the employee from the evil of oppressive wages.” The Court reasoned that “the right to sue for the statutory penalty is a right which belongs to the employee and which the employer may not defeat by obtaining a release.” *Id.* at 705. Similarly, in *D. A. Schulte, Inc. v. Gangi*, 328 U.S. 108, 115 (1946), the Court invalidated a settlement agreement that released FLSA wage claims, stating that “the policy of the Act . . . forbids the use of a release to bar the employee’s right to the statutory minimum.” The WARN Act’s purpose—to provide employees with 60 days’ notice to prepare for job loss—is equally protective. The releases signed by Ellis, Price, and the putative class expressly waive “any claims under . . . the Federal Worker Adjustment and Retraining Notification (WARN) Act.” Such a waiver directly contravenes the Act’s policy and is therefore void. *See also Patterson v. Bark Eudora*, 190 U.S. 169, 172–73 (1903) (seamen’s wage rights, created for their protection, cannot be contracted away).

**II. Even If the WARN Act Could Be Waived, These Releases Were Not Knowing and Voluntary.**

The releases were procured under coercive circumstances that negate any knowing and voluntary consent. DHL announced the end of all domestic shipping on November 10, 2008, and the severance agreements were not finalized until December 5, 2008, with the ten-week plan requiring a decision by December 11—just days later. Employees were simultaneously required to complete a job rebid form, intertwining their future shift preferences with the severance decision. This compressed timeline, coupled with the imminent loss of employment, constituted economic duress. Pre-1970 contract law recognizes that a release obtained under threat of economic harm is voidable. *United States v. Bethlehem Steel Corp.*, 315 U.S. 289, 300–01 (1942) (contracts induced by duress are unenforceable); *Railroad Co. v. Schutte*, 103 U.S. 118, 143 (1880) (a release “obtained by oppression or fraud” is invalid). The seven-day consideration period was illusory given the simultaneous deadline for the ten-week plan. Employees had no realistic opportunity to consult counsel or weigh alternatives. The releases therefore lack the hallmarks of a voluntary, informed waiver. *See also The Steamer St. Lawrence*, 66 U.S. 522, 528 (1861) (a contract signed under “the pressure of immediate necessity” is not freely made).

**III. DHL’s Shutdown of Five Chicagoland Facilities Constitutes a Mass Layoff Under the WARN Act.**

The undisputed facts show that DHL permanently closed five of its six Chicagoland facilities, terminating over 500 employees. This is a “mass layoff” as defined by the Act: a reduction in force affecting at least 50 employees at a single site. The WARN Act requires 60 days’ written notice. DHL gave less than 60 days. The releases do not relieve DHL of its statutory obligation. In interpreting remedial statutes, courts must look to the “plain and obvious” purpose of the legislation. *Holy Trinity Church v. United States*, 143 U.S. 457, 459 (1892). The Act’s purpose is to provide notice, not to allow employers to buy a waiver with severance pay. The district court’s enforcement of the releases subverts the statutory scheme.

**CONCLUSION**

For these reasons, the releases are void, and the district court should be reversed. The case should be remanded for a determination of DHL’s liability for failing to provide the required 60-day notice.
