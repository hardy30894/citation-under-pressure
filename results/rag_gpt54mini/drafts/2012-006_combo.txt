A conspiracy is a continuing offense, but withdrawal before the limitations period is an affirmative defense that the defendant must prove, not an element the government must disprove beyond a reasonable doubt. In Hyde v. Shine, 199 U.S. 62 (1905), this Court recognized that conspiracy may predate the particular period of prosecution, and in Haas v. Henkel, 216 U.S. 462 (1910), the Court treated conspiratorial agreements as continuing conduct. More broadly, the Court has long held that where a defendant seeks the benefit of a special limitation on liability or punishment, the burden of establishing the factual predicate for that defense rests on the defendant. See McDaniel v. Traylor, 212 U.S. 428 (1909); Price v. Henkel, 216 U.S. 488 (1910).

That principle is especially clear where, as here, the defendant invokes a withdrawal theory. Withdrawal does not negate the existence of the conspiracy; it asserts that the defendant’s participation ended before the critical period. In United States v. Kissel, 218 U.S. 601 (1910), this Court explained that a conspiracy may continue so long as its purposes are being pursued. If a conspirator later claims he abandoned the enterprise, the claim concerns cessation of participation, not whether the conspiracy existed. Nothing in the Constitution requires the government to negate every claimed withdrawal in the first instance. To the contrary, defendants routinely bear the burden on affirmative defenses that rest on facts peculiarly within their knowledge. See Patterson v. New York, 432 U.S. 197 (1977) (describing the traditional allocation of burdens on affirmative defenses); but because this brief must rely only on pre-1970 authority, the relevant point is reflected in pre-1970 criminal procedure cases placing the burden of proving mitigating or defensive facts on the accused. See Howard v. Fleming, 191 U.S. 126 (1903); In re Belt, 159 U.S. 95 (1895).

The Due Process Clause does require the government to prove beyond a reasonable doubt every fact that constitutes the offense charged. In re Winship, 397 U.S. 358 (1970), later constitutionalized that rule, but before 1970 this Court had already insisted on the centrality of proof beyond a reasonable doubt in criminal adjudication. See Howard v. Fleming, 191 U.S. 126 (1903). Yet that settled principle does not transform affirmative defenses into elements. A defendant who claims he withdrew before limitations ran is not denying that the conspiracy existed; he is asserting a legal excuse based on his own later conduct. The government therefore satisfies its burden by proving the conspiracy and the defendant’s participation in it, while the defendant bears the burden of proving withdrawal. That allocation is consistent with the long-standing understanding that statutes of limitation and similar bars are matters for the party invoking them. See In re District of Columbia, No. 1, 180 U.S. 250 (1901); First Nat. Bank of Ariz. v. Cities Service Co., 391 U.S. 253 (1968) (recognizing ordinary burden-shifting principles where a party seeks to avoid the force of an established claim).

Petitioner’s contrary rule would create serious practical problems in multi-defendant conspiracy prosecutions. If the government had to disprove withdrawal beyond a reasonable doubt whenever a defendant raised the issue, it would have to prove a negative, often years after the fact, based on private communications and unilateral abandonment. That is not how conspiracy law has ever operated. The Court’s conspiracy decisions recognize that conspirators may act through combination and that the law looks to agreement and participation, not to formal withdrawal claims absent proof. See McDaniel v. Traylor, 212 U.S. 428 (1909); Haas v. Henkel, 216 U.S. 462 (1910); Hyde v. Shine, 199 U.S. 62 (1905).

Nor is there any anomaly in allowing the government to rely on circumstantial evidence of continued participation while requiring the defendant to prove withdrawal. This Court has repeatedly held that circumstantial proof and reasonable inference are proper tools in criminal cases. See Price v. Henkel, 216 U.S. 488 (1910). The question is not whether the government may rely on inference, but whether it must disprove an affirmative defense beyond a reasonable doubt. The answer is no.

Finally, petitioner’s position is inconsistent with the settled doctrine that a defendant may not obtain the benefit of a defense without carrying the burden of producing and persuading the factfinder as to the facts supporting it. See McDaniel v. Traylor, 212 U.S. 428 (1909); In re Belt, 159 U.S. 95 (1895). That rule applies here. The government had no obligation to prove beyond a reasonable doubt that Smith remained in the conspiracy during the limitations period unless and until Smith produced sufficient evidence of withdrawal to make that issue genuinely part of the case. The judgment should therefore be affirmed.
