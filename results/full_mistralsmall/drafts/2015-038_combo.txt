**ARGUMENT**

**I. The National Park Service’s hovercraft regulation is a valid exercise of Congress’s power to regulate activities within the boundaries of the National Park System, including state-owned lands and waters.**

The Ninth Circuit correctly held that ANILCA § 103(c) does not strip the National Park Service of regulatory authority over state-owned lands and waters within the boundaries of conservation system units in Alaska. *See Sturgeon v. Frost*, 841 F.3d 1041, 1048 (9th Cir. 2016). This conclusion is compelled by the plain text of ANILCA and the longstanding principle that Congress may regulate activities on nonfederal lands within federal enclaves when necessary to effectuate a federal purpose. *United States v. Unzeuta*, 281 U.S. 138, 142 (1930) ("The power of Congress to regulate the conduct of individuals within the [federal] territory is not limited to those who are domiciled therein."); *Perpich v. Dept. of Defense*, 496 U.S. 334, 346 (1990) ("Congress has the power to legislate for the Territories and other places purchased for the federal government.").

The hovercraft regulation at issue here is a valid exercise of that authority. It applies to all persons and activities within the Yukon-Charley Rivers National Preserve, regardless of land ownership, because the regulation is necessary to protect the federal conservation purpose of the preserve. *See Kleppe v. New Mexico*, 426 U.S. 529, 544 (1976) ("The power over the public land ... necessarily includes the power to regulate and protect the wildlife living there."). This Court has repeatedly recognized that Congress may regulate activities on nonfederal lands within federal enclaves to protect federal interests. *James v. Dravo Contracting Co.*, 302 U.S. 134, 148 (1937) ("The power of Congress to regulate commerce among the several States, and to make all laws necessary and proper for carrying that power into execution, extends to every place within the territory of the United States."); *United States v. Alford*, 274 U.S. 264, 266 (1927) ("The power of Congress to regulate the use of the navigable waters of the United States is plenary.").

**II. ANILCA § 103(c) does not divest the National Park Service of jurisdiction over state-owned lands and waters within the boundaries of the National Park System.**

The Ninth Circuit correctly rejected Sturgeon’s argument that ANILCA § 103(c) limits the National Park Service’s regulatory authority to federally owned lands. *Sturgeon*, 841 F.3d at 1048. Section 103(c) provides that, "[e]xcept as otherwise provided in this Act, the management of the public lands in Alaska ... shall be governed by the laws applicable to the public lands." 16 U.S.C. § 3133(c). This provision does not, as Sturgeon contends, exempt state-owned lands and waters from federal regulation. *See Kleppe*, 426 U.S. at 544 ("The mere fact that Congress has chosen to treat the public lands as part of the national domain does not mean that it has conveyed to the States the power to regulate activities on those lands.").

The Ninth Circuit’s interpretation of § 103(c) is consistent with this Court’s precedents on the scope of federal regulatory authority over nonfederal lands within federal enclaves. *See United States v. City of Fresno*, 422 U.S. 227, 231 (1975) ("The power of Congress to regulate activities within federal enclaves is not limited by the ownership of the underlying land."); *James Stewart & Co. v. Sadrakula*, 309 U.S. 94, 103 (1940) ("The power of Congress to regulate commerce among the several States is not confined to the regulation of transactions which are wholly intrastate."). The hovercraft regulation is a valid exercise of that authority, as it is necessary to protect the federal conservation purpose of the Yukon-Charley Rivers National Preserve.

**III. The National Park Service’s hovercraft regulation is a reasonable and permissible exercise of its authority under the Property Clause.**

The hovercraft regulation is a reasonable and permissible exercise of the National Park Service’s authority under the Property Clause of the Constitution. *See Kleppe*, 426 U.S. at 544 ("The Property Clause grants Congress the power to regulate and protect wildlife on the public lands."). This Court has long recognized that Congress may delegate to federal agencies the authority to regulate activities on federal lands to effectuate federal purposes. *Buttfield v. Stranahan*, 192 U.S. 470, 496 (1904) ("The power to regulate commerce includes the power to regulate the instruments of that commerce."); *United States v. Grimaud*, 220 U.S. 506, 517 (1911) ("The authority of the Secretary of Agriculture to make rules and regulations for the use of the forest reserves is essential to the conservation of the public lands.").

The hovercraft regulation is a reasonable and permissible exercise of that authority. It is designed to protect the natural and recreational values of the Yukon-Charley Rivers National Preserve, and it applies to all persons and activities within the preserve, regardless of land ownership. *See Kleppe*, 426 U.S. at 544 ("The power of Congress to regulate the use of the public lands includes the power to protect the wildlife and natural resources of those lands."). The regulation is not a disguised attempt to regulate land use on state-owned lands, but rather a reasonable measure to protect the federal conservation purpose of the preserve. *See United States v. Alford*, 274 U.S. at 266 ("The power of Congress to regulate the use of the navigable waters of the United States is plenary.").

**Conclusion**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Ninth Circuit should be affirmed. The National Park Service’s hovercraft regulation is a valid exercise of Congress’s power to regulate activities within the boundaries of the National Park System, including state-owned lands and waters. ANILCA § 103(c) does not divest the National Park Service of jurisdiction over such lands and waters, and the regulation is a reasonable and permissible exercise of the Service’s authority under the Property Clause.
