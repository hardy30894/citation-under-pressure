**ARGUMENT**

**I. The District Court Lacked Subject Matter Jurisdiction Because This Case Presents a Purely State-Law Dispute Over Property Rights.**

The district court erred in asserting jurisdiction under 28 U.S.C. § 1331 and 16 U.S.C. § 825p. A federal question must appear on the face of a well-pleaded complaint. *Louisville & Nashville R.R. Co. v. Mottley*, 211 U.S. 149, 152 (1908). The Pressls’ complaint seeks only a declaration of their rights under a flowage easement—a state-law property dispute. No federal statute, regulation, or constitutional provision is invoked. APCO’s removal cannot create jurisdiction where none exists. *See Gully v. First Nat’l Bank*, 299 U.S. 109, 113 (1936) (federal question must be an “essential element” of plaintiff’s claim).

The district court relied on 16 U.S.C. § 825p, which grants federal jurisdiction over “suits brought to enforce” the Federal Power Act. But the Pressls do not seek to enforce, challenge, or interpret any federal license or statute. They seek only to construe a private easement. This Court has long held that “a suit to enforce a right which takes its origin in the laws of the United States is not necessarily, or for that reason alone, one arising under those laws.” *Shulthis v. McDougal*, 225 U.S. 561, 569 (1912). The Pressls’ right to build a dock arises from their deed and the easement—instruments of state law. *See also American Well Works Co. v. Layne & Bowler Co.*, 241 U.S. 257, 260 (1916) (Holmes, J.) (a suit “arises under the law that creates the cause of action”).

**II. The Federal Power Act Does Not Confer Jurisdiction Over a Dispute That Does Not Implicate Federal Regulatory Authority.**

Section 825p provides jurisdiction only for “suits brought to enforce” the Federal Power Act or FERC orders. The Pressls’ complaint does not seek to enforce any federal law. It asks only that the court declare the parties’ rights under the easement. Even if APCO’s license touches the property, that does not transform a state-law dispute into a federal case. *See Puget Sound Power & Light Co. v. Seattle*, 291 U.S. 619, 625 (1934) (federal jurisdiction does not arise merely because a federal license is “incidentally involved”). The Supreme Court has cautioned that “the federal nature of the right to be established is decisive—not the source of the authority under which the defendant may act.” *Gully*, 299 U.S. at 116.

**III. The District Court Erred in Dismissing the Complaint on the Merits After Erroneously Assuming Jurisdiction.**

Because the district court lacked subject matter jurisdiction, it had no authority to dismiss the complaint on the merits. *See Mansfield, C. & L.M. Ry. Co. v. Swan*, 111 U.S. 379, 382 (1884) (federal courts must first determine jurisdiction before proceeding to the merits). The court’s analysis of the easement under Virginia law and its suggestion that the Pressls must first exhaust FERC remedies were therefore void. *See Ex parte McCardle*, 74 U.S. (7 Wall.) 506, 514 (1868) (jurisdiction is “the power to declare the law”).

**CONCLUSION**

For these reasons, the judgment of the district court should be vacated, and the case remanded with instructions to remand to state court.
