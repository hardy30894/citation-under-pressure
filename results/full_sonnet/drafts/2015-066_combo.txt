ARGUMENT

I. Tribal court convictions are valid judicial acts of a separate sovereign to which the Sixth Amendment does not apply.

The Sixth Amendment right to appointed counsel, first fully articulated in Johnson v. Zerbst, 304 U.S. 458 (1938), and extended to the States in Gideon v. Wainwright, 372 U.S. 335 (1963), constrains only proceedings conducted under federal or state authority. As this Court held in Gideon, "any person haled into court, who is too poor to hire a lawyer, cannot be assured a fair trial unless counsel is provided for him," 372 U.S. at 344, but that guarantee arises from and is limited to the constitutional structure binding the federal government and, through the Fourteenth Amendment, the States. It does not reach the Northern Cheyenne Tribal Court, a forum whose authority derives not from the United States Constitution but from the Tribe's own retained, pre-constitutional sovereignty. See Talton v. Mayes, 163 U.S. 376 (1896).

That principle is not new. In Ex parte Crow Dog, 109 U.S. 556 (1883), this Court recognized that Indian tribes retained the power to punish offenses committed by their own members according to their own law and custom, unless and until Congress provided otherwise. And in United States v. Kagama, 118 U.S. 375 (1886), the Court explained that the tribes' relationship to the federal government does not convert tribal governance into an exercise of federal power subject to federal constitutional restraint; rather, the tribes are "a separate people, with the power of regulating their internal and social relations," id. at 381-82, whose authority "arises," in part, from the tribes being, "from their very weakness and helplessness," dependent wards entitled to federal protection, id. at 383-84 — a dependency that Congress may accommodate, but that does not itself import the Sixth Amendment into tribal proceedings. Because Bryant's tribal convictions were rendered by a sovereign never bound by the Sixth Amendment, they were not "constitutionally infirm" in any sense cognizable by this Court's precedents; they were simply valid exercises of a different sovereign's law.

II. Use of a valid, uncounseled prior conviction as an element of a status-based recidivist offense does not offend the Sixth Amendment because the defendant is punished only for the offense of conviction.

Section 117(a) is a recidivist statute of the type this Court has long sustained. In Graham v. West Virginia, 224 U.S. 616 (1912), and McDonald v. Massachusetts, 180 U.S. 311 (1901), the Court upheld habitual-offender laws that increase punishment based on the fact of prior convictions, explaining that such statutes punish only the most recent offense, with the defendant's history serving merely to establish the character and seriousness of that offense — not to reopen or re-punish the earlier convictions themselves. See McDonald, 180 U.S. at 312-13; Moore v. Missouri, 159 U.S. 673, 677 (1895) (habitual-criminal statutes "impos[e] a heavier penalty for the second offense, taking into consideration the first offense as a factor"). This Court reaffirmed the same principle in Oyler v. Boles, 368 U.S. 448 (1962), and Spencer v. Texas, 385 U.S. 554 (1967), sustaining the evidentiary and pleading use of prior convictions—valid when rendered—in later proceedings without requiring the government to relitigate their procedural pedigree.

Section 117(a) operates identically. The predicate tribal convictions are not "essential elements" in the sense of separately punishable conduct; they establish Bryant's status as a repeat domestic violence offender, a status that Congress may rationally use to define an enhanced federal offense, just as the habitual-offender statutes in Graham, McDonald, and Moore used prior convictions to define enhanced state offenses. Because those prior convictions were valid when entered — tribal courts never having been obligated to provide Sixth Amendment counsel — their subsequent use to establish recidivist status works no constitutional injury.

III. Principles of comity owed to tribal court judgments preclude treating a facially valid tribal conviction as though it were void.

This Court has long held that domestic courts owe substantial deference to the judgments of other sovereigns, extending recognition to judgments rendered under procedures that differ markedly from the Anglo-American model. In Hilton v. Guyot, 159 U.S. 113 (1895), the Court explained that such recognition, or "comity," "is neither a matter of absolute obligation on the one hand, nor of mere courtesy and good will, upon the other," but reflects respect for the regularity of another sovereign's judicial process, id. at 163-64. Tribal courts warrant no less respect. In Williams v. Lee, 358 U.S. 217 (1959), this Court insisted that federal courts must be vigilant not to undermine tribal authority to "make their own laws and be ruled by them," id. at 220, particularly where, as here, Congress itself has calibrated the procedural protections owed in tribal proceedings through its own legislative judgment rather than through wholesale importation of the Sixth Amendment. To hold that a valid tribal conviction becomes retroactively void for federal recidivism purposes because the tribal forum did not supply Sixth Amendment counsel would disregard the comity due tribal judgments and supplant Congress's considered judgment with a judicially invented equivalence requirement found nowhere in the Constitution.

For these reasons, Bryant's uncounseled but valid tribal convictions were properly used to satisfy § 117(a)'s predicate-offense requirement.
