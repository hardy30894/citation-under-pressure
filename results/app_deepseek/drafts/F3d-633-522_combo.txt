**ARGUMENT**

This appeal challenges the validity of releases that extinguished Appellants’ rights under the Worker Adjustment and Retraining Notification Act, 29 U.S.C. §§ 2101-2109. The releases are unenforceable because they were obtained under circumstances that deprived Appellants of a meaningful opportunity to understand the rights they were surrendering, and because they require the waiver of statutory protections in a manner contrary to public policy.

First, the releases cannot stand because they were procured through pressure and inadequate time for reflection. Appellants had only a few days—sometimes just two—to decide whether to accept severance and abandon their WARN Act claims. A release of a federal statutory right must be “made with full understanding” of the right being waived. *Callen v. Pennsylvania R.R. Co.*, 332 U.S. 625, 630 (1948). Where a release is executed under conditions that prevent a “free and voluntary” choice, it is void. *Dice v. Akron, Canton & Youngstown R.R. Co.*, 342 U.S. 359, 362 (1952). The truncated decision periods here—combined with the simultaneous requirement to complete job rebid forms under threat of losing employment—created a coercive atmosphere that undermined any knowing waiver. See *Burch v. Union Pac. R.R. Co.*, 349 U.S. 504, 508 (1955) (release obtained by duress or misrepresentation invalid).

Second, the releases are invalid because they purport to waive Appellants’ rights under the WARN Act before the triggering event—a mass layoff—had even occurred. The Act requires employers to give 60 days’ notice before ordering a plant closing or mass layoff, 29 U.S.C. § 2102(a). By requiring Appellants to sign releases and resign immediately, DHL effectively forced them to relinquish claims based on a layoff that DHL had *already* publicly announced for January 30, 2009. A release that extinguishes a right *before* it has fully accrued violates the principle that a party cannot contract away a statutory entitlement that has not yet ripened. See *J.I. Case Co. v. NLRB*, 321 U.S. 332, 337 (1944) (individual contracts cannot waive rights secured by federal labor law); *NLRB v. National Licorice Co.*, 309 U.S. 350, 365 (1940) (contracts that “interfere with the exercise of rights guaranteed by the Act” are void).

Third, the releases contravene the public policy embodied in the WARN Act. Congress enacted the WARN Act to protect workers and communities from sudden job loss, and to ensure that employees have time to seek new employment or retraining. Allowing an employer to bypass this notice requirement by pressuring employees into signing releases—especially when the union negotiated the releases—undermines the statute’s remedial purpose. A contract that violates a “public policy clearly expressed in the law” is unenforceable. *Henningsen v. Bloomfield Motors, Inc.*, 32 N.J. 358, 404 (1960). Here, the releases effectively nullify the WARN Act’s notice mandate for the 506 workers who accepted severance, and thus cannot be upheld.

Fourth, the union that negotiated these releases could not validly waive Appellants’ individual statutory rights. A union acts as a bargaining representative, but it cannot—without the employees’ informed and voluntary consent—surrender rights that Congress intended to protect individually. *Steele v. Louisville & Nashville R.R. Co.*, 323 U.S. 192, 204 (1944) (union cannot “sacrifice the rights of the minority” or “negotiate a contract which is utterly discriminatory”). Moreover, the union’s involvement does not immunize the releases from scrutiny. *Machinists v. NLRB*, 133 F.2d 868, 872 (7th Cir. 1943) (union cannot waive employees’ statutory rights without their “free and unrestrained consent”). The record shows that Appellants were given no meaningful choice: they could either accept the severance package—with its release of WARN claims—or face layoff without any severance at all. This is not the “free and voluntary” consent the law requires.

Finally, the releases are overbroad. They waive “any and all” claims, including those under the WARN Act, without any individual consideration of the specific rights being relinquished. A general release of unknown statutory claims is presumptively invalid unless the employee “knowingly and voluntarily” agrees to it. *Callen*, 332 U.S. at 630. Here, the releases were presented as a take-it-or-leave-it term, and the seven-day consideration period—while technically satisfied—was illusory given the simultaneous demands to complete job rebid forms. The totality of the circumstances demonstrates that Appellants did not understand the significance of waiving their WARN Act rights.

For these reasons, the releases are unenforceable. DHL violated the WARN Act by failing to provide 60 days’ notice of the mass layoff that effectively occurred when Appellants were forced to resign under the pressure of the severance program. The Court should reverse the district court’s judgment and remand for further proceedings consistent with the statutory protections Congress enacted.
