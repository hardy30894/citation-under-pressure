**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Plain Language of the FDCPA Establishes That a Purchaser of Defaulted Debt Collecting for Its Own Account Is a Creditor, Not a Debt Collector.**

The Fair Debt Collection Practices Act (“FDCPA”) defines a “debt collector” as any person who “regularly collects or attempts to collect, directly or indirectly, debts owed or due or asserted to be owed or due *another*.” 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(6) (emphasis added). The word “another” is the critical term. It limits the definition to persons who collect debts on behalf of a third party. A person who collects debts owed to itself is a “creditor,” not a “debt collector.” *See* 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(4) (defining “creditor” as “any person who offers or extends credit creating a debt or to whom a debt is owed”).

This distinction between collecting for oneself and collecting for another is fundamental. The FDCPA was enacted to regulate third-party debt collectors, not creditors collecting their own debts. *See* S. Rep. No. 95-382, at 2 (1977) (“The primary purpose of this bill is to eliminate abusive debt collection practices by *debt collectors*.”). Congress did not intend to transform every purchaser of debt into a debt collector simply because the debt was in default at the time of purchase. *See id.* at 3-4 (explaining that the definition of “debt collector” covers “third persons who regularly collect debts for others”).

**II. The Default Status of a Debt Is Irrelevant to Whether a Purchaser Is a Debt Collector.**

Petitioners argue that because Santander purchased debts that were already in default, it must be a debt collector. This argument misreads the statute. The FDCPA’s definition of “debt collector” does not mention the default status of a debt. *See* 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(6). The only reference to default appears in an exclusion from the definition, which provides that a person collecting debts “not in default at the time it was obtained” is *not* a debt collector. *Id.* § 1692a(6)(F)(iii). That exclusion confirms that Congress knew how to use default status when it wanted to, but chose not to make it a defining feature of a debt collector.

Petitioners’ reliance on the exclusion in the definition of “creditor” is equally misplaced. That exclusion provides that a person who receives an assignment of a debt “in default solely for the purpose of facilitating collection of such debt *for another*” is not a creditor. 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(4) (emphasis added). Santander did not purchase the debts to facilitate collection for CitiFinancial; it purchased them for its own account. The exclusion therefore does not apply.

**III. Santander Was Collecting Its Own Debts, Not Debts Owed to Another.**

The complaint alleges that Santander purchased the plaintiffs’ loans from CitiFinancial on December 1, 2011. After that date, Santander owned the debts. When Santander attempted to collect from the plaintiffs, it was collecting debts owed to itself. It was therefore acting as a creditor, not a debt collector. *See* 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(4). The FDCPA does not regulate creditors collecting their own debts. *See* 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(6).

Petitioners’ attempt to characterize Santander as a debt collector because it previously serviced the loans for CitiFinancial fails. The relevant conduct is Santander’s collection activity *after* it purchased the debts. At that time, Santander was collecting for itself. The FDCPA does not impose perpetual liability on entities that once acted as debt collectors but later become creditors. *See* 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(6) (defining debt collector based on the nature of the collection activity, not the entity’s history).

**IV. The FDCPA’s Purpose Does Not Support Petitioners’ Interpretation.**

The FDCPA’s purpose is to “eliminate abusive debt collection practices by debt collectors.” 15 U.S.C. § 1692(e). Creditors collecting their own debts are subject to other state and federal laws, including the Truth in Lending Act and state unfair trade practices statutes. Congress did not intend to duplicate those protections by subjecting creditors to the FDCPA. *See* S. Rep. No. 95-382, at 3 (noting that the FDCPA “does not apply to creditors collecting their own debts”).

Petitioners’ interpretation would create a perverse result: any entity that purchases defaulted debt would automatically become a debt collector, even if it collects only for itself. This would sweep thousands of routine commercial transactions into the FDCPA’s regulatory framework, contrary to Congress’s intent. *See* 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(6) (limiting the definition to those who collect debts “owed or due *another*”).

**V. The Fourth Circuit’s Decision Is Consistent with the Statute’s Plain Language.**

The Fourth Circuit correctly held that Santander was not a debt collector because it was collecting debts it owned. The court’s analysis followed the statute’s plain language and structure. *See* 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(6). The court also correctly rejected petitioners’ argument that default status determines whether a purchaser is a debt collector. *See* 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(6)(F)(iii) (using default status only as an exclusion, not a definition).

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Fourth Circuit should be affirmed.
