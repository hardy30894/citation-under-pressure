**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE DELEGATION OF TEMPORARY SCHEDULING AUTHORITY TO THE ATTORNEY GENERAL IS CONSTITUTIONAL BECAUSE CONGRESS PROVIDED AN INTELLIGIBLE PRINCIPLE TO GUIDE THE EXERCISE OF THAT AUTHORITY.**

The Constitution vests “[a]ll legislative Powers herein granted . . . in a Congress of the United States.” U.S. Const. art. I, § 1. While Congress may not delegate its core legislative authority, it may delegate power to executive officials so long as it provides an “intelligible principle” to guide the delegate’s discretion. *Mistretta v. United States*, 488 U.S. 361, 372 (1989) (quoting *J.W. Hampton, Jr., & Co. v. United States*, 276 U.S. 394, 409 (1928)). The Controlled Substances Act (CSA) satisfies this requirement.

The CSA’s temporary scheduling provision, 21 U.S.C. § 811(h), directs the Attorney General to schedule a substance if he finds that doing so is “necessary to avoid an imminent hazard to the public safety.” *Id.* § 811(h)(1). The statute further specifies that the Attorney General must consider three factors: (1) the substance’s history and current pattern of abuse, (2) the scope, duration, and significance of that abuse, and (3) the risk the substance poses to public health. *Id.* § 811(h)(3). These criteria are sufficiently definite to cabin the Attorney General’s discretion. *SeeMistretta*, 488 U.S. at 373 (upholding delegation where Congress provided “considerable specificity” in directing the Sentencing Commission).

The Toubys argue that the delegation is unconstitutional because it empowers the Attorney General to criminalize conduct. *SeeTouby v. United States*, 909 F.2d 712, 720 (3d Cir. 1990) (Sloviter, J., concurring). But the Supreme Court has repeatedly upheld delegations that permit executive officials to define criminal conduct, so long as Congress provides adequate standards. *SeeUnited States v. Grimaud*, 220 U.S. 506, 517 (1911) (upholding delegation to Secretary of Agriculture to regulate grazing on public lands); *Yakus v. United States*, 321 U.S. 414, 426 (1944) (upholding delegation to set price ceilings during wartime). The CSA’s temporary scheduling provision fits squarely within this tradition.

**II. THE ATTORNEY GENERAL’S AUTHORITY TO SUBDELEGATE TEMPORARY SCHEDULING POWER TO THE DEA IS AUTHORIZED BY STATUTE AND CONSTITUTIONAL.**

The CSA grants the Attorney General broad authority to delegate his functions under the statute. 21 U.S.C. § 871(a) provides that the Attorney General “may authorize the performance by any officer, employee, or agency of the Department of Justice of any function of the Attorney General.” The Supreme Court has recognized that executive officials may subdelegate authority where Congress has expressly or implicitly authorized it. *SeeCudahy Packing Co. v. Holland*, 315 U.S. 357, 364 (1942) (upholding subdelegation where statute authorized delegation of inspection duties).

Here, the Attorney General’s subdelegation of temporary scheduling authority to the DEA Administrator is consistent with the CSA’s structure. The DEA is the Department of Justice’s principal agency for enforcing the CSA, and the Attorney General has long delegated scheduling functions to the DEA. *See* 28 C.F.R. § 0.100(b) (1989). The Third Circuit correctly upheld this subdelegation as a permissible exercise of the Attorney General’s discretion. *Touby*, 909 F.2d at 718.

The Toubys contend that the subdelegation is improper because the CSA’s temporary scheduling provision does not expressly mention subdelegation. But the Supreme Court has rejected the argument that a statute must expressly authorize subdelegation to be valid. *SeeCudahy Packing*, 315 U.S. at 364. The CSA’s broad delegation to the Attorney General necessarily includes the authority to subdelegate to subordinate officials within the Department of Justice.

**III. THE STATUTORY BAR ON JUDICIAL REVIEW OF TEMPORARY SCHEDULING ORDERS DOES NOT PRECLUDE CONSTITUTIONAL REVIEW OF THE DELEGATION.**

Section 811(h)(6) of the CSA provides that a temporary scheduling order “is not subject to judicial review.” 21 U.S.C. § 811(h)(6). The Supreme Court has held that such jurisdictional bars do not apply to constitutional challenges. *SeeCalifano v. Sanders*, 430 U.S. 99, 109 (1977) (“[C]onstitutional questions are . . . presumed to be reviewable, and courts will not read a statutory scheme to take the ‘extraordinary’ step of foreclosing jurisdiction unless Congress’ intent to do so is manifested by ‘clear and convincing’ evidence.”). The Third Circuit correctly concluded that the bar on judicial review does not apply to the Toubys’ nondelegation claim. *Touby*, 909 F.2d at 716.

**CONCLUSION**

The delegation of temporary scheduling authority to the Attorney General is constitutional because Congress provided an intelligible principle to guide its exercise. The Attorney General’s subdelegation of that authority to the DEA is authorized by statute and consistent with Supreme Court precedent. The statutory bar on judicial review does not preclude constitutional review of the delegation. The judgment below should be affirmed.
