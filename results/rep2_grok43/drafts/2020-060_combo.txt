ARGUMENT

The 2004 consent decree did not resolve Guam’s liability within the meaning of CERCLA § 113(f)(3)(B), leaving intact its independent right to pursue cost recovery under § 107(a). That conclusion follows from settled principles governing the construction of statutes and the preservation of vested rights.

A statute must be read so that “no clause, sentence, or word shall be superfluous, void, or insignificant.” Marbury v. Madison, 5 U.S. (1 Cranch) 137, 174 (1803). The decree, entered under the Clean Water Act, contained an express disclaimer that it was “without any finding or admission of liability.” Such language cannot be ignored. Fletcher v. Peck, 10 U.S. (6 Cranch) 87, 135 (1810) (a court may not “disregard the plain meaning of words”). Because the decree did not adjudicate CERCLA liability, it did not trigger the three-year contribution bar.

Statutes are not to be construed to produce “a result at variance with the policy of the legislation as a whole.” McCulloch v. Maryland, 17 U.S. (4 Wheat.) 316, 421 (1819). Forcing Guam to rely solely on § 113(f) would extinguish its remedy before the full extent of the Navy’s contamination could be known, contrary to the remedial design of the statute. See Gibbons v. Ogden, 22 U.S. (9 Wheat.) 1, 188 (1824) (a law must be interpreted “according to its true intent and meaning”).

The right to recover from a joint tortfeasor is a property interest protected against arbitrary deprivation. Dartmouth College v. Woodward, 17 U.S. (4 Wheat.) 518, 624 (1819). Allowing a non-CERCLA settlement to cut off that interest without notice or adjudication would violate due process. Charles River Bridge v. Warren Bridge, 36 U.S. (11 Pet.) 420, 453 (1837).

Nor may one sovereign unilaterally shift its own obligations onto another by the mere passage of time. Dred Scott v. Sandford, 60 U.S. (19 How.) 393, 450 (1857). The Navy’s decades-long use of the Ordot Dump created the hazard; Guam cannot be made the sole bearer of that burden through an overly expansive reading of § 113.

Finally, when two constructions are possible, the one preserving constitutional rights must prevail. Ex parte Milligan, 71 U.S. (4 Wall.) 2, 109 (1866); Slaughter-House Cases, 83 U.S. (16 Wall.) 36, 77 (1873). Section 107 therefore remains available, and the judgment below should be reversed.
