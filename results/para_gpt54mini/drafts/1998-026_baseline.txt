**ARGUMENT**

A district court’s failure to advise a defendant of the right to appeal does not, by itself, entitle the defendant to habeas relief when the defendant already knew of that right and chose not to appeal. Rule 32’s notice requirement is designed to ensure that a defendant is informed of appellate rights; where that purpose has already been satisfied, the omission causes no cognizable prejudice and affords no basis to vacate the sentence. See *United States v. Timmreck*, 441 U.S. 780, 783-85 (1979); *Hill v. United States*, 368 U.S. 424, 428-29 (1962).

Rule 32(a)(2) required the sentencing court to advise a defendant who pleaded guilty of any right to appeal. But the Supreme Court has repeatedly held that collateral relief is not available for every technical violation of the criminal rules. In *Hill*, the Court held that a Rule 32 omission did not justify relief under 28 U.S.C. § 2255 absent a “fundamental defect” resulting in a “complete miscarriage of justice.” 368 U.S. at 428. Likewise, in *Timmreck*, the Court refused habeas relief for a Rule 11 error, explaining that “collateral relief is not available when all that is shown is a failure to comply with the formal requirements” of a rule, absent a showing of prejudice or “exceptional circumstances.” 441 U.S. at 785. The same principle applies here. A Rule 32 warning is a procedural safeguard, not a jurisdictional prerequisite to finality. Where the defendant already knew of the right to appeal, the court’s omission did not deprive him of any substantive opportunity to seek review.

That is precisely why the courts below denied relief. The District Court found, after an evidentiary hearing, that Peguero knew of his right to appeal at the time of sentencing. On that finding, the omission did not frustrate the rule’s purpose. The Court of Appeals correctly recognized that any Rule 32 error was harmless because “the rule’s purpose had been served.” That conclusion is consistent with this Court’s repeated insistence that habeas is not a substitute for ordinary appellate review. See *United States v. Frady*, 456 U.S. 152, 165-66 (1982).

Peguero’s contrary rule—automatic vacatur whenever the district court fails to give the Rule 32 advisement—finds no support in this Court’s habeas precedents. The governing question is whether the error worked actual prejudice. In *Bousley v. United States*, 523 U.S. 614, 621-22 (1998), the Court reiterated that collateral relief generally requires a showing that the error had a material effect on the defendant’s ability to pursue review. Where, as here, the defendant was aware of his appellate rights and elected not to appeal, he cannot show that the court’s omission caused the loss of an appeal he otherwise would have taken.

Nor does Rule 32 itself demand the extraordinary remedy Peguero seeks. The Rules are designed to secure fair procedure, not to create automatic collateral relief for every deviation. In *Bank of Nova Scotia v. United States*, 487 U.S. 250, 254-56 (1988), the Court explained that absent a showing of prejudice, violations of criminal procedural rules do not warrant dismissal or reversal. That principle controls here. Peguero identifies no lost issue, no foregone appeal, and no resulting injustice. He simply complains that the judge failed to repeat advice he already had. Habeas relief does not lie for such a harmless omission.

Peguero may argue that the rule’s command is mandatory and that the district court’s failure to comply should itself require resentencing. But this Court has distinguished between a rule’s mandatory language and the availability of collateral relief for its violation. In *Timmreck*, the Court rejected the contention that a formal rule violation automatically entitles a prisoner to § 2255 relief, emphasizing that collateral review is reserved for constitutional or jurisdictional errors, or for exceptional circumstances amounting to a miscarriage of justice. 441 U.S. at 784-85. The same logic forecloses Peguero’s claim. Even if the sentencing judge erred, the error was harmless because it did not affect Peguero’s understanding or decision-making.

Finally, affirmance is consistent with the strong interest in the finality of criminal judgments. *Frady*, 456 U.S. at 166, teaches that “[o]nce the defendant’s chance to appeal has been waived or exhausted, we are entitled to presume he stands fairly and finally convicted.” That presumption would be undermined if a defendant who knew of his appellate rights could obtain habeas relief based solely on the district court’s failure to recite a warning already known to him.

Because Peguero was aware of his right to appeal and chose not to pursue it, the district court’s failure to give the Rule 32 advisement caused no prejudice and no miscarriage of justice. The judgment below should be affirmed.
