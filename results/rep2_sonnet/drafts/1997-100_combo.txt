# ARGUMENT

## I. The Double Jeopardy Clause Bars a Second Attempt to Prove a Fact After a Reviewing Court Has Found the Evidence Insufficient to Sustain That Finding.

The Double Jeopardy Clause has, from its earliest construction, been understood to forbid the State from making repeated attempts to establish a fact necessary to justify a defendant's confinement once a court has determined the State's proof failed. In *Ex parte Lange*, 85 U.S. (18 Wall.) 163 (1873), this Court recognized that the Clause protects against more than a second *punishment*; it forecloses a second *trial* of the same issue once a determination has been reached. That principle finds its clearest expression in *United States v. Ball*, 163 U.S. 662 (1896), where the Court held that "the verdict of acquittal was final, and could not be reviewed, on error or otherwise, without putting him twice in jeopardy, and thereby violating the Constitution." 163 U.S. at 671. The same rule barred appellate review of an acquittal in *Kepner v. United States*, 195 U.S. 100 (1904), and this Court reaffirmed it decades later in the per curiam decision of *Fong Foo v. United States*, 369 U.S. 141 (1962), holding that even a legally erroneous judgment of acquittal is final and unreviewable, because to permit its correction "would result in a defendant being tried twice for the same offense."

That rule does not depend on the label a State attaches to the proceeding; it depends on the function the proceeding serves. When a jury convicts on a lesser charge, this Court has treated the necessarily implied rejection of the greater charge as the functional equivalent of an acquittal, immune from a second attempt at proof. *Green v. United States*, 355 U.S. 184 (1957). In *Green*, this Court explained that "the underlying idea, one that is deeply ingrained in at least the Anglo-American system of jurisprudence, is that the State with all its resources and power should not be allowed to make repeated attempts to convict an individual for an alleged offense, thereby subjecting him to embarrassment, expense and ordeal and compelling him to live in a continuing state of anxiety and insecurity." *Id.* at 187–88. The same logic controls here. The California Court of Appeal held, as a matter of law, that the State's evidence was insufficient to prove the prior-conviction allegation beyond a reasonable doubt. That determination is indistinguishable in substance from the acquittal in *Green* or the acquittals in *Ball*, *Kepner*, and *Fong Foo*: in each case a tribunal concluded the State failed to prove what it was required to prove, and in each case the Constitution forbids giving the State a second chance.

## II. The Proceeding to Prove a Prior Conviction Bears the Hallmarks of a Trial and Therefore Falls Within the Clause's Protection.

Petitioner does not contend that every sentencing determination triggers double jeopardy protection. This Court has long recognized that a sentencing judge may rely on informal information not subject to adversarial testing. *Williams v. New York*, 337 U.S. 241 (1949). But *Williams* rested on the absence of any adjudicative procedure resembling a trial. Where, as here, the State elects to provide a trial-like adjudication—complete with the right to a jury (or its waiver), proof beyond a reasonable doubt, application of the rules of evidence, and the right of confrontation and cross-examination—the constitutional calculus changes. This Court held in *Specht v. Patterson*, 386 U.S. 605 (1967), that where a proceeding requires "a new finding of fact" that was "not an ingredient of the offense charged," due process demands that the defendant "be confronted with witnesses against him [and] to cross-examine" them. *Id.* at 610. California's own procedure for proving a prior-conviction allegation supplies exactly these safeguards. A proceeding so thoroughly modeled on a criminal trial cannot be stripped of the trial's most fundamental protection—finality of an adverse evidentiary ruling.

Nor does *Stroud v. United States*, 251 U.S. 15 (1919), compel a different result. *Stroud* permitted a harsher sentence on retrial only because the original judgment was reversed for *trial error*, not because the evidence was held legally insufficient. That distinction is decisive: reversal for trial error leaves open the possibility that the State's proof was adequate all along, whereas a finding of evidentiary insufficiency conclusively establishes that the State's case failed on the merits—precisely the circumstance in which *Ball*, *Kepner*, and *Fong Foo* bar a second attempt.

## III. Barring Retrial Here Vindicates the Core Purposes of the Double Jeopardy Clause.

This Court has repeatedly explained that the Clause "protects against a second prosecution for the same offense after acquittal." *North Carolina v. Pearce*, 395 U.S. 711, 717 (1969). That guarantee is "fundamental to the American scheme of justice" and binds the States through the Fourteenth Amendment. *Benton v. Maryland*, 395 U.S. 784, 796 (1969). Even in the context of successive prosecutions by different sovereigns, this Court in *Bartkus v. Illinois*, 359 U.S. 121 (1959), took care to preserve the principle that a single sovereign may not repeatedly hale a defendant into court to relitigate a factual issue already resolved against it. California's three-strikes enhancement proceeding, once concluded in petitioner's favor by operation of an appellate insufficiency ruling, is such an issue. To permit retrial would subject petitioner to precisely the "embarrassment, expense and ordeal" the Clause was designed to prevent. *Green*, 355 U.S. at 187–88. The judgment of the California Supreme Court should be reversed.
