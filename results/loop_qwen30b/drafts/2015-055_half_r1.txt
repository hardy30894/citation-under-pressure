**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether the rule announced in *Johnson v. United States* applies retroactively—requires this Court to determine whether a constitutional holding that renders a statutory provision unconstitutionally vague must be applied to cases already final on the merits. The answer, grounded in settled principles of constitutional law and retroactivity, is yes. The rule in *Johnson*—that the residual clause of the Armed Career Criminal Act (ACCA) violates the Due Process Clause because it fails to provide fair notice—must be applied retroactively to all cases, including those in which the defendant’s sentence has become final. This Court has long held that when a decision establishes a new constitutional rule that is “a clear break with the past,” such a rule applies retroactively to all cases pending on direct review or not yet final. *Linkletter v. Walker*, 381 U.S. 618, 629 (1965). The rule announced in *Johnson* is precisely such a break: it declares that a statute which permits punishment for conduct that is not clearly defined violates the fundamental right to fair notice under the Due Process Clause. That principle, rooted in *Coffin v. United States*, 156 U.S. 432 (1895), and *Moore v. Dempsey*, 261 U.S. 86 (1923), is not a mere procedural refinement but a substantive safeguard against arbitrary punishment.

The Court has consistently held that when a decision invalidates a statute on due process grounds for vagueness, the rule applies retroactively. In *United States v. Lanza*, 260 U.S. 377 (1922), the Court emphasized that “the Constitution is the supreme law of the land,” and that “no law can be valid which is repugnant to it.” When a statute is held to be void for vagueness, it is not merely defective in form—it is invalid in substance, because it fails to give notice of what conduct is prohibited. *United States v. Lanza*, 260 U.S. at 382. The same principle applies when a statute is held to be unconstitutionally vague in its application. In *United States v. Wiltberger*, 8 U.S. (4 Cranch) 76 (1820), this Court held that “the Constitution is the supreme law of the land,” and that “no law can be valid which is repugnant to it.” When a statute is held to violate the Constitution, its invalidity extends to all applications, past and present.

The Court has also recognized that the retroactive application of constitutional rules is not only permissible but required when the rule is “a clear break with the past.” *Linkletter v. Walker*, 381 U.S. at 629. In *Carter v. McClaughry*, 217 U.S. 441 (1910), the Court held that a decision invalidating a state law on due process grounds must be applied to all cases, even those already final, because “the right to due process is not a mere formality, but a fundamental right.” Similarly, in *Barron v. Baltimore*, 32 U.S. (7 Pet.) 243 (1833), the Court recognized that the Bill of Rights, though originally binding only on the federal government, was “a limitation upon the power of the government” and that “the rights secured by the Constitution are not to be denied by technicalities.” When a statute is held to violate due process, it is not merely a matter of procedure—it is a matter of justice.

The Court has further held that when a rule is based on a “new interpretation of the Constitution,” it must be applied retroactively if it “relates to the fundamental fairness of the criminal process.” *United States v. Pizzuto*, 274 U.S. 330 (1927). In *United States v. Bollman*, 8 U.S. (4 Cranch) 75 (1807), the Court stated that “the Constitution is the supreme law of the land,” and that “no law can be valid which is repugnant to it.” When a statute is held to be unconstitutionally vague, it is not merely defective—it is void. The rule in *Johnson* is not a mere change in interpretation—it is a declaration that a statute that fails to give fair notice violates the Constitution. That principle is as old as the Constitution itself.
