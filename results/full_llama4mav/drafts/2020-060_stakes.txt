The United States respectfully submits that the Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit correctly held that Guam's claim against the United States is time-barred under the Comprehensive Environmental Response, Compensation, and Liability Act (CERCLA). The plain language of CERCLA § 113(f)(3)(B) dictates that a potentially responsible party (PRP) that has resolved its liability to the United States or a State in an administrative or judicially approved settlement may seek contribution from other PRPs. 42 U.S.C. § 9613(f)(3)(B).

The 2004 Consent Decree between Guam and the EPA constitutes a qualifying settlement under § 113(f)(3)(B). The Decree required Guam to undertake a "response action" within the meaning of CERCLA § 101(25), 42 U.S.C. § 9601(25), by closing the Ordot Dump and designing and installing a cover system. Consent Decree 5-12, J.A. 94-101. By agreeing to these terms, Guam "resolved [its] liability" to the United States for the response action, thereby triggering its right to seek contribution under § 113(f)(3)(B). See Cooper Industries, Inc. v. Aviall Services, Inc., 543 U.S. 157, 167 (2004) (explaining that § 113(f)(3)(B) "provide[s] a PRP with a cause of action for contribution" upon settlement with the United States or a State).

Guam's argument that the Consent Decree did not resolve its liability due to the inclusion of a disclaimer of liability is unavailing. As the Sixth Circuit has observed, "parties often expressly refuse to concede liability under a settlement agreement, even while assuming obligations consistent with a finding of liability." Florida Power Corp. v. FirstEnergy Corp., 810 F.3d 996, 1002 (6th Cir. 2015). The substantive provisions of the Consent Decree, not the disclaimer, control the analysis. See Refined Metals Corp. v. NL Industries, 937 F.3d 928, 931 (7th Cir. 2019) (holding that a consent decree triggered § 113(f)(3)(B) despite a disclaimer of liability).

Furthermore, Guam's contention that the Consent Decree falls outside CERCLA's provisions because it addressed Clean Water Act violations is incorrect. The Complaint demanded that Guam "complete construction of [a] cover system to eliminate discharges of untreated leachate," CWA Compl. ¶ 29, which is a "remedial action" under CERCLA § 101(24), 42 U.S.C. § 9601(24).

The Court of Appeals correctly applied the plain language of CERCLA and relevant case law to conclude that Guam's claim is time-barred. Guam's failure to bring its contribution claim within the three-year statute of limitations under § 113(g)(3) bars its action. 42 U.S.C. § 9613(g)(3). The Court should affirm the judgment of the Court of Appeals.
