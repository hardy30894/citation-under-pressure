ARGUMENT

I. The Constitutional Test for Title Navigability Requires a Segment-Specific Inquiry Fixed at Statehood, and the Montana Supreme Court Correctly Applied It

The equal-footing doctrine holds that when a State enters the Union, title to the beds of its navigable waters passes to the State as an incident of sovereignty. See United States v. Chicago, Milwaukee, St. Paul & Pac. R.R. Co., 312 U.S. 592 (1941). Because that title vests at the moment of admission, this Court has long recognized that "the rights of the state and of riparian owners in navigable waters within the state and to the soil beneath are matters" fixed by the condition of the waterway at that historical moment, not by later developments. Fox River Paper Co. v. Railroad Commission, 274 U.S. 651, 655 (1927) (citing Barney v. Keokuk, 94 U.S. 324, 338 (1876)); see also Packer v. Bird, 137 U.S. 661 (1891); Hardin v. Jordan, 140 U.S. 371 (1891). The District Court's approach—asking whether each disputed stretch of the Missouri, Madison, and Clark Fork Rivers was navigable in 1889—was faithful to this settled framework, and PPL identifies no authority for displacing it.

Equally well established is that navigability for title purposes must be assessed reach by reach, not by treating an entire river system as a single undifferentiated unit. In United States v. Utah, 283 U.S. 801 (1931), this Court's decree separately adjudicated the navigability of discrete stretches of the Colorado and San Juan Rivers, declaring some reaches navigable and others "not . . . navigable," with title to each stretch determined independently. Id. That segment-specific methodology reflects the reality that a single river may present dramatically different physical conditions—rapids, falls, shallows—along its course, conditions that bear directly on whether a given stretch was, at the relevant time, "used, or susceptible of being used" as a highway of commerce. The same segment-by-segment logic underlies Federal Power Commission v. Union Electric Co., 381 U.S. 90 (1965), where the Court examined the East Fork of the Black River as a discrete waterway distinct from the Black and White Rivers into which it flowed, assessing its own capacity to affect commerce rather than imputing the character of the larger system to every tributary reach. And in Grand River Dam Authority v. Grand-Hydro, 335 U.S. 359 (1949), the Court likewise treated the Grand River, "a nonnavigable stream, tributary to the Arkansas River which is a navigable stream," id. at 359, as an independent unit for purposes of state licensing and hydroelectric development, refusing to conflate its character with that of the navigable river it fed.

The District Court's findings followed precisely this course. It examined the physical and commercial history of each of the specific stretches at issue—the reaches beneath PPL's dams—rather than issuing a single, undifferentiated pronouncement about the Missouri, Madison, and Clark Fork Rivers as a whole. That is exactly what the constitutional test, as illuminated by Utah, Union Electric, and Grand-Hydro, demands.

II. Because Title to the Navigable Stretches Vested in Montana at Statehood, PPL Must Compensate the State for Its Use of State-Owned Riverbeds

Once it is established that a stretch of riverbed passed to the State under the equal-footing doctrine, the State's proprietary interest in that land—and its right to seek compensation for its use—follows as a matter of course. This Court has repeatedly recognized that the beds of navigable waters, and the value inherent in the flow of water across them, belong to the public rather than to private parties who happen to exploit them. In United States v. Twin City Power Co., 350 U.S. 222 (1956), the Court held that a riparian owner could not claim compensation for "the value of the land as a site for hydroelectric power operations" on a navigable stream, because that value derived from the public's right to control the flow of navigable water, not from any private entitlement. Id. at 224. The corollary is equally true here: a private licensee that occupies and exploits state-owned riverbed to generate hydroelectric power for profit must compensate the sovereign titleholder for that use. See also United States v. Rands, 389 U.S. 121 (1967) (reaffirming that the servitude over navigable waters reflects the public, not private, character of the interests at stake).

PPL's predecessor never paid such compensation, notwithstanding decades of commercial use of state-owned riverbed. The Hydroelectric Resources Act, Title 77, chapter 4, part 2, MCA, simply operationalizes the State's proprietary title by requiring lease payments for use of "power sites" on state lands—precisely the state-law property right that federal courts have recognized survives Federal Power Act licensing. See Jordan v. Randolph Mills, Inc., 716 F.2d 1053, 1055 (4th Cir. 1983) (a federal license "neither transfers nor diminishes any right of possession or enjoyment possessed by" the landowner). Nothing in this Court's navigability-for-title jurisprudence—from Utah's segment-specific decree to Grand-Hydro's stream-by-stream analysis—suggests that a State's title, once vested at statehood in a navigable reach, can be defeated or rendered uncompensated merely because a private licensee later obtains federal authorization to build a dam upon it.

For these reasons, the judgment below, which correctly applied the constitutional segment-specific, statehood-fixed test for title navigability and correctly required compensation for use of state-owned riverbed, should be affirmed.
