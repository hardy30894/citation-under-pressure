ARGUMENT

I. Section 1338(a) Does Not Confer Exclusive Jurisdiction Over a State Legal-Malpractice Claim That Merely Requires Resolution of a Patent-Law Issue.

Congress has granted federal courts exclusive jurisdiction over civil actions “arising under any Act of Congress relating to patents.” 28 U.S.C. § 1338(a). The phrase “arising under” carries the same meaning in § 1338(a) as in the general federal-question statute, 28 U.S.C. § 1331. Christianson v. Colt Indus. Operating Corp., 486 U.S. 800, 808-09 (1988). A state-law claim therefore falls within § 1338(a) only when “a well-pleaded complaint establishes either that federal patent law creates the cause of action or that the plaintiff’s right to relief necessarily depends on resolution of a substantial question of federal patent law.” Id. at 809.

Minton’s malpractice claim is created by Texas common law. See Alexander v. Turtur & Assocs., 146 S.W.3d 113, 117 (Tex. 2004). Jurisdiction therefore turns on whether the claim “necessarily raise[s] a stated federal issue, actually disputed and substantial, which a federal forum may entertain without disturbing any congressionally approved balance of federal and state judicial responsibilities.” Grable & Sons Metal Prods., Inc. v. Darue Eng’g & Mfg., 545 U.S. 308, 314 (2005). The claim fails at least the third and fourth elements of that test.

A. The embedded patent issue is not “substantial” within the meaning of Grable.

The experimental-use exception to the on-sale bar, 35 U.S.C. § 102(b), is undoubtedly contested in this malpractice action. But Grable requires more than a contested federal question; the question must be “substantial” in the sense that its resolution would be “significant to the federal system as a whole.” Gunn v. Minton, 568 U.S. 251, 260 (2013). A state-court adjudication of the exception in the context of a hypothetical patent trial would not alter patent rights, affect the validity of any patent, or control the outcome of any future infringement suit. See Empire Healthchoice Assurance, Inc. v. McVeigh, 547 U.S. 677, 701 (2006) (no federal jurisdiction where the federal issue “would not be controlling in numerous other cases”). The Federal Circuit’s contrary view in Air Measurement Technologies, Inc. v. Akin Gump Strauss Hauer & Feld, L.L.P., 504 F.3d 1262, 1272 (Fed. Cir. 2007), rests on an overly expansive reading of “substantial” that Grable rejected.

B. Federal jurisdiction would upset the congressionally approved balance between state and federal courts.

Even if the patent issue were substantial, exercising exclusive federal jurisdiction would “disturb[] the balance of federal and state judicial responsibilities.” Grable, 545 U.S. at 314. Legal-malpractice actions have traditionally been the province of state courts. See, e.g., Singh v. Duane Morris LLP, 538 F.3d 334, 339 (5th Cir. 2008). Allowing every malpractice suit that touches on patent litigation to migrate to federal court would “herald a potentially enormous shift of traditionally state cases into federal courts.” Grable, 545 U.S. at 319. Congress has given no indication that it intended such a shift when it enacted § 1338(a). See also Merrell Dow Pharm. Inc. v. Thompson, 478 U.S. 804, 811-12 (1986).

II. The Texas Supreme Court Erred in Holding That Exclusive Federal Jurisdiction Exists.

The court below concluded that the patent issue was “necessary, disputed, and substantial” and that federal jurisdiction would not upset the federal-state balance. That conclusion misapplies Grable. Because Minton’s claim does not satisfy the Grable test, the state courts retain jurisdiction. The judgment of the Texas Supreme Court should be reversed.
