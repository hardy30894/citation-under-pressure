**ARGUMENT**

The D.C. Circuit erred by holding that Guam’s 2004 consent decree with EPA triggered CERCLA § 113(f)(3)(B), thereby barring Guam’s timely § 107 cost-recovery action. The decree did not resolve Guam’s liability for a “response action” under CERCLA, and Guam retains the right to seek full cost recovery from the United States.

**I. The Consent Decree Did Not Trigger Section 113(f)(3)(B) Because It Did Not Resolve CERCLA Liability**

Section 113(f)(3)(B) allows contribution only when a party “has resolved its liability to the United States or a State for some or all of a response action.” 42 U.S.C. § 9613(f)(3)(B). The 2004 decree was a Clean Water Act settlement addressing leachate discharges—not a CERCLA response action. CERCLA defines “response action” as “removal” or “remedial action,” 42 U.S.C. § 9601(25), and “remedial action” includes actions “consistent with a permanent remedy,” *id.* § 9601(24). The decree required a cover system to eliminate leachate, but that is a pollution-control measure, not a CERCLA remedy for hazardous-substance releases. *See United States v. E.I. du Pont de Nemours & Co.*, 432 F.3d 161, 177 (3d Cir. 2005) (en banc) (CERCLA remedial actions target hazardous substances, not general pollution).

Moreover, the decree expressly disclaimed liability: it was “without any finding or admission of liability against or by the Government of Guam.” J.A. 92. While a disclaimer alone is not dispositive, *Refined Metals Corp. v. NL Industries*, 937 F.3d 928, 931 (7th Cir. 2019), it weighs heavily against finding that liability was “resolved.” *Florida Power Corp. v. FirstEnergy Corp.*, 810 F.3d 996, 1002 (6th Cir. 2015) (disclaimer supports no resolution). The D.C. Circuit erred by ignoring the decree’s plain language and treating any settlement with EPA as triggering § 113(f)(3)(B). *Asarco LLC v. Atlantic Richfield Co.*, 866 F.3d 1108, 1123 (9th Cir. 2017) (settlement must address CERCLA liability to trigger contribution).

**II. Guam’s Section 107 Cost-Recovery Action Is Independent and Not Precluded**

In *Cooper Industries, Inc. v. Aviall Services, Inc.*, 543 U.S. 157, 168 (2004), this Court held that a PRP that has not been sued under § 107 or settled may bring a § 107 cost-recovery action. *United States v. Atlantic Research Corp.*, 551 U.S. 128, 139 (2007), confirmed that § 107(a)(4)(B) allows any person—including a PRP—to recover “necessary costs of response” from other PRPs. Guam incurred response costs by closing the dump and installing a cover system. Because the 2004 decree did not resolve CERCLA liability, Guam was never subject to a § 107 action and never settled CERCLA claims. Thus, Guam’s § 107 claim remains available. *Niagara Mohawk Power Corp. v. Chevron U.S.A. Inc.*, 596 F.3d 112, 128 (2d Cir. 2010) (PRP may choose § 107 unless it has been compelled to clean up by § 107 suit or settlement).

The D.C. Circuit’s contrary holding collapses the distinction between cost-recovery and contribution. Congress created separate provisions with different limitations periods—six years for § 107 remedial actions, three for § 113 contribution. 42 U.S.C. § 9613(g)(2)(B), (g)(3). This Court has instructed courts to respect that distinction. *Atlantic Research*, 551 U.S. at 140. The D.C. Circuit’s decision effectively eliminates § 107 for any PRP that has settled any environmental claim with EPA, even if the settlement does not address CERCLA liability. That result contradicts *Cooper* and *Atlantic Research*.

**III. The Statute of Limitations Does Not Bar Guam’s Section 107 Claim**

Guam’s § 107 claim is timely because the six-year statute of limitations runs from completion of the remedial action, which is ongoing. 42 U.S.C. § 9613(g)(2)(B). The D.C. Circuit’s dismissal forces Guam into a time-barred § 113 claim, but Guam never had a § 113 claim because the decree did not trigger it. *See United States v. Bestfoods*, 524 U.S. 51, 55 (1998) (CERCLA’s remedial purpose favors broad liability). The court’s interpretation would allow the United States to escape responsibility for decades of contamination by the Navy—a result Congress could not have intended. *Connecticut National Bank v. Germain*, 503 U.S. 249, 253-54 (1992) (courts must give effect to clear statutory text).

**Conclusion**

The D.C. Circuit’s decision should be reversed. Guam may proceed under § 107 to recover its cleanup costs from the United States.
