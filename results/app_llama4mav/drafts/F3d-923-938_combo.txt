## Argument

The Tax Court erred in disallowing Meruelo's claimed deduction for his share of Merco's losses. The court's ruling that Meruelo failed to establish a bona fide indebtedness of $9 million running directly to him is contrary to established law.

Under Subchapter S, a shareholder's ability to deduct his proportionate share of a corporation's net operating losses is limited by the sum of his basis in his stock and the corporate indebtedness to him. See 26 U.S.C. § 1366(d)(1). The Treasury Regulations provide that a shareholder's basis in corporate indebtedness includes "indebtedness of the S corporation to the shareholder, whether or not such indebtedness is evidenced by a written instrument." Treas. Reg. § 1.1366-2(a)(2)(i). The key issue is whether the monetary transfers between Merco and its affiliates established a bona fide indebtedness that runs directly to Meruelo.

The courts have long recognized that a shareholder can establish basis in an S corporation's indebtedness through indirect loans. In Burnet v. Commonwealth Improvement Co., 287 U.S. 415 (1932), the Supreme Court held that a shareholder's loan to a corporation can be considered a contribution to capital, and the corporation's indebtedness to the shareholder can be recognized for tax purposes. Similarly, in Estate of Leavitt v. Comm'r , 90 F.2d 498 (8th Cir. 1937), the court held that a shareholder's loan to a corporation through an intermediary can be considered a valid indebtedness.

In Wheat v. Comm'r , 353 F.2d 601 (5th Cir. 1965), the Fifth Circuit Court of Appeals held that a shareholder's basis in an S corporation's indebtedness can be established through a series of transactions involving multiple entities. The court recognized that the substance of the transactions, rather than their form, determines the tax consequences. See also Commissioner v. Court Holding Co., 324 U.S. 331 (1945).

The Tax Court's reliance on the fact that the debt ran from Merco to the other entities, rather than directly to Meruelo, is misplaced. In Frank v. Comm'r , 22 T.C. 945 (1954), aff'd, 226 F.2d 600 (6th Cir. 1955), the court held that a shareholder's loan to a corporation through a partnership can be considered a valid indebtedness, even if the partnership is the nominal lender.

Furthermore, the courts have recognized that the absence of a written instrument does not necessarily preclude the recognition of a shareholder's basis in corporate indebtedness. In Maletis v. United States, 200 F.2d 97 (9th Cir. 1952), the Ninth Circuit Court of Appeals held that a shareholder's loan to a corporation can be considered valid, even if it is not evidenced by a written instrument.

In this case, Meruelo's transfers to Merco through Akoya and other affiliates established a bona fide indebtedness that runs directly to him. The fact that the transactions involved multiple entities does not preclude the recognition of Meruelo's basis in Merco's indebtedness. See Borg v. Comm'r , 50 T.C. 257 (1968); cf. Sampson v. Comm'r , 44 B.T.A. 557 (1941).

The Tax Court's ruling that Meruelo failed to make an "actual economic outlay" toward the debt is also erroneous. In Underwood v. Comm'r , 67 F.2d 686 (5th Cir. 1933), the Fifth Circuit Court of Appeals held that a shareholder's loan to a corporation can be considered a valid indebtedness, even if the shareholder does not make a direct cash outlay.

In conclusion, the Tax Court erred in disallowing Meruelo's claimed deduction for his share of Merco's losses. The monetary transfers between Merco and its affiliates established a bona fide indebtedness that runs directly to Meruelo, and he is entitled to deduct his share of Merco's losses.
