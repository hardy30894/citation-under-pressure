**ARGUMENT**

**THE EIGHTH AMENDMENT DOES NOT CATEGORICALLY PROHIBIT THE EXECUTION OF MENTALLY RETARDED OFFENDERS.**

**A. This Court’s Precedent in *Penry v. Lynaugh* Controls and Compels Affirmance.**

In *Penry v. Lynaugh*, 492 U.S. 302 (1989), this Court squarely held that the Eighth Amendment does not categorically bar the execution of mentally retarded persons. *Id.* at 340. The Court reasoned that while mental retardation is a mitigating factor that must be considered in sentencing, it does not automatically render a defendant ineligible for the death penalty. *Id.* at 338. The Virginia Supreme Court correctly applied *Penry* in rejecting Atkins’ claim. *Atkins v. Commonwealth*, 534 S.E.2d 312, 321 (Va. 2000).

*Penry* remains good law. The Court there examined the same evolving-standards argument Atkins now presses and concluded that “there is insufficient evidence of a national consensus against executing mentally retarded people.” *Penry*, 492 U.S. at 335. Nothing has changed to warrant overruling that determination. Only two states—Georgia and Maryland—had enacted statutes barring execution of the mentally retarded at the time of *Penry*. *Id.* at 334. Since then, a handful of additional states have joined them, but the number remains far from the “objective indicia” this Court has required to establish a national consensus. *See Stanford v. Kentucky*, 492 U.S. 361, 370-71 (1989) (plurality opinion) (rejecting claim that execution of juveniles violated Eighth Amendment where only 15 states barred the practice). The continued existence of capital punishment for mentally retarded offenders in the majority of jurisdictions that authorize the death penalty demonstrates that no such consensus has emerged.

**B. Mental Retardation Does Not Automatically Diminish Culpability to the Level Required for Categorical Exemption.**

The Eighth Amendment prohibits punishments that are “excessive” because they are disproportionate to the offender’s culpability. *Coker v. Georgia*, 433 U.S. 584, 592 (1977) (plurality opinion). This Court has reserved categorical exemptions for offenses or offenders that “involve conduct that is particularly less reprehensible” than the typical capital crime. *Kennedy v. Louisiana*, 554 U.S. 407, 420 (2008). For example, the Court has barred execution for rape of an adult woman, *Coker*, 433 U.S. at 592, and for felony murder where the defendant did not kill or intend to kill, *Emmund v. Florida*, 458 U.S. 782, 797 (1982).

Mental retardation, however, is not a fixed category that uniformly reduces culpability. As *Penry* recognized, “mental retardation is a condition, not a specific disability,” and its severity varies widely. 492 U.S. at 338. Some mentally retarded individuals possess sufficient cognitive capacity to understand the wrongfulness of their actions and to conform their conduct to the law. *Id.* at 339. The individualized sentencing process—which already requires consideration of mitigating evidence, including mental retardation—adequately ensures that only those who deserve the ultimate punishment receive it. *See Lockett v. Ohio*, 438 U.S. 586, 604 (1978) (plurality opinion).

**C. Atkins’ Individual Culpability Supports the Death Sentence.**

The jury heard extensive evidence regarding Atkins’ mental capacity and nonetheless concluded that death was the appropriate sentence. The defense’s own expert testified that Atkins was “mildly” mentally retarded, with an IQ of 59. *Atkins*, 534 S.E.2d at 316. But the jury also heard evidence of Atkins’ violent conduct: he abducted Eric Nesbitt at gunpoint, forced him to withdraw money from an ATM, and then shot him eight times. *Id.* at 314-15. The jury was entitled to weigh Atkins’ mental limitations against the brutality of his crimes and to determine that the aggravating circumstances outweighed the mitigating ones. *See Penry*, 492 U.S. at 328 (requiring jury to consider mitigating evidence but not mandating a particular outcome).

**CONCLUSION**

The Eighth Amendment does not categorically prohibit the execution of mentally retarded offenders. *Penry v. Lynaugh* controls, and the judgment of the Virginia Supreme Court should be affirmed.
