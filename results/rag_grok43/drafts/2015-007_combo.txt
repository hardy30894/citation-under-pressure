The Sixth Amendment’s jury-trial guarantee does not extend to every factual determination bearing on capital punishment. As this Court has long recognized, “a sentencing judge . . . is not confined to the narrow issue of guilt.” Williams v. New York, 337 U.S. 241, 246 (1949). The task of selecting punishment “within fixed statutory or constitutional limits” remains distinct from the adjudication of guilt, id. at 247, and the Constitution permits the judge to consider “the fullest information possible concerning the defendant’s life and characteristics,” id. at 247.

Florida’s scheme respects that distinction. The jury’s role ends with its advisory recommendation; the trial court then determines the sentence after an independent review of aggravating and mitigating circumstances. That allocation accords with historical practice. See Andres v. United States, 333 U.S. 740, 752 (1948) (noting that some jurisdictions left the choice between death and life imprisonment to judicial discretion after a guilty verdict). It likewise comports with the principle that “the punishment should fit the offender and not merely the crime.” Williams, 337 U.S. at 247.

The same reasoning governs claims that mental-retardation evidence must be submitted to the jury as an absolute bar. Precedent establishes that evidence of mental condition is relevant to the “appropriate penalty,” not to the issue of guilt. United States ex rel. Smith v. Baldi, 344 U.S. 561, 568 (1953). A State may therefore channel such evidence into the sentencing phase without violating the Sixth Amendment. See Williams v. Oklahoma, 358 U.S. 576, 584-85 (1959) (approving consideration of prior criminal record and other facts at sentencing even though they had been the subject of a separate prosecution).

Nor does the Eighth Amendment demand jury unanimity. This Court has repeatedly upheld capital-sentencing procedures that leave the final choice to the court. See Wilkerson v. Utah, 99 U.S. 130, 134-35 (1879) (affirming a death sentence imposed by the court under a statute permitting the jury only to recommend life imprisonment); McElvaine v. Brush, 142 U.S. 155, 159 (1891) (same). The Eighth Amendment inquiry focuses on whether the punishment itself is cruel and unusual, not on the precise division of labor between judge and jury. Winston v. United States, 172 U.S. 303, 313 (1899).

Petitioner’s reliance on the right to a representative jury is likewise misplaced. Witherspoon v. Illinois, 391 U.S. 510, 519-20 (1968), protects the venire from systematic exclusion of those opposed to capital punishment, but it does not dictate the number of jurors who must concur in a sentencing recommendation. Sigler v. Losieau, 396 U.S. 988 (1969) (denying certiorari in a case upholding a non-unanimous enhancement finding). United States v. Jackson, 390 U.S. 570, 581 (1968), confirms that the Constitution does not require every procedural safeguard available at trial to be replicated at sentencing.

Because Florida’s capital-sentencing statute assigns to the jury only those functions historically entrusted to it and reserves to the judge the ultimate sentencing determination, the scheme satisfies both the Sixth and Eighth Amendments.
