I. The ADEA Does Not Apply to Appointed State Judges.

The ADEA excludes from its definition of “employee” any “appointee on the policymaking level.” 29 U.S.C. § 630(f). Missouri judges appointed under the Non-Partisan Court Plan fall squarely within this exception. Policymaking “is indisputably a part of the function of judging to the extent that judging involves lawmaking to fill the interstices of authority found in constitutions, statutes, and precedents.” EEOC v. Massachusetts, 858 F.2d 52, 55 (1st Cir. 1988). Judges exercise “the same sort of discretion in decisionmaking” and “thoughtful judgment” required of other high-level appointees. Id. Missouri’s Supreme Court, chief judges, and presiding circuit judges further exercise “considerable policymaking responsibility in the supervision of the Missouri court system.” Id. at 56 (citing Mo. Const. art. V, § 4).

Congress did not manifest a clear intent to intrude upon this core state function. “In traditionally sensitive areas, such as legislation affecting the federal balance, the requirement of clear statement assures that the legislature has in fact faced, and intended to bring into issue, the critical matters involved.” United States v. Bass, 404 U.S. 336, 349 (1971). Absent such an unequivocal statement, the ADEA does not displace Missouri’s constitutional retirement rule. See also Orzel v. City of Wauwatosa Fire Dep’t, 697 F.2d 743, 752 (7th Cir. 1983) (recognizing supremacy principles but requiring clear congressional intent in sensitive domains).

II. The Mandatory Retirement Provision Satisfies Equal Protection.

Age is not a suspect classification, so rational-basis review applies. Massachusetts Bd. of Retirement v. Murgia, 427 U.S. 307, 313-14 (1976) (per curiam). Under that standard, the provision need only be “rationally related to a legitimate state interest.” Id. at 314. Missouri’s rule advances several legitimate objectives: it “eliminates the unpleasantness of selectively removing aged . . . judges,” Malmed v. Thornburgh, 621 F.2d 565, 572 (3d Cir. 1980); reduces delays from death or disability that increase with age, Trafelet v. Thompson, 594 F.2d 623, 629 (7th Cir. 1979); and “makes it possible to increase the proportion of minority group members and women in the judiciary,” Apkin v. Treasurer & Receiver Gen., 401 Mass. 427, 517 N.E.2d 141, 146 (1988). A classification survives rational-basis scrutiny even if “it is not made with mathematical nicety or because it results in some inequality.” San Antonio Indep. Sch. Dist. v. Rodriguez, 411 U.S. 1, 42 (1973) (quoting Dandridge v. Williams, 397 U.S. 471, 485 (1970)). Because Missouri’s rule rests on plausible, legitimate grounds, it does not violate the Equal Protection Clause.
