### Argument

The Constitution does not permit a separate, heightened “reasoned choice” standard for determining whether a defendant is competent to plead guilty or waive counsel. The Due Process Clause requires only that a defendant possess the minimum level of competence demanded by **Dusky v. United States, 362 U.S. 402 (1960) (per curiam)**: “sufficient present ability to consult with his lawyer with a reasonable degree of rational understanding” and “a rational as well as factual understanding of the proceedings against him.” **Drope v. Missouri, 420 U.S. 162, 171-72 (1975)**; **Dusky, 362 U.S. at 402**. This Court has repeatedly treated that standard as the constitutional measure of competence, not a more exacting test keyed to the gravity of the decision. See **Pate v. Robinson, 383 U.S. 375, 385-86 (1966)**; **Drope, 420 U.S. at 171-72**.

Moran’s counsel may argue that because waiving counsel and pleading guilty forfeit fundamental rights, the Constitution requires more than competency to stand trial. But this Court’s precedents distinguish between the competency required to make a decision and the separate requirement that the waiver be knowing and voluntary. In **Godinez v. Moran, 509 U.S. 389 (1993)**, the Court rejected the Ninth Circuit’s “reasoned choice” formulation and held that “the standard for competence to plead guilty or to waive the right to counsel is the competence to stand trial.” **Id. at 398**. The Court explained that the issue of competence asks whether the defendant has the capacity to make the choice, while the validity of the waiver turns on whether the choice was knowing and voluntary. **Id. at 400-01**. That reasoning controls here.

The Ninth Circuit’s approach wrongly conflated competence with wisdom. A defendant may make a bad, impulsive, or self-destructive decision and still be competent under the Fourteenth Amendment. **Godinez, 509 U.S. at 400**. The Constitution does not require courts to assess whether the defendant is making the best choice, only whether he is capable of understanding the proceedings and assisting counsel. **Dusky, 362 U.S. at 402**; **Drope, 420 U.S. at 171-72**. As the Court noted in **Godinez**, the “reasoned choice” test is not found in the Due Process Clause and would improperly “impose a higher standard of competence for pleading guilty and waiving the right to counsel than for standing trial.” **509 U.S. at 399**.

Nor does **Westbrook v. Arizona, 384 U.S. 150 (1966) (per curiam)** support the Ninth Circuit’s rule. **Westbrook** held only that the trial court could not assume that a prior determination of competence to stand trial automatically established competence to waive counsel. **Id. at 150-51**. It did not create a new constitutional standard of competence; rather, it required the court to make an informed inquiry into waiver. Likewise, **Johnson v. Zerbst, 304 U.S. 458, 464 (1938)** requires that a waiver of counsel be “an intentional relinquishment or abandonment of a known right or privilege,” but that speaks to the knowing and voluntary character of the waiver, not to a heightened mental-competence threshold. See also **Iowa v. Tovar, 541 U.S. 77, 88-89 (2004)**.

The Ninth Circuit also erred in treating Moran’s medication, suicide attempt, and desire to foreclose mitigating evidence as proof of incompetence. Those facts may have required careful inquiry at the plea colloquy, see **Pate, 383 U.S. at 385-86**; **Drope, 420 U.S. at 179-80**, but they do not establish a distinct “reasoned choice” standard. Competence remains a threshold question of capacity, not a retrospective evaluation of the quality of the defendant’s decision. **Godinez, 509 U.S. at 401 n.12**.

Finally, the state court’s factual findings and plea colloquy support the conclusion that Moran was competent under the correct standard. The federal habeas inquiry does not authorize reweighing the wisdom of the plea, only whether due process was satisfied under the governing constitutional rule. **Miller v. Fenton, 474 U.S. 104, 112-17 (1985)**. Because the Ninth Circuit imposed a higher test than the Constitution requires, its judgment should be reversed.
