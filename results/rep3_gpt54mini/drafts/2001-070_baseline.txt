**ARGUMENT**

The Eighth Amendment does not permit the execution of persons with intellectual disability. In *Penry v. Lynaugh*, 492 U.S. 302 (1989), the Court rejected a categorical bar on the death penalty for such offenders only because, at that time, the Nation’s practice did not yet demonstrate the “consensus” necessary to find a national standard of decency. Id. at 334. But *Penry* itself made clear that the Eighth Amendment is not static; it draws its meaning from “the evolving standards of decency that mark the progress of a maturing society.” *Trop v. Dulles*, 356 U.S. 86, 101 (1958) (plurality opinion). The question here is whether those evolving standards, together with the constitutional purposes of capital punishment, now foreclose execution of the intellectually disabled. They do.

First, objective evidence confirms that a national consensus has emerged. Since *Penry*, many States have enacted statutes exempting intellectually disabled offenders from capital punishment, and others have not carried out such executions. The Court has treated precisely this kind of legislative movement as highly probative of Eighth Amendment meaning. See *Atkins v. Virginia*, 536 U.S. 304, 311–12 (2002); *Penry*, 492 U.S. at 330–34; *Stanford v. Kentucky*, 492 U.S. 361, 373 (1989). The Court does not require unanimity, only a clear trend of state action sufficient to establish society’s judgment. *Atkins*, 536 U.S. at 314–15; *Gregg v. Georgia*, 428 U.S. 153, 173 (1976) (joint opinion). By the time of this case, that trend is unmistakable. *Atkins*, 536 U.S. at 315–16.

Second, the penological justifications for capital punishment are dramatically weaker when applied to intellectually disabled offenders. Retribution is diminished because intellectual disability reduces culpability: such offenders often have limited reasoning ability, impaired judgment, and diminished capacity to process information, communicate, and learn from experience. *Atkins*, 536 U.S. at 318; *Ford v. Wainwright*, 477 U.S. 399, 409–10 (1986). Deterrence is also reduced, because the same impairments make intellectually disabled persons less likely to weigh future consequences in the manner assumed by deterrence theory. *Atkins*, 536 U.S. at 318–19. When the death penalty’s chief justifications do not meaningfully apply, execution becomes “nothing more than the purposeless and needless imposition of pain and suffering.” *Coker v. Georgia*, 433 U.S. 584, 592 (1977).

Third, a categorical rule is necessary because the characteristics of intellectual disability create an unacceptable risk of wrongful or unreliable capital sentencing. This Court has long recognized that the Eighth Amendment requires special reliability in death cases. *Woodson v. North Carolina*, 428 U.S. 280, 305 (1976); *Lockett v. Ohio*, 438 U.S. 586, 604–05 (1978). Intellectual disability may impair a defendant’s ability to communicate, assist counsel, and make a persuasive case for mitigation, while also making the defendant more susceptible to suggestion and less able to testify effectively. *Atkins*, 536 U.S. at 320–21. Those features increase the danger that a jury will overestimate moral culpability or give undue weight to a defendant’s demeanor or limited articulation. Id. This Court has repeatedly held that the death penalty must be reserved for the “worst of the worst,” a principle incompatible with executing offenders whose diminished capacities make them categorically less blameworthy. *Enmund v. Florida*, 458 U.S. 782, 798 (1982); *Tison v. Arizona*, 481 U.S. 137, 157–58 (1987).

Virginia’s reliance on *Penry* is misplaced. *Penry* did not hold that the Constitution affirmatively permits execution of intellectually disabled offenders; it held only that the then-existing record did not establish a national consensus. 492 U.S. at 340. Once consensus and contemporary standards evolved, the Constitution’s meaning changed accordingly. *Atkins*, 536 U.S. at 311–12; *Trop*, 356 U.S. at 101. Nor does the absence of a federal definition defeat respondent’s claim. States retain authority to craft reasonable procedures for determining intellectual disability, but they may not impose death on a class the Eighth Amendment forbids them to execute. *Atkins*, 536 U.S. at 317; *Ford*, 477 U.S. at 416–17.

The judgment should therefore be affirmed on the ground that the Eighth Amendment bars execution of intellectually disabled persons.
