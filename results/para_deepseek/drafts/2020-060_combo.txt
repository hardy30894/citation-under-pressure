**ARGUMENT**

The court of appeals erred in holding that Guam’s 2004 consent decree triggered CERCLA § 113(f)(3)(B), thereby barring its § 107 cost-recovery claim. The decree did not resolve Guam’s liability for a CERCLA “response action,” and the plain language of the statute, together with settled principles of statutory interpretation, sovereign immunity, and due process, compels reversal.

**I. The Consent Decree Did Not Resolve Guam’s Liability for a CERCLA Response Action**

The decree’s text—and the underlying EPA complaint—confine its scope to Clean Water Act violations, not CERCLA hazardous substance releases. *Caminetti v. United States*, 242 U.S. 470, 485 (1917) (statutory interpretation begins with “the language of the statute itself”). The decree required Guam to design a cover system to eliminate discharges of untreated leachate into the Lonfit River, a remedy for water pollution, not for contamination from munitions, DDT, or Agent Orange. *See Holy Trinity Church v. United States*, 143 U.S. 457, 459 (1892) (courts must consider “the evil which the statute was designed to remedy”). CERCLA’s contribution provision, § 113(f)(3)(B), applies only when a party has “resolved its liability to the United States … for some or all of a response action.” 42 U.S.C. § 9613(f)(3)(B). The decree never mentions CERCLA, hazardous substances, or “response action.” It imposed no obligation to clean up hazardous waste under CERCLA. *Sturges v. Crowninshield*, 17 U.S. (4 Wheat.) 122, 197 (1819) (contract terms must be “fairly construed” according to their “plain import”). Because the decree did not resolve CERCLA liability, Guam’s right to sue under § 107 remained intact.

**II. The Court of Appeals’ Interpretation Violates Established Canons of Statutory Construction**

Congress intended § 107 to remain available for parties compelled to clean up sites before any CERCLA settlement or judgment. *United States v. American Trucking Ass’ns*, 310 U.S. 534, 542 (1940) (statutes must be “interpreted in the light of the whole law and its object and policy”). Reading § 113(f)(3)(B) to extinguish § 107 claims based on a non-CERCLA decree would produce an absurd result: a party could lose its cost-recovery remedy without ever having resolved CERCLA liability. *United States v. Fisher*, 6 U.S. (2 Cranch) 358, 386 (1805) (courts must avoid “a construction that would lead to an absurdity”). The D.C. Circuit’s approach effectively writes § 107 out of the statute for any party that has entered any settlement with the government—even one wholly unrelated to CERCLA. That cannot be what Congress intended.

**III. The Government’s Construction Deprives Guam of Property Without Due Process**

Guam’s right to seek contribution from other PRPs is a property interest protected by the Fifth Amendment. *International News Service v. Associated Press*, 248 U.S. 215, 236 (1918) (a “quasi property” right in intangible claims). *Mullane v. Central Hanover Bank & Trust Co.*, 339 U.S. 306, 313 (1950) (due process requires “notice reasonably calculated … to apprise interested parties of the pendency of the action”). The consent decree extinguished Guam’s right to contribution from the United States—and from any other PRP—without any notice that it would have that effect. The decree explicitly disclaimed liability, stating it was “without any finding or admission of liability.” J.A. 92. Guam could not have anticipated that this disclaimer would trigger a three-year statute of limitations on a CERCLA claim. Allowing the government to later assert that the decree resolved CERCLA liability unfairly blindsides Guam and violates fundamental fairness.

**IV. Waivers of Sovereign Immunity Must Be Strictly Construct**

The United States, as sovereign, is immune from suit unless it has consented. *United States v. Sherwood*, 312 U.S. 584, 590 (1941) (waivers of sovereign immunity “must be strictly observed”). CERCLA § 107(a)(4)(B) provides a clear waiver of immunity for “any other necessary costs of response incurred by any other person.” Guam incurred those costs. The government now argues that § 113(f) is the exclusive remedy for a party that has settled with EPA, even if the settlement did not resolve CERCLA liability. That interpretation would narrow the waiver beyond its terms. *Schillinger v. United States*, 155 U.S. 163, 166 (1894) (sovereign immunity cannot be impliedly waived). The court of appeals’ decision effectively reads into § 113(f) a limitation that Congress did not write, thereby impermissibly contracting the government’s exposure.

**V. The Decision Undermines CERCLA’s Remedial Purpose**

Congress enacted CERCLA to ensure prompt cleanup of hazardous waste sites. *SEC v. Chenery Corp.*, 318 U.S. 80, 94 (1943) (agencies must act within the “purpose of the statute”). The court of appeals’ rule discourages voluntary cleanup by PRPs, who now face the risk that any settlement—even for non-CERCLA violations—will trigger a truncated limitations period and bar their ability to recover from other polluters. *The Paquete Habana*, 175 U.S. 677, 700 (1900) (courts must interpret statutes to avoid “harsh and oppressive” results). Guam, a small territory with limited resources, stepped up to close the Ordot Dump at the EPA’s insistence. It should not be penalized for doing so.

**CONCLUSION**

For these reasons, the judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed, and Guam’s § 107 claim should be allowed to proceed.
