# ARGUMENT

## I. THE REINSTATEMENT STATUTE DOES NOT APPLY TO ALIENS WHO REENTERED BEFORE ITS 1997 EFFECTIVE DATE

**A. Statutes are presumed to operate prospectively, and Congress must speak clearly before a court will give a statute retroactive effect.**

It is a settled canon of construction that a new statute is "addressed to the future, not to the past" unless Congress plainly directs otherwise. *Union Pacific R.R. Co. v. Laramie Stock Yards Co.*, 231 U.S. 190, 199 (1913). This Court has long required a clear expression of retroactive intent before construing a statute to reach conduct that occurred before its enactment. *United States v. Heinszen & Co.*, 206 U.S. 370, 386 (1907). That presumption is not a mere rule of thumb; it protects the reasonable expectation that private conduct will be judged by the law in force when it occurred. *Chew Heong v. United States*, 112 U.S. 536 (1884) (refusing to read an extension of the Chinese exclusion laws to disturb rights a laborer had already acquired under prior law, absent unmistakable congressional command).

Nothing in the text of § 241(a)(5) speaks to aliens who, like Mr. Fernandez-Vargas, reentered the country years before the statute existed. Congress's silence on this point is fatal to the government's position, because silence cannot supply the "unequivocal" statement of retroactive intent that this Court's precedents require. *Chew Heong*, 112 U.S. 536.

**B. Because deportation statutes work severe and irreversible consequences, ambiguities must be resolved in favor of the alien, not the government.**

Even if the reinstatement statute's temporal reach were genuinely uncertain, that uncertainty must be resolved against the harsher construction. This Court has repeatedly recognized that deportation is "a drastic measure and at times the equivalent of banishment or exile." *Delgadillo v. Carmichael*, 332 U.S. 388, 391 (1947). Because "the stakes are considerable for the individual," the Court "will not assume that Congress meant to trench on his freedom beyond that which is required by the narrowest of several possible meanings of the words used." *Fong Haw Tan v. Phelan*, 333 U.S. 6, 10 (1948). This interpretive rule applies with special force where, as here, the government seeks not merely to deport but to foreclose every form of discretionary relief—including adjustment of status based on marriage to a United States citizen—based on conduct completed under a different statutory regime. See *Costello v. Immigration & Naturalization Service*, 376 U.S. 120 (1964) (construing deportation grounds narrowly against the government). The Tenth Circuit's construction inverts this settled rule, resolving statutory silence in favor of the harshest possible result.

## II. IF THE STATUTE APPLIES, THAT APPLICATION IS IMPERMISSIBLY RETROACTIVE

**A. Attaching new and severe legal disabilities to conduct completed before the statute's enactment offends due process principles this Court has long enforced.**

Even statutes that Congress intended to operate retroactively may not be applied where doing so disturbs rights and expectations already settled under prior law. This Court has invalidated retroactive legislation that reached back to tax or penalize a completed private transaction, holding that due process forbids attaching new burdens to conduct that was lawful, or differently regulated, when it occurred. *Untermyer v. Anderson*, 276 U.S. 440 (1928) (retroactive application of a gift tax to a completed gift held unconstitutional); *Nichols v. Coolidge*, 274 U.S. 531 (1927) (retroactive estate tax could not reach an irrevocable trust created before the statute's passage); *Blodgett v. Holden*, 275 U.S. 142 (1927) (same, as to gift tax). The principle these decisions establish is that a settled transaction—one on which a person has already acted and arranged his affairs—cannot fairly be reassigned new legal consequences by subsequent legislation.

Mr. Fernandez-Vargas's 1982 reentry, and the two decades of residence, marriage, and family life he built upon it, is exactly such a settled transaction. Under the law in force in 1982, an alien in his position retained the possibility of eventually seeking adjustment of status. Section 241(a)(5), applied here, retroactively strips that possibility away and imposes, for the first time, a categorical bar to all relief—a legal consequence attached long after the primary conduct (reentry) was complete and could not be altered.

**B. This case is unlike the retroactive-deportation decisions in which the Court upheld Congress's plenary power, because those cases did not involve the wholesale elimination of all relief.**

The government may invoke *Galvan v. Press*, 347 U.S. 522 (1954), and *Harisiades v. Shaughnessy*, 342 U.S. 580 (1952), for the proposition that Congress may make past conduct a ground for present deportation. But even those decisions, upholding deportation for pre-enactment Communist Party membership, did not sanction stripping an alien of every avenue of discretionary relief based on conduct that predated the statute. Justice Frankfurter's opinion in *Galvan* acknowledged the gravity of retroactive immigration legislation even while sustaining it under Congress's plenary power over admission and exclusion of aliens. *Fong Yue Ting v. United States*, 149 U.S. 698 (1893). That plenary power does not extend so far as to permit Congress, through statutory silence, to erase a settled expectation of eligibility for relief that an alien had already earned through years of residence and marriage to a citizen.

For these reasons, § 241(a)(5) should not be read to reach Mr. Fernandez-Vargas's pre-1997 reentry, and, if it is so read, its application to him is impermissibly retroactive.
