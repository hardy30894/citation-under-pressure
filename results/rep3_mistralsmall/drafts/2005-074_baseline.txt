**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE REINSTATEMENT STATUTE DOES NOT APPLY TO ALIENS WHO RE-ENTERED BEFORE ITS EFFECTIVE DATE**

The reinstatement statute, 8 U.S.C. § 1231(a)(5), permits the Attorney General to reinstate a prior deportation order against an alien who "has reentered the United States illegally after having been removed." The statute does not expressly address whether it applies to re-entries that occurred before its enactment. However, this Court has repeatedly held that "[s]tatutes are not to be given retroactive effect absent clear congressional intent." *Landgraf v. USI Film Prods.*, 511 U.S. 244, 262 (1994). The Tenth Circuit erred in concluding that Congress intended § 1231(a)(5) to apply retroactively to pre-enactment re-entries.

The Supreme Court has made clear that "[a] statute does not operate retroactively merely because it is applied in a case arising from conduct antedating the statute's enactment." *Id.* at 269. Rather, retroactivity occurs only when the statute "attaches new legal consequences to events completed before its enactment." *Id.* at 270. Here, the reinstatement statute imposes new legal consequences—denial of all forms of relief and immediate removal—on Fernandez-Vargas’s pre-1997 re-entry. The Tenth Circuit’s interpretation thus runs afoul of *Landgraf*’s prohibition on retroactive application absent clear congressional intent.

Congress has not expressed such intent. The statute’s text contains no reference to pre-enactment re-entries, and its legislative history is silent on the issue. *See* H.R. Rep. No. 104-825, at 224 (1996). This Court has cautioned that "[a]bsent a clear statement of congressional intent, we will not apply a statute retroactively." *Martin v. Hadix*, 527 U.S. 343, 352 (1999). The Tenth Circuit’s contrary holding misapplies this principle.

**II. THE REINSTATEMENT STATUTE, AS APPLIED TO PRE-EN ACTMENT RE-ENTRIES, IS IMPERMISSIBLY RETROACTIVE**

Even if § 1231(a)(5) applies to pre-enactment re-entries, its application is impermissibly retroactive. The Supreme Court has held that a statute is retroactive if it "takes away or impairs vested rights acquired under existing laws." *Union Pac. R.R. Co. v. Laramie Stock Yards Co.*, 231 U.S. 190, 199 (1913). Fernandez-Vargas acquired a vested right to apply for adjustment of status upon marrying a U.S. citizen. *See INA § 245(a), 8 U.S.C. § 1255(a)*. The reinstatement statute strips him of that right based on conduct that occurred before its enactment, rendering its application retroactive.

The Tenth Circuit erred in concluding that Fernandez-Vargas lacked a "protectable expectation" of adjusting his status. *See Fernandez-Vargas v. Gonzales*, 428 F.3d 1198, 1205 (10th Cir. 2005). The Supreme Court has recognized that "[l]egislation is retroactive if it ‘changes the legal consequences of acts completed before its effective date.’" *Landgraf*, 511 U.S. at 270 (quoting *Weaver v. Graham*, 450 U.S. 24, 31 (1981)). Fernandez-Vargas’s pre-1997 re-entry was a completed act, and the reinstatement statute alters its legal consequences by barring relief. This is precisely the type of retroactive application the Court has condemned.

The Tenth Circuit’s reliance on *INS v. St. Cyr*, 533 U.S. 289 (2001), is misplaced. *St. Cyr* held that the Antiterrorism and Effective Death Penalty Act (AEDPA) did not strip courts of jurisdiction over habeas petitions filed by aliens who pleaded guilty before AEDPA’s enactment. *Id.* at 326. Unlike *St. Cyr*, where the alien’s conduct (pleading guilty) occurred before the statute’s effective date, Fernandez-Vargas’s re-entry was not a basis for removal under pre-reinstatement law. The reinstatement statute thus imposes a new penalty for past conduct, not a procedural change.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the Court should reverse the Tenth Circuit’s decision. The reinstatement statute does not apply to pre-enactment re-entries, and even if it did, its application would be impermissibly retroactive.
