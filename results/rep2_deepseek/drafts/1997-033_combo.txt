# Argument

The question presented is one of statutory interpretation, and the statute answers it with unusual clarity. Section 1407(a) authorizes the Judicial Panel on Multidistrict Litigation to transfer civil actions "to any district for coordinated or consolidated pretrial proceedings," but commands that "[e]ach action so transferred shall be remanded by the panel" to the transferor district "at or before the conclusion of such pretrial proceedings." Nothing in § 1407(a) grants a transferee court power to assign a case to itself for trial, and nothing in § 1404(a) supplies that power.

## I. Section 1407(a) Unambiguously Commands Remand

The word "shall" is mandatory. United States v. Lexington Mill & Elevator Co., 232 U.S. 399, 412 (1914); Escoe v. Zerbst, 295 U.S. 490, 493 (1935). Congress directed that each transferred action "shall be remanded," and it committed that duty exclusively to the Panel. The transferee court's function is limited to pretrial proceedings; once they conclude, its commission expires. The statute's repeated use of "pretrial proceedings" confines the transfer's purpose as well as its duration. A self-assignment for trial is not a pretrial proceeding; it is the very adjudication Congress removed from the transferee court's authority. The court below admitted that its contrary conclusion sits in tension with "a clear statutory mandate," yet it countenanced the practice based on circuit precedent and an internal Panel rule. Neither can override the statutory text.

Where statutory language is plain and admits of no more than one meaning, the duty of interpretation does not arise. Caminetti v. United States, 242 U.S. 470, 485 (1917); United States v. American Trucking Ass'ns, 310 U.S. 534, 543 (1940). "Shall be remanded" is not ambiguous; it is a command. Whatever efficiencies respondents imagine in retaining the case for trial are matters for Congress, not the courts. Caminetti, 242 U.S. at 490.

## II. The Specific MDL Statute Controls Over the General Venue Statute

Section 1404(a) is a general venue-transfer provision. Section 1407 is a specific, later-enacted statute addressed to multidistrict litigation. Where a specific statute and a general one conflict, the specific governs. United States v. Chase, 135 U.S. 255, 260 (1890); Ginsberg & Sons v. Popkin, 285 U.S. 204, 208 (1932). This Court has applied that canon to venue statutes in particular, holding that the general transfer provision of § 1404(a) yields where a more specific venue statute applies. Fourco Glass Co. v. Transmirra Products Corp., 353 U.S. 222, 228–29 (1957). If the general venue statute cannot override a specific venue statute, it certainly cannot override a statute that not merely specifies venue but commands remand.

Permitting the transferee court to invoke § 1404(a) to retain the case effectively repeals § 1407(a)'s remand command. Courts must, if possible, give effect to every word of a statute. Montclair v. Ramsdell, 107 U.S. 147, 152 (1883). The only construction that preserves both provisions is one that limits the transferee court to pretrial proceedings and leaves remand to the Panel. A § 1404(a) motion made by a party at the close of consolidated pretrial proceedings does not serve the statute's purpose—coordinating common pretrial issues—but rather operates to defeat the Panel's exclusive remand authority.

## III. Section 1404(a) Cannot Authorize a Transfer to the Court Already Holding the Case

Even aside from § 1407, the respondent's self-transfer cannot be squared with § 1404(a)'s text. That statute permits a district court to transfer a case "to any other district or division where it might have been brought" (emphasis added). The phrase "any other district" presupposes a different district; it cannot encompass the very district in which the case is pending. A transfer from Arizona "to" Arizona transfers nothing and is a legal fiction. Giving meaning to every word, Montclair, 107 U.S. at 152, § 1404(a) supplies no authority for what the district court did.

Moreover, venue is a personal privilege of the parties, General Investment Co. v. Lake Shore & Michigan Southern Ry. Co., 260 U.S. 261, 275 (1922), and federal courts possess only the jurisdiction and powers Congress confers, Kline v. Burke Construction Co., 260 U.S. 226, 234 (1922). A district court cannot, by fiat, redraw the venue Congress has specified. Lexecon's privilege to maintain suit in its chosen forum—a privilege preserved by § 1407's remand command—was extinguished without any statutory authority.

## IV. Policy Considerations Cannot Justify Departure From the Statutory Text

Respondents and the court below invoked efficiency and convenience: the parties had litigated in Arizona for over a year, and a short trial there would conserve resources. But this Court has limited the power to disregard plain statutory language to cases of genuinely absurd results, A.H. Phillips, Inc. v. Walling, 324 U.S. 490, 493 (1945), and the result Congress prescribed is not absurd. It reflects a deliberate congressional choice: consolidation is for pretrial proceedings; trial returns to the plaintiff's chosen forum. Congress struck that balance when it enacted § 1407, and courts are bound by it. Caminetti, 242 U.S. at 490. That the Panel's rules or the judges of the transferee court find the statute inconvenient does not license disobedience. If the balance is unwise, Congress—not the judiciary—must change it.

## Conclusion

The judgment below should be reversed, and the case remanded with directions to return it to the Judicial Panel on Multidistrict Litigation for remand to the Northern District of Illinois.
