**ARGUMENT**

The Qualifications Clauses of the Constitution are exclusive. Article I, Section 2, Clause 2 provides that “No Person shall be a Representative who shall not have attained to the Age of twenty five Years, and been seven Years a Citizen of the United States, and who shall not, when elected, be an Inhabitant of that State in which he shall be chosen.” Article I, Section 3, Clause 3 imposes parallel requirements for Senators. These provisions “fix the qualifications” of members of Congress, and the qualifications “are exclusive.” Powell v. McCormack, 395 U.S. 486, 522 (1969). A state therefore may not “alter, add to, or diminish” them. Id. at 538.

Amendment 73 adds a new qualification: prior service of three terms in the House or two terms in the Senate renders a candidate ineligible. The device of denying ballot access does not change the character of the restriction. As the Court observed in Powell, “the right to be elected is a privilege of the people, and not of the states.” Id. at 541 (quoting 1 Annals of Cong. 487 (1789) (remarks of Rep. Madison)). Because the amendment imposes an additional substantive requirement for eligibility, it is invalid under the Qualifications Clauses.

The same conclusion follows from the structure of the federal system. “The Constitution of the United States is the supreme law of the land.” McCulloch v. Maryland, 17 U.S. (4 Wheat.) 316, 426 (1819). The power to prescribe qualifications for federal office is a matter of national, not state, concern. Allowing each state to engraft its own limitations would produce “a patchwork of state qualifications” inconsistent with the uniform national legislature contemplated by the Framers. Powell, 395 U.S. at 546. The Arkansas amendment therefore exceeds the authority reserved to the states by the Tenth Amendment. See U.S. Term Limits, Inc. v. Thornton, 514 U.S. 779, 798-800 (1995) (reaffirming that the states’ reserved powers do not include authority to add congressional qualifications).

The severability clause cannot rescue the federal-office provisions. Section 3 is not an independent regulation of ballot access; it is a direct disqualification that violates the Qualifications Clauses. Because the people of Arkansas were presented with an “all or nothing” package linking state and federal term limits, the invalid federal provisions taint the entire measure. See Combs v. Glenn Falls Ins. Co., 237 Ark. 745, 375 S.W.2d 809 (1964).

The judgment of the Arkansas Supreme Court should be affirmed.
