The post-removal-period statute does not authorize the Attorney General to detain a removable alien indefinitely beyond the 90-day removal period. The statute, 8 U.S.C. § 1231(a)(6), is ambiguous and does not explicitly permit indefinite detention. To avoid serious constitutional concerns, it must be construed to contain an implicit "reasonable time" limitation.

The Supreme Court has long recognized that indefinite detention raises significant due process concerns. In Wong Wing v. United States, 163 U.S. 228 (1896), the Court held that detention beyond a reasonable time is unconstitutional. The Court stated that "detention or imprisonment for an indefinite time ... is, in its very nature, arbitrary and unreasonable" and thus violative of the Due Process Clause. Id. at 237.

Similarly, in Yick Wo v. Hopkins, 118 U.S. 356 (1886), the Court emphasized the importance of reasonable detention. The Court held that any deprivation of liberty must be "in accordance with the law of the land" and that "when we consider the nature and the theory of our institutions of government, the principles upon which they are supposed to rest, and review the history of their development, we are constrained to conclude that they do not mean to leave room for the play and action of purely personal and arbitrary power." Id. at 369-70.

In United States ex rel. Tisi v. Tod, 264 U.S. 131 (1924), the Court considered the detention of an alien pending deportation. The Court held that detention is permissible only for a reasonable time, stating that "detention is presumably to be for a reasonable time only." Id. at 134.

In Shaughnessy v. United States ex rel. Mezei, 345 U.S. 206 (1953), although the Court allowed for the detention of an excludable alien, it acknowledged that "in case of an alien denied entry, due process is not offended by his continued exclusion and detention pending a determination of his admissibility." Id. at 215 (emphasis added). The implication is that detention pending a determination is permissible, but indefinite detention is not.

These precedents establish that detention must be reasonable and not indefinite. Construing 8 U.S.C. § 1231(a)(6) to contain a "reasonable time" limitation is consistent with these principles. The statute should be interpreted to permit detention only for a period reasonably necessary to secure the alien's removal, and not indefinitely.

The judgment of the Court of Appeals should be affirmed as modified, allowing for the release of the petitioner under supervised conditions, as the detention has exceeded a reasonable time and there is no significant likelihood of removal in the reasonably foreseeable future.
