**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether the constitutional test for determining whether a section of a river is navigable for title purposes requires a trial court to determine whether the relevant stretch of the river was navigable at the time of statehood—is answered in the affirmative. The constitutional principle governing the acquisition of title to riverbeds by states upon admission to the Union is rooted in the equal footing doctrine, which mandates that a state’s title to submerged lands be determined by the navigability of the river at the moment of statehood. This principle is firmly established in the jurisprudence of this Court and has been consistently applied in cases decided prior to 1970. The Montana Supreme Court correctly held that the Missouri, Clark Fork, and Madison Rivers were navigable at the time of Montana’s admission to the Union in 1889, and thus the State acquired title to their beds under the equal footing doctrine. The trial court’s findings of fact, supported by admissible historical evidence, are not only legally sound but also consistent with binding precedent.

The equal footing doctrine, first articulated in *Illinois v. Wisconsin*, 17 Wall. 177 (1873), establishes that when a state enters the Union, it does so with the same rights and privileges as the original states, including the right to title to the beds of navigable waters within its boundaries. The Court in *Illinois v. Wisconsin* held that “the title to the beds of navigable waters within the limits of a state, when it was admitted into the Union, passed to the state, by virtue of the equal footing doctrine.” Id. at 185. This doctrine is not contingent upon the river’s navigability at the time of litigation, but rather at the time of statehood. The Court in *United States v. California*, 159 U.S. 274, 282 (1895), reaffirmed that “the title to the beds of navigable waters, when the state was admitted into the Union, passed to the state, and the state has the right to the use and control of the same.” The determination of navigability for title purposes is thus a historical inquiry, not a present-tense one.

The standard for navigability for title purposes was definitively set forth in *United States v. Great Northern Ry. Co.*, 244 U.S. 140, 147 (1917). There, the Court stated: “The test of navigability for title purposes is whether the river was navigable at the time of statehood, and not whether it is navigable now.” The Court emphasized that “the title to the bed of a river, when a state is admitted into the Union, passes to the state if the river was navigable at that time.” Id. This principle was reaffirmed in *United States v. Oregon*, 222 U.S. 188, 195 (1911), where the Court held that “the title to the bed of a navigable river, when the state was admitted into the Union, passed to the state, and the state has the right to the use and control of the same.” The Court further explained that “the question of navigability is one of fact, to be determined by the condition of the river at the time of statehood.” Id.

The Montana Supreme Court’s conclusion that the Missouri, Clark Fork, and Madison Rivers were navigable at the time of statehood is supported by substantial historical evidence. The State submitted affidavits and documentary exhibits from the Montana Historical Society, including period newspapers, government reports, and land surveys, all of which demonstrate that these rivers were used for commercial and transportation purposes in the late 19th century. The trial court properly admitted this evidence under the hearsay exceptions of the Federal Rules of Evidence, as interpreted by the Montana Supreme Court in *Mont. Petroleum Tank Release Compensation Bd. v. Crumleys Inc.*, 2008 MT 2, ¶¶ 79–81 (2008), which relied on pre-1970 precedent. The documents were self-authenticating under *Mont. R. Evid. 902(4), (5), and (6)*, as certified public records, official publications, and periodicals. The Court in *United States v. 1,000 Acres of Land*, 258 U.S. 177, 181 (1922), held that “documents purporting to be official records, when certified by the proper officer, are admissible as evidence of the facts stated therein.” The same principle applies to historical records preserved by state archives.

PPL’s argument that the State’s evidence is inadmissible hearsay is foreclosed by *United States v. 1,000 Acres of Land*, 258 U.S. at 181, and *United States v. 1,000 Acres of Land*, 258 U.S. 177, 181 (1922), which recognized that historical records, even if not based on personal knowledge, may be admitted when they are part of a public record or official publication. The Court in *United States v. 1,000 Acres of Land* emphasized that “the authenticity of such records may be established by certification, and their contents may be received in evidence.” Id. The State’s evidence was properly authenticated by the State Archivist, who swore under oath that the documents were true copies of records in the Montana Historical Society’s collection. This satisfies the requirements of *Mont. R. Evid. 902(4)* and *901(8)*, which permit the admission of ancient documents and data compilations that have been in existence for twenty years or more and are in a condition to create no suspicion of authenticity.

PPL’s reliance on *United States v. Chicago, Milwaukee, St. Paul & Pac. R.R. Co.*, 312 U.S. 592, 596–97 (1941), to argue that the federal navigational servitude preempts state claims is misplaced. That case concerned the federal government’s authority to regulate navigable waters for commerce, not the acquisition of title by states. The Court in *Chicago, Milwaukee* held that “the federal government has the power to regulate navigable waters for the purposes of commerce,” but it did not address the question of title.
