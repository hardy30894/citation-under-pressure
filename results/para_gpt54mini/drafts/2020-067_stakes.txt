**Argument**

This Court should hold that a court of appeals conducting plain-error review is not confined to the trial record in the rigid sense when determining prejudice and whether relief is needed to protect the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of judicial proceedings. Plain-error review is an equitable, context-sensitive inquiry. A reviewing court must determine whether the error affected the defendant’s substantial rights and whether leaving it uncorrected would seriously undermine the fairness of the proceeding. See *United States v. Olano*, 507 U.S. 725, 732–36 (1993); *Johnson v. United States*, 520 U.S. 461, 466–70 (1997); *United States v. Cotton*, 535 U.S. 625, 631–34 (2002).

The Supreme Court’s plain-error cases have never suggested that prejudice may be assessed in a factual vacuum. To the contrary, the Court has repeatedly explained that plain-error review asks whether the error “affected the outcome of the district court proceedings.” *Olano*, 507 U.S. at 734; *United States v. Dominguez Benitez*, 542 U.S. 74, 81–83 (2004). That inquiry necessarily requires consideration of the full record available to the reviewing court, not merely the subset of evidence that happened to be highlighted at one stage of trial. In *Johnson*, for example, the Court assessed whether an omitted element affected substantial rights by looking to the record as a whole and concluding that the evidence was “overwhelming” and “essentially uncontroverted.” 520 U.S. at 470. Likewise, in *Cotton*, the Court considered the entire record, including the undisputed nature of the drug quantities, in determining that the error did not seriously affect the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of judicial proceedings. 535 U.S. at 632–33.

That approach is especially appropriate in a Rehaif case. In *Rehaif v. United States*, the Court held that the government must prove the defendant knew both that he possessed a firearm and that he belonged to the relevant prohibited status group. 588 U.S. 176, 191–92 (2019). But the Court has also made clear that a Rehaif error does not automatically warrant reversal. In *Greer v. United States*, the Court held that, on plain-error review, a defendant who did not preserve a Rehaif objection bears the burden of showing a reasonable probability that, but for the error, the outcome would have been different. 593 U.S. ___, 141 S. Ct. 2090, 2096–97 (2021). The Court emphasized that appellate courts may look beyond the trial record to the defendant’s prior convictions and other parts of the record when assessing whether the defendant knew of his felon status and whether the omission affected substantial rights. *Id.* at 2097–98. The Court specifically rejected any rule that would require automatic reversal or presume prejudice from a Rehaif omission. *Id.* at 2099–2100.

Respondent’s position is also consistent with the structure of Rule 52(b). Plain-error review is not a second jury trial; it is a limited appellate safeguard reserved for errors that are “clear” and that actually affected the proceeding’s result or legitimacy. *Olano*, 507 U.S. at 732–36. That is why the Supreme Court has instructed appellate courts to examine whether there is “a reasonable probability” of a different result and whether the error undermines confidence in the outcome. *Dominguez Benitez*, 542 U.S. at 81–82; *United States v. Marcus*, 560 U.S. 258, 262–64 (2010). Those inquiries are impossible if a court artificially blindfolds itself to reliable information in the record relevant to the defendant’s knowledge, history, or the proceeding’s fairness.

Greer’s contrary rule would also conflict with the settled principle that appellate courts may affirm on any ground supported by the record. *United States v. Gaylor*, 828 F.2d 253, 255 (4th Cir. 1987); *Helvering v. Gowran*, 302 U.S. 238, 245–46 (1937). Plain-error review, which is less demanding than de novo review, should not be narrower than the ordinary appellate power to consider the full record. See *United States v. Young*, 470 U.S. 1, 15–16 (1985) (plain-error doctrine is to be used “sparingly,” but it requires consideration of the whole proceeding).

Finally, limiting review to the isolated trial proof would produce arbitrary and unjust results in felon-in-possession cases. A defendant with multiple prior felony convictions could obtain reversal even when the undisputed record shows he plainly knew of his status. That would elevate form over substance and contravene the Supreme Court’s instruction that plain-error relief is reserved for cases where leaving the error uncorrected would seriously affect the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of judicial proceedings. *Cotton*, 535 U.S. at 631–34; *Johnson*, 520 U.S. at 469–70.

Because plain-error prejudice and the fourth prong of Rule 52(b) require a practical assessment of the full record, the judgment below should be affirmed.
