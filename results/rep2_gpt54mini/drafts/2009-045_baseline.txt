Respondent’s fee enhancement should be affirmed only if this Court concludes that a reasonable fee under a federal fee-shifting statute may be increased for superior performance and exceptional results alone. It should not. The Supreme Court has repeatedly held that the lodestar is the “guiding light” and that it is “presumed to be the reasonable fee.” Pennsylvania v. Delaware Valley Citizens’ Council for Clean Air, 478 U.S. 546, 565-66 (1986); Blum v. Stenson, 465 U.S. 886, 897 (1984).

In Blum, the Court squarely rejected the contention that “quality of representation” or “results obtained” may justify an enhancement where those considerations are already reflected in the lodestar. 465 U.S. at 898-900. The Court explained that those factors “are presumably fully reflected in the number of billable hours recorded by counsel and in the reasonable hourly rates” and therefore “normally should not be used to adjust the lodestar.” Id. at 898-99. The Court further held that an enhancement based on superior performance is permissible only upon “specific evidence” that the lodestar does not account for the quality of service rendered and the results achieved. Id. at 899-901. But Blum did not authorize a premium merely because the district court believes counsel did an excellent job. To the contrary, the Court rejected an enhancement where the applicant failed to make the required evidentiary showing. Id. at 901.

Delaware Valley I reaffirmed that approach. The Court again stated that the lodestar “includes most, if not all, of the relevant factors constituting a ‘reasonable’ attorney’s fee,” and that “it is unnecessary to enhance the fee for superior performance in order to serve the statutory purpose.” 478 U.S. at 566. The Court emphasized that “the overall quality of performance ordinarily should not be used to adjust the lodestar” and that enhancement is justified only in the “rare” and “exceptional” case supported by “specific evidence” and detailed findings. Id. at 565-66, 568. Those decisions foreclose an enhancement based solely on a court’s general view that the result was especially good. See City of Burlington v. Dague, 505 U.S. 557, 562-67 (1992) (reaffirming that the lodestar figure is strongly presumed to be sufficient and rejecting contingency enhancements because they are not tied to the market value of the legal services rendered).

The Eleventh Circuit’s contrary rule cannot be squared with this Court’s precedents. A fee enhancement is proper only if the lodestar fails to capture a factor not already subsumed in hours and rate. Blum, 465 U.S. at 898-99; Delaware Valley I, 478 U.S. at 565-66. But “quality” and “results” are precisely the kinds of considerations the lodestar is meant to absorb. Blum, 465 U.S. at 899. Allowing a multiplier whenever a judge thinks counsel’s work was exceptional would resurrect the very kind of subjective, free-ranging fee determination the lodestar method was designed to eliminate. See Hensley v. Eckerhart, 461 U.S. 424, 433, 436 (1983) (explaining that the lodestar is the starting point and that fee awards must be tied to the degree of success obtained).

That rule also promotes administrability and judicial restraint. The lodestar provides an objective measure based on market rates and hours reasonably expended, not post hoc admiration for counsel’s advocacy. Blum, 465 U.S. at 895-96, 899. If an enhancement could be imposed simply because the court deemed the representation “superb,” then nearly every successful fee case would become a candidate for a multiplier, contrary to the Supreme Court’s repeated instruction that enhancements are reserved for rare circumstances. Delaware Valley I, 478 U.S. at 565-66; Dague, 505 U.S. at 562-67.

Respondent may argue that extraordinary results warrant extraordinary compensation. But this Court has already rejected that logic as a standalone basis for enhancement. The statute authorizes a “reasonable attorney’s fee,” not a bonus for excellence. 42 U.S.C. § 1988(b); Blum, 465 U.S. at 894-95. Where the lodestar already reflects prevailing market rates for competent counsel, an additional award for “quality” and “results” alone is impermissible. Blum, 465 U.S. at 898-901.

The judgment should therefore be reversed insofar as it permits enhancement based solely on performance and results.
