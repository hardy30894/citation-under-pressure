**ARGUMENT**

**I. The District Court’s Failure to Advise Petitioner of His Right to Appeal Is Not a Ground for Habeas Relief Because Petitioner Was Aware of His Right and Suffered No Prejudice.**

Under 28 U.S.C. § 2255, habeas relief is available only for errors that are “jurisdictional, constitutional, or constitute a fundamental defect which inherently results in a complete miscarriage of justice.” *Hill v. United States*, 368 U.S. 424, 428 (1962). The district court’s failure to comply with Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 32(a)(2) does not meet this exacting standard. In *Hill*, this Court held that a trial court’s failure to afford a defendant the right of allocution under Rule 32(a) was not cognizable under § 2255 because the error was not of “the character or magnitude” that “could be reached by a writ of habeas corpus.” *Id.* at 428. The same reasoning applies here. Rule 32(a)(2) is a procedural safeguard, not a constitutional command. Its violation, without more, does not undermine the fairness of the proceedings or the reliability of the sentence.

The sole purpose of Rule 32(a)(2) is to ensure that a defendant is informed of his right to appeal. That purpose is fully satisfied when the defendant already knows of that right. Here, the district court found, after an evidentiary hearing, that Petitioner was “aware of his right to appeal at the time of sentencing.” That finding is supported by the record and is not clearly erroneous. Because Petitioner knew of his right, the omission of the formal advisement caused him no harm. He cannot show that he would have appealed but for the court’s oversight. Accordingly, the error is harmless.

**II. The Harmless Error Standard Applies to Rule 32(a)(2) Violations.**

Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 52(a) provides that “any error, defect, irregularity, or variance which does not affect substantial rights shall be disregarded.” The failure to advise a defendant of his appeal right does not affect substantial rights if the defendant already possesses that knowledge. This Court has consistently applied harmless error analysis to procedural errors, even those that implicate important rights. *See Kotteakos v. United States*, 328 U.S. 750, 764-65 (1946) (error is harmless unless it “had substantial influence” on the outcome). The Courts of Appeals to consider this question have uniformly held that a Rule 32(a)(2) violation is subject to harmless error review. *See, e.g., United States v. Drummond*, 128 F.3d 753, 755 (2d Cir. 1997) (per curiam) (“[F]ailure to advise a defendant of his right to appeal is harmless error where the defendant knew of his right to appeal.”); *United States v. Robinson*, 20 F.3d 270, 273 (7th Cir. 1994) (same). Petitioner’s argument that any violation of Rule 32(a)(2) requires automatic vacatur of the sentence finds no support in the text of the rule or in this Court’s precedent.

**III. Even Assuming Constitutional Error, the Defect Is Harmless Beyond a Reasonable Doubt.**

The district court’s failure to advise Petitioner of his appeal right is, at most, a statutory error. But even if the error were constitutional, it would be harmless beyond a reasonable doubt. *See Chapman v. California*, 386 U.S. 18, 24 (1967). Petitioner does not claim that he was unaware of his right to appeal or that he was denied the opportunity to exercise it. To the contrary, he elected not to appeal. The error thus had no effect on his decision or on the fundamental fairness of the proceeding. Because the record demonstrates that Petitioner knew of his right and chose not to appeal, the error is harmless under any standard.

**IV. The District Court’s Factual Finding That Petitioner Knew of His Right Is Entitled to Deference.**

The district court conducted an evidentiary hearing and made a specific factual finding that Petitioner “knew of his right to appeal when the sentencing hearing occurred.” That finding is based on credible evidence, including Petitioner’s prior experience with the criminal justice system. This Court reviews such findings for clear error, and they must be upheld unless the reviewing court is “left with the definite and firm conviction that a mistake has been committed.” *United States v. United States Gypsum Co.*, 333 U.S. 364, 395 (1948). Petitioner offers no basis for overturning this finding. The court of appeals correctly affirmed.

**CONCLUSION**

The judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
