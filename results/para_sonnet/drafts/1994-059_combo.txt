# ARGUMENT

## I. The Qualifications Clauses of Article I Prescribe the Exclusive Requirements for Membership in Congress, and Amendment 73 Adds to Them.

Article I fixes the qualifications for Representative and Senator: age, citizenship, and inhabitancy. Nothing in the constitutional text authorizes a State to supplement that list. This Court confirmed the exclusivity of the enumerated qualifications in *Powell v. McCormack*, 395 U.S. 486 (1969), holding that in passing on the fitness of members "Congress is limited to the standing qualifications prescribed in the Constitution." *Id.* at 522. If the body chosen by the people of the Nation to sit in judgment of its own members' qualifications may not go beyond the constitutional text, a fortiori a single State may not do so by external device. Amendment 73's ban on ballot access after a fixed number of terms is not a procedural regulation; it operates precisely as an added disqualification, foreclosing candidates who meet every qualification the Framers wrote into the Constitution. Calling the exclusion "ballot access" rather than "ineligibility" does not change its function, and this Court has long looked past labels to substance in evaluating restrictions on access to federal office. *See McCulloch v. Maryland*, 17 U.S. (4 Wheat.) 316, 405 (1819) (rejecting attempts by a State to burden the operations of the national government, for "the government of the Union, though limited in its powers, is supreme").

## II. Membership in Congress Is a National Office Created by and Answerable to the People of the Whole Union, Not the People of a Single State.

The House of Representatives and the Senate are institutions of the national government, and the franchise that fills them is a federal right belonging to the people as a whole. In *Ex parte Yarbrough*, 110 U.S. 651 (1884), this Court explained that the right to vote for members of Congress "has its foundation in the Constitution of the United States," not merely in the laws of the State in which the election is held. *Id.* at 663. That principle governs here: because a member of Congress represents a national constituency and exercises power over the whole Union, no single State may unilaterally redefine who is fit to hold that office. *United States v. Classic*, 313 U.S. 299 (1941), reaffirmed that congressional elections are federal in character, subject ultimately to federal constitutional limits rather than the unilateral will of a State's electorate.

This Court's reapportionment decisions confirm the same structural point from a different angle. In *Wesberry v. Sanders*, 376 U.S. 1 (1964), the Court held that "as nearly as is practicable one man's vote in a congressional election is to be worth as much as another's," *id.* at 7-8, because the House is meant to be "the People's House," representing the Nation, not a patchwork of local variants. In *Gray v. Sanders*, 372 U.S. 368 (1963), the Court described the constitutional conception of political equality as "one person, one vote." *Id.* at 381. And in *Reynolds v. Sims*, 377 U.S. 533 (1964), the Court emphasized "the fundamental principle of representative government in this country is one of equal representation for equal numbers of people." *Id.* at 560-61. Permitting Arkansas to disqualify otherwise-eligible candidates would allow one State to distort this uniform, national structure, producing exactly the "patchwork" of qualifications that uniform national offices were designed to avoid.

## III. The Elections Clause Authorizes Regulation of the Times, Places, and Manner of Elections—Not the Substantive Qualifications of Candidates.

Petitioners' reliance on state authority over elections proves too much. In *Smiley v. Holm*, 285 U.S. 355 (1932), this Court construed the state legislature's power over the "Manner of holding Elections" to reach only procedural mechanics of the electoral process, subject always to congressional override. *Id.* at 366. Nothing in that clause, or in this Court's construction of it, extends state authority to the substantive question of who may stand for office once qualified under Article I. Just as the Senate alone judges "the elections, returns and qualifications of its own members," *Barry v. United States ex rel. Cunningham*, 279 U.S. 597, 613 (1929), no State legislature or electorate may pre-empt that constitutional judgment by excluding qualified candidates before the ballot is even printed.

## IV. Because Section 3 Is Unconstitutional, and the State and Federal Provisions of Amendment 73 Were Presented to the Voters as an Indivisible Package, the Entire Amendment Must Fall.

Even accepting that Arkansas may regulate its own officers' terms, Amendment 73 fused that valid objective to the invalid restriction on congressional candidates in a single, undifferentiated vote. Arkansas law measures severability by "whether the [measure] would have passed" without the offending portion. *Combs v. Glens Falls Ins. Co.*, 237 Ark. 745, 750, 375 S.W.2d 809, 812 (1964). No record evidence answers that question, and the proponents who drafted the "package" bear the burden of that uncertainty. Sections 1 through 3 were presented as a single "Term Limitation Amendment," and there is no principled basis to sever the constitutionally infirm federal provision from the state provisions with which it was inseparably bound at the ballot box.

## CONCLUSION

The judgment of the Arkansas Supreme Court invalidating Section 3 of Amendment 73 should be affirmed.
