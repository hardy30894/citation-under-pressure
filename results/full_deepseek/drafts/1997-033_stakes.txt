**ARGUMENT**

The plain text of 28 U.S.C. § 1407(a) commands that actions transferred for coordinated or consolidated pretrial proceedings “shall be remanded by the panel” to the transferor district “at or before the conclusion of such pretrial proceedings.” Congress used the word “shall,” which this Court has consistently held imposes a mandatory duty. *See, e.g., Mallard v. United States District Court*, 490 U.S. 296, 302 (1989) (“The word ‘shall’ usually connotes a requirement.”). Despite this unequivocal statutory directive, the district court invoked 28 U.S.C. § 1404(a) to assign Lexecon’s case to itself for trial, and the court of appeals declined to correct that error. The Ninth Circuit’s decision—which approved a practice that allows a transferee court to circumvent § 1407’s mandatory remand requirement—cannot be reconciled with the statute Congress enacted.

**I. Section 1407(a) Mandates Remand; It Leaves No Room for a Transferee Court to Retain a Case for Trial Under § 1404(a).**

The structure of § 1407 is clear. Subsection (a) authorizes the Judicial Panel on Multidistrict Litigation to transfer civil actions to any district “for coordinated or consolidated pretrial proceedings.” It then imposes an unqualified obligation: “Each action so transferred shall be remanded by the panel at or before the conclusion of such pretrial proceedings to the district from which it was transferred.” 28 U.S.C. § 1407(a). The word “shall” leaves no discretion. *See Lexecon Inc. v. Milberg Weiss Bershad Hynes & Lerach*, 523 U.S. 26, 35 (1998) (“[T]he Panel’s instruction is mandatory.”).

Nothing in § 1407 authorizes a transferee court to invoke § 1404(a) to retain a case for trial. To the contrary, the statute’s text, structure, and history all demonstrate that Congress intended the Panel alone to determine where trial will occur, and only after the Panel remands the case to the transferor district. *Id.* at 40–41. The transferee court’s role is limited to managing pretrial proceedings; once those proceedings conclude, its authority over the case ends. *Id.* at 38.

The practice of using § 1404(a) to bypass § 1407’s remand requirement—endorsed by the Ninth Circuit below and by some other courts—rests on a fundamental misinterpretation of the two statutes. Section 1404(a) provides that “[f]or the convenience of parties and witnesses, in the interest of justice, a district court may transfer any civil action to any other district or division where it might have been brought.” 28 U.S.C. § 1404(a). But a transferee court cannot transfer a case under § 1404(a) before the Panel has remanded it, because until remand, the transferee court lacks jurisdiction over the case for purposes of venue transfer. *Lexecon*, 523 U.S. at 40. The Panel retains exclusive authority over the case’s venue until it issues a remand order. *Id.* at 39–40.

The Ninth Circuit’s reliance on Multidistrict Litigation Rule 14(b) and decisions such as *In re Fine Paper Antitrust Litigation*, 685 F.2d 810 (3d Cir. 1982), and *Pfizer, Inc. v. Lord*, 447 F.2d 122 (2d Cir. 1971), is misplaced. Those authorities conflict with the plain text of § 1407. As Judge Kozinski observed in dissent, § 1407(a) reflects “an important policy choice: Cases may be transferred to remote locations, but only for the duration of pretrial procedures.” Pet. App. 19a. When trial approaches, plaintiffs retain “their ancient right to select the forum.” *Id.* Congress did not authorize transferee courts to override that choice through § 1404(a). *Lexecon*, 523 U.S. at 40–41.

**II. The Ninth Circuit’s Reluctance to Grant Mandamus Does Not Salvage the District Court’s Erroneous Order.**

The court of appeals denied mandamus principally because it concluded that the district court’s order was not “clearly erroneous as a matter of law” and because it believed that review on direct appeal remained available. Pet. App. 10a–12a. But the court acknowledged that petitioners’ statutory argument “appears to be a clear statutory mandate.” Pet. App. 12a. And this Court has since confirmed that the district court’s action was indeed legally erroneous. *Lexecon*, 523 U.S. at 40–41. The Ninth Circuit’s refusal to correct the error in the first instance—and its suggestion that the issue could be deferred to a later appeal—does not immunize the district court’s order from review. The mandamus standard does not permit a court to ignore a clear statutory command simply because the error might be corrected later.

**III. The District Court’s Order Violates the Separation of Powers and Undermines Congressional Intent.**

Congress enacted § 1407 to achieve efficiency in multidistrict litigation while preserving litigants’ venue choices for trial. *Lexecon*, 523 U.S. at 34–35. The statute represents a carefully calibrated balance: The Panel may centralize pretrial proceedings, but must remand each case before trial so that the parties may litigate in the forum of their choosing. *Id.* at 35. By allowing a transferee court to retain a case under § 1404(a), the courts below upset that balance. They substituted their own policy judgment for that of Congress. *Id.* at 41 (noting that “the transferee court’s assumption of trial jurisdiction” contravenes “the statutory design”). This Court has repeatedly held that federal courts must enforce the plain meaning of a statute, not rewrite it to suit their views of convenience. *See, e.g., Hartford Underwriters Ins. Co. v. Union Planters Bank, N.A.*, 530 U.S. 1, 6 (2000).

**CONCLUSION**

The judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed, and the case remanded with instructions to order the district court to remand the action to the Northern District of Illinois under § 1407(a).
