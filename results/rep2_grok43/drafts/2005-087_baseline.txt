ARGUMENT

Arizona's rule confining expert mental-disease evidence to the insanity defense comports with the Due Process Clause. A state may allocate the burden of proving insanity to the defendant, Leland v. Oregon, 343 U.S. 790, 799 (1952), and may "regulate the admissibility of evidence" without violating fundamental fairness, Montana v. Egelhoff, 518 U.S. 37, 43 (1996) (plurality opinion). The Clause "does not require the States to conform to any particular model" for defining or proving mens rea. Patterson v. New York, 432 U.S. 197, 211 (1977).

The Arizona scheme preserves the prosecution's burden to prove every element beyond a reasonable doubt while permitting the defendant to show, through lay and expert testimony, that he lacked the requisite intent. The limitation on expert evidence merely channels that proof through the affirmative defense the State has chosen to recognize. Such channeling is constitutional because "the due process clause does not permit the accused to introduce any and all evidence he deems relevant." Egelhoff, 518 U.S. at 43.

Petitioner's reliance on Chambers v. Mississippi, 410 U.S. 284 (1973), is misplaced. Chambers addressed the exclusion of reliable, exculpatory evidence that was "critical" to the defense and bore "assurances of trustworthiness." Id. at 302. Arizona's rule does not exclude all mental-state evidence; it restricts only one category of expert testimony to the defense that state law has designated for that purpose. The restriction is therefore "a familiar feature of the common law" rather than an arbitrary barrier. Egelhoff, 518 U.S. at 48.

Because the rule is both traditional and rationally related to the State's legitimate interest in orderly adjudication of insanity claims, it does not offend due process. The judgment of the Arizona Court of Appeals should be affirmed.
