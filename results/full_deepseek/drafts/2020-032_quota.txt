**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Case Is Moot Because the Challenged Policies Have Been Unambiguously Repealed and No Live Controversy Remains.**

Article III of the Constitution limits federal jurisdiction to “Cases” and “Controversies.” *Already, LLC v. Nike, Inc.*, 568 U.S. 85, 90 (2013). A case becomes moot “when the issues presented are no longer ‘live’ or the parties lack a legally cognizable interest in the outcome.” *City of Los Angeles v. Lyons*, 461 U.S. 95, 101 (1983). Here, Georgia Gwinnett College revised its “Freedom of Expression Policy” and removed the challenged portion of its “Student Code of Conduct” while this litigation was pending. The new policies permit students to speak anywhere on campus without a permit, except in limited circumstances. The district court found that the college “unambiguously terminated the Prior Policies and there is no reasonable basis to expect that it will return to them.” That finding is binding on appeal and is fully supported by the record. Appellants do not challenge this finding. Consequently, their claims for declaratory and injunctive relief are unquestionably moot. *See Lewis v. Continental Bank Corp.*, 494 U.S. 472, 477 (1990) (a case is moot when “the parties lack a legally cognizable interest in the outcome”).

**II. The Voluntary-Cessation Doctrine Does Not Salvage This Lawsuit.**

The voluntary-cessation doctrine is a narrow exception to mootness, but it applies only when there is a “reasonable expectation” that the defendant will resume the challenged conduct. *Friends of the Earth, Inc. v. Laidlaw Environmental Services (TOC), Inc.*, 528 U.S. 167, 189 (2000). That standard is not met here. A government entity’s repeal of an unconstitutional policy is entitled to “special solicitude” and is presumed to be permanent unless the plaintiff demonstrates a “substantial likelihood” of reenactment. *Already, LLC*, 568 U.S. at 91. Appellants have offered no evidence—and the record contains none—that GGC intends to revert to the prior restrictive policies. To the contrary, the college’s prompt revision demonstrates a good-faith effort to comply with constitutional requirements. The voluntary-cessation doctrine therefore does not apply.

**III. Nominal Damages Do Not Rescue an Otherwise Moot Constitutional Claim.**

Appellants rely solely on a request for nominal damages to preserve a live controversy. But nominal damages are a “technical” form of relief that vindicate a right without compensating for any actual injury. *Steel Co. v. Citizens for a Better Environment*, 523 U.S. 83, 108 (1998). This Court has never held that a prayer for nominal damages, standing alone, satisfies Article III’s case-or-controversy requirement when the underlying injunctive and declaratory claims are moot. To the contrary, “a plaintiff must demonstrate a ‘personal stake’ in the outcome at all stages of the litigation.” *Lewis*, 494 U.S. at 477. A claim for nominal damages based solely on a past constitutional violation, without any ongoing or future harm, does not create a “live” controversy. *See Lyons*, 461 U.S. at 109 (past injury alone insufficient to support standing for injunctive relief). The Eleventh Circuit correctly recognized in *Flanigan’s Enterprises, Inc. of Georgia v. City of Sandy Springs*, 868 F.3d 1248, 1270 (11th Cir. 2017) (en banc), that “a prayer for nominal damages generally will not save an otherwise moot challenge to an allegedly unconstitutional policy or law.”

Appellants urge this Court to adopt a contrary rule, but doing so would eviscerate the mootness doctrine. Every plaintiff who had been injured by a now-repealed policy could continue to litigate the policy’s constitutionality indefinitely by seeking nominal damages. That result would convert federal courts into advisory bodies tasked with rendering opinions on the validity of defunct laws. *See Already, LLC*, 568 U.S. at 90 (“Federal courts are not in the business of pronouncing upon past disputes.”). The Constitution forbids such advisory opinions. *Steel Co.*, 523 U.S. at 101.

**IV. Appellants’ Failure to Plead Compensatory Damages or Any Concrete Injury Confirms Mootness.**

Even if a request for nominal damages could sometimes survive a mootness challenge, Appellants’ case is particularly weak because they have not alleged any actual injury. To recover compensatory damages under 42 U.S.C. § 1983, a plaintiff must plead “actual injury” such as monetary loss, physical pain, or emotional distress. *Slicker v. Jackson*, 215 F.3d 1225, 1230 (11th Cir. 2000). Appellants’ complaint mentions “injury” only twice, without specifying what that injury was, and repeatedly invokes “irreparable harm” without any factual elaboration. The district court properly concluded that the complaint sought only nominal damages. Appellants’ belated attempt to characterize their request for “damages in an amount to be determined by the evidence” as a claim for compensatory damages is unavailing. The complaint’s prayer for relief explicitly requests only “nominal damages,” and the factual allegations are devoid of any claim of actual harm. Because Appellants have asserted no concrete, redressable injury, their claim for nominal damages cannot supply the “personal stake” required by Article III. *See Honig v. Doe*, 484 U.S. 305, 317 (1988) (mootness applies when there is “no reasonable expectation that the alleged violation will recur” and “interim relief or events have completely and irrevocably eradicated the effects of the alleged violation”).

**CONCLUSION**

The judgment of the Court of Appeals should be affirmed.
