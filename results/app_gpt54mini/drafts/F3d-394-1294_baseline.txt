**ARGUMENT**

The Tax Court erred in sustaining the Commissioner’s disallowance of the marital deduction. The amended trust gives Mrs. Davis the qualifying income interest for life required by I.R.C. § 2056(b)(7)(B)(ii), because she is entitled to all trust income payable at least quarterly and no one holds a power to divert any part of the property to anyone other than her. The court’s contrary conclusion rested on an overly restrictive reading of the amendment that is inconsistent with the statute and controlling authority.

Section 2056(b)(7) allows a marital deduction for qualified terminable interest property, which exists where “the surviving spouse has a qualifying income interest for life” and “no person has a power to appoint any part of the property to any person other than the surviving spouse.” I.R.C. § 2056(b)(7)(B)(i), (ii). The statute focuses on the spouse’s enforceable right to current income and on the absence of a competing dispositive power. Estate of Clayton v. Commissioner, 976 F.2d 1486, 1490-91 (5th Cir. 1992); Estate of Spencer v. Commissioner, 43 F.3d 226, 228-29 (6th Cir. 1995). The Davis amendment satisfies both requirements.

First, the amendment directs that, “[a]fter the death of trustor survived by his spouse and during the lifetime of his surviving spouse, the trustee shall pay to or apply for the benefit of the surviving spouse, in quarter annual or more frequent installments, all of the net income from the trust estate.” That language tracks the statute’s mandate that the spouse be “entitled to all the income from the property, payable annually or at more frequent intervals.” I.R.C. § 2056(b)(7)(B)(ii)(I). A spouse need not be given unfettered control over the trust corpus to qualify; the hallmark of QTIP is the right to all income for life, not a general power over principal. Estate of Howard v. Commissioner, 910 F.2d 633, 635-36 (9th Cir. 1990); Estate of Spencer, 43 F.3d at 229.

Second, the amendment’s discretionary-support standard does not defeat the deduction. Mrs. Davis argues only for subsection 2 and the resulting trust income interest; the trust terms do not give any other beneficiary a present right to income. Under the statute, the trust qualifies so long as no one else may appoint the property away from the surviving spouse. I.R.C. § 2056(b)(7)(B)(ii)(II). Here, successor trustees—including the daughters—may administer the trust, but administrative discretion is not the same as a power to appoint principal or income to another beneficiary. Estate of Tatum v. United States, 706 F.2d 483, 487-88 (5th Cir. 1983); Estate of Rapp v. Commissioner, 140 F.3d 1211, 1214-15 (9th Cir. 1998). The amendment nowhere authorizes the trustee to make distributions to anyone other than Mrs. Davis, and subsection 5 permits invasion of principal only “for the benefit of surviving spouse.” That is not a competing power of appointment; it is an additional benefit for the spouse.

The Commissioner and the Tax Court focused on the trustee’s “reasonable discretion” and the instruction that the trustee “may consider any other income or resources of the beneficiary known to the trustee and reasonably available.” But a discretion to determine the amount and timing of distributions for the spouse’s welfare does not eliminate the spouse’s qualifying income interest where the trustee remains obligated to pay all net income to or for the spouse and has no authority to divert benefits elsewhere. Estate of Clayton, 976 F.2d at 1491; Estate of Howard, 910 F.2d at 636. The statute expressly permits income interests that are payable more frequently than annually, and nothing in § 2056(b)(7) requires a fixed, unconditional distribution formula free of all fiduciary judgment. See Estate of Spencer, 43 F.3d at 229.

Nor does subsection 5’s principal invasion provision disqualify the trust. Section 2056(b)(7) allows QTIP treatment so long as the spouse’s income interest is not terminable by a power to benefit another person. A trustee’s power to invade principal for the spouse’s own support strengthens, rather than weakens, the spouse’s beneficial interest. Estate of Rapp, 140 F.3d at 1215. The daughters’ contingent succession as trustees likewise does not create an impermissible interest. Trustees may succeed one another without becoming beneficiaries. Estate of Howard, 910 F.2d at 636.

Because the amended trust gives Mrs. Davis a qualifying income interest for life and no person has a power to appoint any part of the property to anyone other than her, the Davis estate was entitled to the marital deduction under I.R.C. § 2056(b)(7). The decision of the Tax Court should be reversed.
