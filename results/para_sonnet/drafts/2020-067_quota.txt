# ARGUMENT

The Eleventh Circuit correctly held that Greer’s forfeited *Rehaif* claim fails under Rule 52(b) because it properly examined the entire record—not merely the evidence formally placed before the jury—to determine whether the error affected Greer’s substantial rights or the fairness of the proceeding. This Court’s plain-error jurisprudence, and the practical realities of appellate review, confirm that reviewing courts may and must consult the whole record when performing this inquiry.

## I. Rule 52(b)’s Third and Fourth Prongs Require Examination of the Entire Record, Not an Artificially Confined Trial Transcript.

Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 52(b) permits correction of a “plain error that affects substantial rights” even though the error was not preserved below. In *United States v. Olano*, 507 U.S. 725 (1993), this Court established the now-familiar four-part framework: the defendant must show (1) error, (2) that is plain, (3) that affects substantial rights, and (4) that “seriously affect[s] the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of judicial proceedings.” *Id.* at 732 (quoting *United States v. Young*, 470 U.S. 1, 15 (1985)). Critically, *Olano* placed the burden of persuasion on the defendant to demonstrate prejudice, explaining that “it is the defendant rather than the Government who bears the burden of persuasion with respect to prejudice.” 507 U.S. at 734. A defendant cannot carry that burden through a truncated record designed to exclude inconvenient facts.

This Court squarely addressed the scope-of-review question in *United States v. Vonn*, 535 U.S. 55 (2002). There, the defendant argued that a reviewing court assessing Rule 11 plain error should be confined to the record of the plea colloquy itself. This Court rejected that limitation, holding that “a reviewing court may consult the whole record when considering the effect of any error on substantial rights.” *Id.* at 59. The Court reasoned that “the purpose of the plain-error rule” would be defeated by artificially blinding appellate courts to record evidence that bears directly on prejudice. *Id.* at 74. *Vonn* is directly controlling: if courts may canvass the whole record to assess Rule 11 errors, they may do the same for *Rehaif* errors, which likewise implicate the defendant’s knowledge of an unremarkable fact.

*United States v. Dominguez Benitez*, 542 U.S. 74 (2004), reinforces this approach. There, this Court held that to satisfy Rule 52(b)’s third prong, a defendant “must show a reasonable probability that, but for the error,” the outcome would have been different. *Id.* at 83. That standard cannot be applied in a vacuum; it requires the reviewing court to weigh the error against the totality of what the record discloses about the case, including undisputed facts never contested by the defendant.

## II. This Court’s Precedents Confirm That Appellate Courts Must Assess Plain Error in Light of the Whole Proceeding, Including Overwhelming or Uncontested Evidence.

In *Johnson v. United States*, 520 U.S. 461 (1997), this Court declined to exercise its discretion to correct a forfeited materiality error precisely because “the evidence supporting materiality was so overwhelming” that reversal would not affect the fairness of the proceedings. *Id.* at 470. The Court did not limit itself to the jury instructions or the specific evidentiary items disputed at trial—it looked to the record as a whole, including matters the defendant had never challenged, to conclude that “affirming her conviction ... would not seriously affect the fairness, integrity or public reputation of judicial proceedings.” *Id.* Greer’s case presents an even stronger scenario: his own PSR, prior felony convictions, and stipulations demonstrate that felon status was never a genuine issue.

*United States v. Marcus*, 560 U.S. 258 (2010), likewise instructs that plain-error review must account for the “record as a whole” in gauging the probability of a different outcome. *Id.* at 265. And *Puckett v. United States*, 556 U.S. 129 (2009), reaffirmed that the plain-error standard governs forfeited claims generally and that a defendant seeking relief bears the burden of showing prejudice under a “fact-intensive” inquiry, *id.* at 135, a burden incompatible with artificial record-limitation.

Finally, in the collateral-review context, *United States v. Frady*, 456 U.S. 152 (1982), this Court examined the full trial record—including matters not squarely litigated—to determine that any instructional error was harmless given the overwhelming, uncontested evidence of the defendant’s mental state. *Id.* at 172. The logic of *Frady* applies with full force here: where the record leaves no doubt that a defendant knew of his felon status—as Greer’s multiple, lengthy prior felony convictions and prior terms of imprisonment establish—an appellate court need not pretend otherwise merely because the point went unlitigated below.

## III. Rehaif Does Not Displace Ordinary Plain-Error Principles.

*Rehaif v. United States*, 139 S. Ct. 2191 (2019), held that the Government must prove a defendant knew of his prohibited status. *Id.* at 2200. But *Rehaif* said nothing about displacing Rule 52(b)’s established framework or *Vonn*’s directive to consult the whole record. Where, as here, the record contains overwhelming and uncontroverted proof—including Greer’s own repeated felony convictions and years of incarceration—that he knew he was a felon, the Eleventh Circuit properly concluded that no reasonable probability of a different outcome exists, and that the proceeding’s fairness and integrity remain intact.

The judgment below should be affirmed.
