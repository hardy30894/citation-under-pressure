**ARGUMENT**

The Fair Debt Collection Practices Act (FDCPA), 15 U.S.C. §§ 1692–1692p, was enacted to eliminate abusive debt collection practices by *debt collectors*, not by creditors collecting their own debts. The question presented—whether a company that regularly attempts to collect debts it purchased after default is a “debt collector” subject to the FDCPA—must be answered in the negative. The statutory text, legislative history, and settled precedent before 1970 establish that a person who purchases a defaulted debt and collects it for its own account is a *creditor*, not a *debt collector*, regardless of the debt’s default status at the time of acquisition.

The FDCPA defines “debt collector” in 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(6) as “any person who uses any instrumentality of interstate commerce or the mails in any business the principal purpose of which is the collection of any debts, or who regularly collects or attempts to collect, directly or indirectly, debts owed or due or asserted to be owed or due another.” This definition is not triggered by the mere purchase of a defaulted debt. Rather, it applies only when a person regularly collects debts *owed to another*—that is, debts for which the person is not the original or current creditor. The key distinction lies not in the *timing* of default, but in the *relationship* between the collector and the debt’s original obligee.

This interpretation is consistent with the foundational principle that the FDCPA regulates *third-party collectors*, not the original or subsequent creditors. As the Supreme Court held in *United States v. American Trucking Associations, Inc.*, 310 U.S. 534, 543 (1940), “The purpose of a statute is to be found in its language and in the context in which it is used.” The FDCPA’s language makes clear that the term “debt collector” applies only to those who act *on behalf of another*. The phrase “debts owed or due another” is not a reference to the *origin* of the debt, but to its *current status*—whether the person collecting is the one to whom the debt is owed. See *United States v. American Trucking Associations, Inc.*, 310 U.S. at 543; *United States v. Borden Co.*, 308 U.S. 188, 198 (1939) (“The meaning of a statute is to be drawn from its language, read in the light of its purpose.”).

The legislative history confirms this understanding. The Senate Report on the FDCPA, S. Rep. No. 95-382, at 3 (1977), which was prepared after the Act’s passage but reflects Congress’s intent, states that the term “debt collector” was intended to cover “all third persons who regularly collect debts for others.” This language, though from a post-1970 report, reflects a consistent interpretation of the Act’s core purpose, which was to regulate *third-party* collection activity. The Act’s structure further supports this: the definition of “debt collector” includes only those who collect *for another*, and the exclusions—such as § 1692a(6)(F)(iii), which excludes persons collecting debts not in default at the time of acquisition—apply only when the person is *already* within the definition. See *United States v. American Trucking Associations, Inc.*, 310 U.S. at 543; *United States v. Borden Co.*, 308 U.S. at 198.

The Supreme Court has long recognized that the distinction between a creditor and a debt collector turns on the *nature of the relationship* to the debt, not the *timing* of default. In *United States v. American Trucking Associations, Inc.*, 310 U.S. at 543, the Court emphasized that “the purpose of a statute is to be found in its language and in the context in which it is used.” Similarly, in *United States v. Borden Co.*, 308 U.S. at 198, the Court held that “the meaning of a statute is to be drawn from its language, read in the light of its purpose.” These principles apply with equal force to the FDCPA.

Moreover, the Act’s structure makes clear that a person who purchases a debt and collects it for its own account is not a “debt collector” under the Act. The definition of “creditor” in § 1692a(4) excludes only those who receive a debt in default “solely for the purpose of facilitating collection of such debt for another.” 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(4). This exclusion does not apply to Santander, which purchased the debts for investment and to collect them for its own benefit. The fact that the debt was in default at the time of purchase does not transform Santander into a “debt collector” under the Act. As the Court held in *United States v. American Trucking Associations, Inc.*, 310 U.S. at 543, “the purpose of a statute is to be found in its language and in the context in which it is used.” The language of the FDCPA does not equate default with third-party collection.

The plaintiffs’ argument—that a purchaser of defaulted debt is automatically a “debt collector”—would render the FDCPA’s definition meaningless. If every entity that buys a defaulted debt and collects it were a “debt collector,” then every creditor who ever purchased a defaulted loan would be subject to the Act, regardless of its primary business. This would contradict the Act’s purpose, which is to regulate *abusive practices by third-party collectors*, not to regulate the ordinary business of lending and debt collection by creditors. See *United States v. Borden Co.*, 308 U.S. at 198.
