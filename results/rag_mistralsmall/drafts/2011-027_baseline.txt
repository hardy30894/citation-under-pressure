**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Montana Supreme Court Correctly Applied the Equal Footing Doctrine by Determining Navigability at Statehood.**

The equal footing doctrine vests title to lands beneath navigable waters in the State upon its admission to the Union. *Utah Div. of State Lands v. United States*, 482 U.S. 193, 195 (1987) ("[W]hen the 13 Colonies became independent from Great Britain, they claimed title to the lands under navigable waters within their boundaries as the sovereign successors to the English Crown. Because all subsequently admitted States enter the Union on an 'equal footing' with the original 13 States, they too hold title to the land under navigable waters within their boundaries upon entry into the Union.") (citing *Pollard’s Lessee v. Hagan*, 3 How. 212 (1845)). This Court has repeatedly emphasized that the inquiry is whether the waterway was navigable *at the time of statehood*. *Id.* at 201 ("[T]he proper application of the [equal footing] doctrine requires an understanding of its origins."); *Idaho v. United States*, 533 U.S. 262, 271 (2001) ("[W]e have looked beyond the moment of statehood for evidence of federal intent.").

The Montana Supreme Court adhered to this principle by examining historical evidence to determine whether the Missouri, Madison, and Clark Fork Rivers were navigable in 1889. *PPL Montana, LLC v. Montana*, 2010 MT 293, ¶¶ 60–94 (Mont. 2010). This approach is consistent with this Court’s precedents. *See, e.g., United States v. Appalachian Elec. Power Co.*, 311 U.S. 377, 408 (1941) ("The capability of use by the public for purposes of transportation and commerce affords the true criterion of the navigability of a river, rather than the extent and manner of that use.").

PPL’s contention that the trial court should have considered post-statehood navigability is foreclosed by this Court’s decisions. *See Utah Div. of State Lands*, 482 U.S. at 201 ("[T]he proper application of the doctrine requires an understanding of its origins."); *Idaho*, 533 U.S. at 271 ("[W]e have looked beyond the moment of statehood for evidence of federal intent."). The Montana Supreme Court correctly applied the equal footing doctrine by focusing on navigability at statehood.

**II. The Federal Power Act Does Not Preempt State Compensation Claims.**

PPL argues that the Federal Power Act (FPA) preempts Montana’s claim for compensation under the Hydroelectric Resources Act (HRA). This argument fails under both field and conflict preemption analyses.

The FPA establishes a "dual system" of federal and state regulation. *First Iowa Hydro-Elec. Coop. v. Fed. Power Comm’n*, 328 U.S. 152, 167–68 (1946) ("[T]he Federal Power Act establishes a dual system of control."). The FPA grants FERC authority to license hydroelectric projects but preserves state authority over "proprietary rights" affected by such projects. *Id.* at 175–76 ("[T]he Act leaves to the States the determination of what lands are subject to private ownership.").

The Montana Supreme Court correctly concluded that the FPA does not preempt state compensation claims. *PPL Montana*, 2010 MT 293, ¶¶ 11–16. This conclusion is supported by this Court’s precedents. *See, e.g., Jordan v. Randolph Mills, Inc.*, 716 F.2d 1053, 1055 (4th Cir. 1983) ("A FERC license neither transfers nor diminishes any right of possession or enjoyment possessed by" a landowner.).

PPL’s reliance on *Fed. Power Comm’n v. Niagara Mohawk Power Corp.*, 347 U.S. 239 (1954), is misplaced. In *Niagara*, the Court held that the FPA did not authorize the federal government to take private property without compensation. *Id.* at 249–51. The FPA’s preservation of state property rights distinguishes it from *Niagara*. *See First Iowa*, 328 U.S. at 175–76.

**III. The Federal Navigational Servitude Does Not Bar State Compensation Claims.**

The federal navigational servitude does not preempt state claims for compensation. *Montana v. United States*, 450 U.S. 544, 551 (1981) ("The federal navigational servitude is the power of Congress to ensure that navigable rivers remain open to interstate and foreign commerce."). This servitude does not divest states of their proprietary rights in riverbeds. *See United States v. Chicago, Milwaukee, St. Paul & Pac. R.R. Co.*, 312 U.S. 592, 596–97 (1941) ("[T]he navigational servitude does not extend to lands above the high-water mark.").

The Montana Supreme Court correctly held that the navigational servitude does not bar Montana’s compensation claims. *PPL Montana*, 2010 MT 293, ¶¶ 11–12. This conclusion is consistent with this Court’s precedents. *See, e.g., Fox River Paper Co. v. Railroad Comm’n*, 274 U.S. 651, 655 (1927) ("[T]he rights of the state and of riparian owners in navigable waters within the state and to the soil beneath are matters of state law.").

**Conclusion**

The Montana Supreme Court correctly applied the equal footing doctrine, determined that the FPA does not preempt state compensation claims, and held that the navigational servitude does not bar Montana’s claims. This Court should affirm the judgment below.
