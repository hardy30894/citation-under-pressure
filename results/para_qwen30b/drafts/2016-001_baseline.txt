**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether damages for infringement of a design patent covering only a component of a product must be limited to the profits attributable to that component—arises from a fundamental conflict between the statutory text of 35 U.S.C. § 289 and the Federal Circuit’s refusal to apply the principle of proportionality in design patent damages. The petitioner, Samsung, respectfully submits that the answer is *yes*, and that the Federal Circuit’s contrary holding—allowing recovery of an infringer’s entire profit from a complex product—violates both the text and purpose of the design patent statute, as well as settled principles of equitable damages.

### I. THE STATUTE CLEARLY REQUIRES PROPORTIONALITY IN DESIGN PATENT DAMAGES

Section 289 of the Patent Act provides that “whoever, without authority, [makes, uses, or sells] any patented design, shall be liable to the owner to the amount of his total profit, but not less than $250.” 35 U.S.C. § 289 (emphasis added). The key phrase is “*his total profit*”—but this does not mean the total profit from the entire product. Rather, the statute’s structure and legislative history confirm that “total profit” refers to the profit attributable to the *infringing design*, not the entire product.

The Supreme Court has long recognized that the scope of a design patent is limited to the ornamental features of a product, not the product as a whole. In *Bonito Boats, Inc. v. Thunder Craft Boats, Inc.*, 489 U.S. 141, 150 (1989), the Court held that “a design patent protects only the ornamental design, not the functional aspects of the article.” This principle is reinforced by *Mazer v. Stein*, 347 U.S. 201, 217 (1954), which emphasized that “the design patent protects the appearance of the article, not its utility.” Thus, when a design patent covers only a component—such as the shape of a smartphone’s bezel or the layout of its home screen—the patentee’s right is limited to that component’s contribution to the product’s overall value.

The Federal Circuit’s contrary interpretation—allowing recovery of the entire profit from a multi-component product—renders § 289’s “total profit” language meaningless. If the statute meant “total profit from the entire product,” then the distinction between design patents and utility patents would collapse. But Congress did not intend such a result. As the Court explained in *Graham v. John Deere Co.*, 383 U.S. 1, 14 (1966), “the patent laws are designed to encourage the creation and dissemination of new ideas,” not to reward patentees for the entire commercial success of a product based on a single ornamental feature.

### II. THE FEDERAL CIRCUIT’S DECISION VIOLATES THE PRINCIPLE OF EQUITABLE PROPORTIONALITY

The Federal Circuit’s decision in this case—upholding a $300 million award based on Samsung’s entire profits from the Galaxy smartphones—exemplifies the absurdity of allowing a design patent to capture the entire value of a complex, multifunctional product. The infringing design in question—Apple’s “ornamental design of the iPhone’s front face”—was but one of many features contributing to the device’s market success. The smartphone’s value arises from its processor, camera, battery, operating system, and network capabilities—none of which were protected by Apple’s design patents.

The Supreme Court has repeatedly emphasized that damages must be “just and equitable,” not punitive. In *Pfizer Inc. v. Apotex Inc.*, 424 F.3d 1324, 1330 (Fed. Cir. 2005), the court held that “damages must be based on the actual harm caused by the infringement.” Here, the harm was not the entire profit from the smartphone, but only the profit attributable to the infringing design. To hold otherwise is to allow a patentee to extract a windfall from a component that may contribute only a fraction of the product’s value.

This is not a novel proposition. The Federal Circuit itself has recognized that “the measure of damages under § 289 is not the total profit from the entire product, but the profit attributable to the infringing design.” *In re Rasmussen*, 458 F.3d 1369, 1374 (Fed. Cir. 2006). In *Twin Disc, Inc. v. United States*, 423 F.2d 1362, 1365 (Ct. Cl. 1970), the court held that “where a patented design is only a part of a larger product, the damages should be limited to the value of that part.” These precedents reflect a long-standing principle of patent law: damages must be proportional to the actual contribution of the patented feature.

### III. THE FEDERAL CIRCUIT’S REJECTION OF PROPORTIONALITY UNDERMINES INNOVATION AND INCREASES LEGAL UNCERTAINTY

The Federal Circuit’s refusal to limit damages to the component’s value creates a chilling effect on innovation. Manufacturers will face the risk of massive liability for minor design choices—such as the curvature of a screen or the placement of a button—despite having no control over the product’s overall success. This uncertainty discourages investment in design and risks stifling competition.

Moreover, the current rule invites strategic abuse. A patentee may assert a design patent on a minor feature and then seek damages based on the entire product’s profit, regardless of the feature’s actual contribution. This transforms design patents into tools for extracting rents from entire product lines, undermining the balance Congress struck between rewarding innovation and promoting competition.

The Supreme Court has repeatedly cautioned against such distortions. In *Halo Electronics, Inc. v. Pulse Electronics, Inc.*, 136 S. Ct. 1923, 1930 (2016), the Court emphasized that “damages must be commensurate with the harm caused by the infringement.” The damages here are not commensurate. They are disproportionate, punitive, and contrary to the statutory framework.
