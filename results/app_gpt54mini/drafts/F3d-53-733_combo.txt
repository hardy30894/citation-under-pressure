**ARGUMENT**

I. **The district court erred in admitting American Steamship’s “expert” cause testimony.**

Federal Rule of Evidence 702 permits opinion only when “scientific, technical, or other specialized knowledge” will assist the trier of fact. In *Frye v. United States*, 293 F. 1013 (D.C. Cir. 1923), the court recognized that expert opinion is limited to matters beyond ordinary experience. This Court likewise has long held that opinion evidence is inadmissible where the jury can draw the inference for itself. *United States v. Ball*, 163 U.S. 662 (1896); *United States v. Spaulding*, 106 U.S. 533 (1883). Here, the record showed a straightforward mechanical failure: a rope parted after Cook was lowered. Whether the line broke from heat, age, abrasion, or some other observable condition was within lay understanding, not specialized expertise. Admission of the witness’s opinion therefore invaded the jury’s province. See *Cox v. United States*, 96 U.S. 694 (1878); *The Conqueror*, 166 U.S. 110 (1897).

Even where expert testimony may be helpful, the testimony must be grounded in adequate facts and a reliable foundation. *Miller v. Brass Co.*, 104 U.S. 350 (1881); *The Havilah*, 10 Wall. 261 (1870). The witness here was not shown to have tested the line, inspected it for heat damage, or otherwise connected his conclusion to competent data. An opinion resting on speculation is not evidence. *Baldwin v. Hale*, 68 U.S. (1 Wall.) 223 (1863); *The Slavers*, 119 U.S. 407 (1886). Because the purported expert supplied nothing more than an unsupported conclusion about causation, the testimony should have been excluded.

II. **The court should have granted judgment as a matter of law on unseaworthiness.**

A vessel owner owes an absolute duty to provide a seaworthy vessel and appurtenances reasonably fit for their intended use. *The Osceola*, 189 U.S. 158 (1903); *Mahnich v. Southern S.S. Co.*, 321 U.S. 96 (1944); *Seas Shipping Co. v. Sieracki*, 328 U.S. 85 (1946). The warranty is not satisfied by showing due care; it is breached when an appliance fails in service. *Petterson v. Alaska S.S. Co.*, 347 U.S. 396 (1954); *Mitchell v. Trawler Racer, Inc.*, 362 U.S. 539 (1960). That is exactly what happened here. Cook used a line selected by the vessel, rigged in the ordinary course, and it parted while supporting him in a bos’n’s chair. A line that fails to bear the load for which it is employed is unseaworthy as a matter of law. *The Southwark*, 191 U.S. 1 (1903); *Crumady v. The J.H. Fisser*, 358 U.S. 423 (1959).

The evidence left no genuine factual dispute on this point. American Steamship did not show that the rope was fit for the task, only that it had been stored in a heated space and had been used before. But fitness, not prior use, controls. *Mahnich*, 321 U.S. at 100; *Mitchell*, 362 U.S. at 550. Once the line parted and Cook fell, the vessel’s unseaworthiness was established. The district court therefore erred in submitting that issue to the jury.

III. **The court properly denied Cook’s motion on comparative negligence.**

A Jones Act plaintiff may recover subject to comparative negligence principles. *Socony-Vacuum Oil Co. v. Smith*, 305 U.S. 424 (1939). The question is whether there was evidence from which a reasonable jury could conclude that Cook failed to act with ordinary prudence for his own safety. *Tiller v. Atlantic Coast Line R.R. Co.*, 318 U.S. 54 (1943); *Pfeifer v. Jones & Laughlin Steel Corp.*, 236 F.2d 1 (6th Cir. 1956). There was such evidence here. Cook personally worked on the boom, used a torch to free frozen bearings, and remained in the area while the line was being rigged. A jury could conclude that his own handling of the torch near the rigging area contributed to the failure or to the danger that produced his injuries. Courts do not direct a verdict where reasonable minds may differ. *Gunning v. Cooley*, 281 U.S. 90 (1930); *Brady v. Southern Ry. Co.*, 320 U.S. 476 (1943).

IV. **Evidence of Cook’s alcohol-related ailments was admissible.**

Relevant evidence is admissible if it has any tendency to make a consequential fact more or less probable. *Reynolds v. United States*, 98 U.S. 145 (1879); *United States v. Wood*, 14 Pet. 430 (1840). The defense theory was that Cook’s physical condition, including ailments associated with alcohol use, bore on his ability to perform deckhand duties, his judgment, and the extent to which prior medical problems may have affected his damages. That is proper substantive relevance, not impermissible character attack. See *Spies v. Illinois*, 123 U.S. 131 (1887). The district court also retained discretion to weigh prejudice against probative value, and appellate courts traditionally defer to that ruling absent clear abuse. *Aetna Life Ins. Co. v. Ward*, 140 U.S. 76 (1891); *The Caledonia*, 157 U.S. 124 (1895). No such abuse appears here.

For these reasons, the judgment should be vacated and the case remanded for a new trial.
