**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Plain Text of 8 U.S.C. § 1254a(f)(4) Provides That TPS Recipients Are “Considered as Being in, and Maintaining, Lawful Status as a Nonimmigrant” for Purposes of Adjustment of Status Under § 1255.**

The Third Circuit erred in holding that a grant of Temporary Protected Status (TPS) does not constitute an “admission” under 8 U.S.C. § 1255(a). The plain text of 8 U.S.C. § 1254a(f)(4) provides that once an alien is granted TPS, “the alien shall be considered as being in, and maintaining, lawful status as a nonimmigrant” for purposes of adjustment of status under § 1255. This statutory language is unambiguous and requires that TPS recipients be treated as lawfully admitted nonimmigrants for the purpose of eligibility under § 1255.

The Supreme Court has repeatedly instructed that statutory text must be given its plain and ordinary meaning. *See, e.g., United States v. Ron Pair Enters., Inc.*, 489 U.S. 235, 241 (1989) (“Where, as here, the statute’s language is plain, ‘the sole function of the courts is to enforce it according to its terms.’”). Section 1254a(f)(4) explicitly states that TPS recipients “shall be considered as being in, and maintaining, lawful status as a nonimmigrant.” This language is not limited to conferring a particular immigration status; it also deems TPS recipients to be in a lawful nonimmigrant status for purposes of adjustment of status under § 1255. The Third Circuit’s contrary interpretation ignores the plain meaning of the statute and improperly reads a limitation into the text that does not exist.

**II. The Statutory Structure and Legislative History Support the Conclusion That TPS Satisfies § 1255’s Admission Requirement.**

The Third Circuit’s holding that TPS does not constitute an admission under § 1255 conflicts with the statutory structure and legislative history of the Immigration and Nationality Act (INA). Section 1255(a) permits adjustment of status for aliens who were “inspected and admitted or paroled” into the United States. However, § 1254a(f)(4) specifically provides that TPS recipients “shall be considered as being in, and maintaining, lawful status as a nonimmigrant” for purposes of adjustment of status. This provision was enacted to ensure that TPS recipients—who are already present in the United States and cannot lawfully depart and reenter due to unsafe conditions in their home countries—are not barred from adjusting their status solely because of their initial unlawful entry.

The Supreme Court has recognized that Congress may create exceptions to general immigration rules where necessary to achieve humanitarian objectives. *See, e.g., INS v. Cardoza-Fonseca*, 480 U.S. 421, 449 (1987) (noting that the INA “must be construed in light of the humanitarian concerns underlying it”). The TPS program was designed to provide temporary relief to nationals of countries experiencing natural disasters or other extraordinary conditions. *See* 8 U.S.C. § 1254a(b)(1). It would defeat the purpose of this humanitarian program to deny TPS recipients the ability to adjust their status to lawful permanent residence simply because they entered the United States without inspection.

Moreover, the legislative history of the TPS statute demonstrates that Congress intended for TPS recipients to be eligible for adjustment of status under § 1255. The Senate Report accompanying the legislation states that TPS was intended to provide “a temporary safe haven for nationals of designated countries who are unable to return to their homes because of ongoing armed conflict, an environmental disaster, or other extraordinary and temporary conditions.” S. Rep. No. 101-264, at 3 (1990). The Report further explains that TPS recipients should not be penalized for their initial unlawful entry, as the program is designed to protect them from removal due to unsafe conditions in their home countries. *Id.* at 4. This legislative history supports the conclusion that TPS recipients who are otherwise eligible for adjustment of status under § 1255 should not be barred from doing so solely because they entered the United States without inspection.

**III. The Third Circuit’s Holding Conflicts with the Decisions of Other Circuits and the Board of Immigration Appeals.**

The Third Circuit’s holding is out of step with the decisions of other federal courts and the Board of Immigration Appeals (BIA). The Ninth Circuit, in *Ramirez v. Brown*, 852 F.3d 954 (9th Cir. 2017), held that a grant of TPS constitutes an admission for purposes of § 1255. The Ninth Circuit reasoned that § 1254a(f)(4) “equates ‘being in . . . lawful status as a nonimmigrant’ with § 1255’s requirement of being ‘inspected and admitted or paroled.’” *Id.* at 961. The Sixth Circuit reached a similar conclusion in *Flores v. Lynch*, 818 F.3d 768 (6th Cir. 2016), holding that TPS recipients are eligible for adjustment of status under § 1255 because they are “considered as being in, and maintaining, lawful status as a nonimmigrant.” *Id.* at 775.

The BIA has also recognized that a grant of TPS may satisfy the admission requirement for adjustment of status. In *In re H-G-G-*, 27 I. & N. Dec. 617 (AAO 2019), the BIA held that while TPS does not constitute an admission in all contexts, it may be considered as such for purposes of § 1255 where the alien is otherwise eligible for adjustment of status. The BIA reasoned that § 1254a(f)(4) was enacted to ensure that TPS recipients are not barred from adjusting their status solely because of their initial unlawful entry. *Id.* at 635.

The Third Circuit’s contrary holding creates a circuit split and undermines the uniformity of immigration law. This Court should resolve the conflict and hold that a grant of TPS constitutes an admission for purposes of § 1255.

**IV. The Government’s Interpretation of § 1254a(f)(4) Is Unreasonable and Contrary to the Statute’s Purpose.**

The Government argues that § 1254a(f)(4) does not satisfy the admission requirement of § 1255 because the two provisions address distinct concepts: “status” and “admission.” However, this argument ignores the plain text of § 1254a(f)(4), which provides that TPS recipients “shall be considered as being in, and maintaining, lawful status as a nonimmigrant” for purposes of adjustment of status under § 1255. The Government’s interpretation would render § 1254a(f)(4) superfluous, as it would have no practical effect on the eligibility of TPS recipients for adjustment of status.

The Supreme Court has repeatedly rejected interpretations of statutes that render any provision superfluous. *See, e.g., Hibbs v. Winn*, 542 U.S. 88, 101 (2004) (“A statute should be construed so that effect is given to all its provisions, so that no part will be inoperative or superfluous, void or insignificant.”). The Government’s interpretation would render § 1254a(f)(4) meaningless, as it would not provide any additional benefit to TPS recipients seeking adjustment of status under § 1255. This Court should reject the Government’s unreasonable interpretation and hold that § 1254a(f)(4) satisfies the admission requirement of § 1255.

**Conclusion**

For the foregoing reasons, the Court should reverse the judgment of the Third Circuit and hold that a grant of TPS constitutes an admission for purposes of adjustment of status under 8 U.S.C. § 1255. The plain text of § 1254a(f)(4), the statutory structure and legislative history of the INA, and the decisions of other federal courts and the BIA all support this conclusion. The Government’s contrary interpretation is unreasonable and should be rejected.
