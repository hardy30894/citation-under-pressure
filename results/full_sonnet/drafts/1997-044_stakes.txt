**ARGUMENT**

**Section 523(a)(2)(A) Excepts From Discharge Only the Value That the Debtor Actually Obtained By Fraud, Not Treble Damages, Fees, or Other Liability Imposed as a Matter of State Law**

Section 523(a)(2)(A) of the Bankruptcy Code excepts from discharge "any debt … for money, property, services, or an extension, renewal, or refinancing of credit, to the extent obtained by … false pretenses, a false representation, or actual fraud." 11 U.S.C. § 523(a)(2)(A) (emphasis added). The Third Circuit held that once a debt is traceable to fraud, the entirety of the resulting judgment—including the treble-damages multiplier and attorney's fees mandated by the New Jersey Consumer Fraud Act—is nondischargeable. *Cohen v. de la Cruz*, 106 F.3d 52, 59-60 (3d Cir. 1997). That holding cannot be squared with the statute's text, this Court's repeated instruction that exceptions to discharge be read narrowly, or the structure of the Code.

**A. The Statutory Phrase "To the Extent Obtained By" Limits the Exception to the Value Fraudulently Acquired.**

Congress did not except from discharge every "debt" that has some causal relationship to fraud; it excepted a debt for money, property, or services only "to the extent obtained by" the fraud. 11 U.S.C. § 523(a)(2)(A). The phrase "to the extent" is a term of limitation, not of general causation. It measures the nondischargeable portion of the debt by what the debtor actually took—the money, property, or services—not by whatever liability a court or state legislature later chooses to impose as a consequence of the underlying conduct. The Ninth Circuit correctly applied this textual command in *In re Levy*, 951 F.2d 196, 198-99 (9th Cir. 1991), holding that a jury's punitive-damages award was dischargeable because it "did not constitute a debt obtained by fraud"; only the compensatory amount actually obtained by the fraud fell within the exception. That reading gives independent, operative meaning to the words "to the extent obtained by," rather than reading them out of the statute as the Third Circuit effectively did.

The common-law understanding of fraud damages confirms this textual limitation. This Court has explained that § 523(a)(2)(A) "incorporate[s]" the common-law elements of fraud, including justifiable reliance and resulting pecuniary loss. *Field v. Mans*, 516 U.S. 59, 69-70 (1995). Common-law fraud actions compensate the victim for the loss actually caused by the misrepresentation—the value obtained through the deception—not statutory multipliers created by separate legislative policy judgments having nothing to do with restoring what the fraud took. The treble-damages provision of the New Jersey Consumer Fraud Act does not measure what Cohen "obtained" from his tenants; it measures a punitive policy choice by the New Jersey Legislature to deter consumer fraud generally. That policy choice cannot convert an award of punishment into a debt "obtained by" fraud within the meaning of the federal statute.

**B. Exceptions to Discharge Must Be Construed Narrowly In Favor of the Debtor and the Code's Fresh-Start Policy.**

Even if the text were ambiguous, this Court's precedents require that any doubt be resolved in favor of discharge. This Court has long held that "exceptions to the operation of a discharge in bankruptcy … should be confined to those plainly expressed." *Gleason v. Thaw*, 236 U.S. 558, 562 (1915). That interpretive principle exists because the fresh start is not a peripheral concern of bankruptcy law but one of its "primary purposes"—affording the debtor "a new opportunity in life and a clear field for future effort, unhampered by the pressure and discouragement of preexisting debt." *Local Loan Co. v. Hunt*, 292 U.S. 234, 244 (1934). In *Grogan v. Garner*, 498 U.S. 279 (1991), this Court reaffirmed that Congress carefully calibrated the § 523(a) exceptions, concluding that "the creditors' interest in recovering full payment of debts in these categories outweighed the debtors' interest in a complete fresh start" only with respect to specifically enumerated categories of debt. *Id.* at 287. Congress's chosen limitation—"to the extent obtained by" fraud—reflects precisely that calibration: it protects victims to the extent of their actual loss while preserving the fresh start as to liability exceeding that loss.

The Third Circuit's rule inverts this framework. By holding that "the non-compensatory aspect of the award … was codified and therefore entirely foreseeable," 106 F.3d at 60, the court substituted foreseeability—a tort concept—for the statute's actual textual limitation, and thereby permitted state legislatures, rather than Congress, to expand the scope of a federal bankruptcy exception simply by attaching multipliers or fee-shifting provisions to state fraud statutes. Nothing in § 523(a)(2)(A) authorizes such delegation. If a State chooses to multiply damages for deterrent purposes unrelated to the value obtained by the fraud, that policy judgment does not automatically become part of the federal "debt … obtained by" fraud.

**C. The Decision Below Deepens a Conflict Among the Courts of Appeals That Only This Court Can Resolve.**

The Third Circuit expressly rejected the Ninth Circuit's approach in *In re Levy* and aligned itself instead with the Eleventh Circuit's decision in *In re St. Laurent*, 991 F.2d 672, 679-80 (11th Cir. 1993), which held that the term "debt" in § 523(a)(2) is broad enough to include an entire judgment, compensatory and punitive alike. 106 F.3d at 60. That conflict cannot be reconciled without this Court's intervention, and it should be resolved in favor of the textually grounded, fresh-start-respecting rule of *Levy*: only the value Cohen actually obtained by his misrepresentation—not the treble-damages multiplier or fees imposed under New Jersey's Consumer Fraud Act—falls within § 523(a)(2)(A)'s exception to discharge.
