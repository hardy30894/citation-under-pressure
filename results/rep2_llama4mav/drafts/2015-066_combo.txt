The Sixth Amendment guarantees the right to counsel in criminal prosecutions. The question presented is whether an uncounseled conviction in tribal court can be used to satisfy the predicate offense requirement of 18 U.S.C. 117(a). The Ninth Circuit held that it cannot, relying on its prior decision in United States v. Ant. However, this holding is contrary to Supreme Court precedent and undermines the principles of comity and respect for tribal court judgments.

The use of prior convictions to enhance punishment for subsequent offenses is a long-standing practice. In Gryger v. Burke, 334 U.S. 728 (1948), the Court held that a recidivist statute does not violate the Due Process Clause, as it "does not impose a penalty for the earlier offense, but rather increases the punishment for the later offense based on the defendant's prior convictions." Id. at 732. Similarly, in McDonald v. Massachusetts, 180 U.S. 311 (1901), the Court upheld a recidivist statute, holding that it "is not a second punishment for the first offense, but rather a stiffened penalty for the latest crime, which is considered to be an aggravated offense because a repetitive one." Id. at 312.

The Indian Civil Rights Act (ICRA) governs tribal court proceedings, not the Sixth Amendment. As the Court held in Talton v. Mayes, 163 U.S. 376 (1896), the Fifth Amendment does not apply to Indian tribes, and by extension, neither does the Sixth Amendment. Id. at 384. Tribal courts are not required to provide the same procedural safeguards as federal or state courts. See Ex parte Crow Dog, 109 U.S. 556 (1883).

The Ninth Circuit's reliance on United States v. Ant is misplaced. Ant held that tribal court convictions may be used in subsequent prosecutions only if the tribal court guarantees a right to counsel that is coextensive with the Sixth Amendment right. However, this holding is contrary to the principles of comity and respect for tribal court judgments, as enunciated in Hilton v. Guyot, 159 U.S. 113 (1895). The Court in Hilton held that comity requires that foreign judgments be given effect, even if they were rendered under different procedural rules. Id. at 205.

Furthermore, the use of uncounseled tribal court convictions as predicate offenses under 18 U.S.C. 117(a) is consistent with the Supreme Court's precedent on the use of prior convictions. In Moore v. Michigan, 355 U.S. 155 (1957), the Court held that a prior conviction can be used to enhance punishment for a subsequent offense, even if the prior conviction was obtained without counsel. Id. at 161. Similarly, in Betts v. Brady, 316 U.S. 455 (1942), the Court held that the right to counsel is not absolute and that a conviction obtained without counsel can still be valid. Id. at 461.

The Ninth Circuit's decision creates a circuit split and undermines the effectiveness of 18 U.S.C. 117(a) in combating domestic violence on tribal lands. The decision should be reversed, and the use of uncounseled tribal court convictions as predicate offenses under 18 U.S.C. 117(a) should be upheld.

In conclusion, the Ninth Circuit's holding that an uncounseled conviction in tribal court cannot be used to satisfy the predicate offense requirement of 18 U.S.C. 117(a) is contrary to Supreme Court precedent and undermines the principles of comity and respect for tribal court judgments. The decision should be reversed, and the use of such convictions should be upheld, consistent with the holdings in Gryger v. Burke, McDonald v. Massachusetts, Talton v. Mayes, and other cases.
