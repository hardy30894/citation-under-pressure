# ARGUMENT

## I. The Canon of Constitutional Avoidance Requires Construing § 1231(a)(6) to Contain an Implicit Time Limit on Detention.

This Court has long adhered to "the cardinal principle of statutory construction that a statute be construed, if fairly possible, so as to avoid not only the conclusion that it is unconstitutional but also grave doubts upon that score." *Crowell v. Benson*, 285 U.S. 22, 62 (1932). Where a statute is susceptible of two constructions, "every reasonable construction must be resorted to, in order to save a statute from unconstitutionality." *Hooper v. California*, 155 U.S. 648, 657 (1895). Section 1231(a)(6) contains no express durational limit. If read to permit detention with no foreseeable endpoint, it raises exactly the kind of "grave doubts" this Court has always avoided by adopting a narrower reading. The statute should therefore be construed to authorize detention only for the period reasonably necessary to accomplish removal.

## II. Indefinite Civil Detention of a Lawfully Admitted Alien Violates the Due Process Guarantee That Protects All Persons Within the United States.

The Due Process Clause is not confined to citizens. Its "provisions are universal in their application, to all persons within the territorial jurisdiction." *Yick Wo v. Hopkins*, 118 U.S. 356, 369 (1886). Even as to aliens subject to expulsion, Congress may not act without regard to constitutional limits, for aliens "shall not be deprived of life, liberty, or property without due process of law." *Wong Wing v. United States*, 163 U.S. 228, 238 (1896). This Court there struck down a scheme of administrative imprisonment of aliens because detention amounting to punishment could not be imposed without the ordinary safeguards of due process.

Once an alien is lawfully admitted to residence, his liberty interest is at its zenith. As Justice Brandeis explained for the Court, "[t]o deport one who so claims to be a citizen, obviously deprives him of liberty. . . . It may result also in loss of both property and life; or of all that makes life worth living." *Ng Fung Ho v. White*, 259 U.S. 276, 284 (1922). The same is true, a fortiori, of prolonged physical confinement of a resident alien who cannot be removed. This Court has likewise held that "once an alien lawfully enters and resides in this country he becomes invested with the rights guaranteed by the Constitution to all people within our borders." *Kwong Hai Chew v. Colding*, 344 U.S. 590, 596 (1953). Zadvydas was admitted to permanent residence in 1956 and has lived in this country for decades; he is precisely the kind of alien to whom *Kwong Hai Chew*'s protection extends.

Indefinite, potentially life-long detention untethered to any prospect of removal is not detention "reasonably necessary" to the deportation process—it is preventive detention of a person the Government cannot deport. Such open-ended confinement, imposed by executive determination alone, without any judicial adjudication of dangerousness comparable to ordinary civil-commitment safeguards, cannot be squared with the liberty protected in *Wong Wing* and *Ng Fung Ho*.

## III. The Entry Fiction of *Shaughnessy v. Mezei* Does Not Extend to a Lawfully Admitted Resident Alien Like Zadvydas.

The Fifth Circuit relied heavily on *Shaughnessy v. United States ex rel. Mezei*, 345 U.S. 206 (1953), but that case involved an alien excluded at the border who had never effected entry into the United States and who, under the entry fiction, was treated as if he remained outside the country notwithstanding his physical presence on Ellis Island. That fiction has never been applied to aliens who, like Zadvydas, were lawfully admitted and resided here for years before removal proceedings began. This Court has expressly distinguished the excludable alien "stopped at the border" from the resident alien who, upon return from a temporary absence abroad, is treated "as though he had never left" the country and retains full due process protection. *Kwong Hai Chew*, 344 U.S. at 596 & n.5. Zadvydas is not an excludable alien seeking initial entry; he is a resident alien whose only "exclusion" is from a country that will not take him back. *Mezei*'s narrow holding for border-exclusion cases cannot be stretched to cover him.

## IV. Detention Incident to Deportation Must Bear a Reasonable Relationship to an Achievable Removal.

Even where detention of aliens pending deportation has been sustained, this Court has grounded that holding in the premise that deportation itself remained a real and impending event. In *Carlson v. Landon*, 342 U.S. 524 (1952), the Court upheld detention because "[d]etention is necessarily a part of the deportation procedure," id. at 538—a procedure that, by definition, contemplates an endpoint. Where, as here, no country will accept the alien and removal is not merely delayed but not reasonably foreseeable, detention no longer serves that statutory purpose; it becomes an open-ended deprivation of liberty divorced from any deportation actually "necessary" to secure.

## CONCLUSION

Reading § 1231(a)(6) to permit detention with no reasonably foreseeable end raises the gravest constitutional doubts under *Wong Wing*, *Ng Fung Ho*, and *Kwong Hai Chew*, doubts this Court's avoidance canon, articulated in *Crowell v. Benson* and *Hooper v. California*, requires it to resolve by construing the statute to contain an implicit, judicially enforceable reasonable-time limitation. The judgment of the Court of Appeals should be reversed.
