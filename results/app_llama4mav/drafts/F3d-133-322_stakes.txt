ARGUMENT

I. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN CONVICTING MRS. PARKER OF CONSPIRACY TO COMMIT PUBLIC BRIBERY AND PUBLIC BRIBERY UNDER 18 U.S.C. § 201(b)(2)(C) BECAUSE SHE LACKED THE AUTHORITY TO GRANT OR DENY BENEFITS.

Mrs. Parker argues that her conviction under 18 U.S.C. § 201(b)(2)(C) was improper because she did not have the authority to grant or deny benefits. The statute prohibits public officials from being induced to do or omit any act in violation of their official duty. The key issue is whether Mrs. Parker's actions, although outside her specific authority, can be considered a violation of her official duty.

The definition of "official act" under 18 U.S.C. § 201(a)(3) is broad, encompassing "any decision or action on any question, matter, cause, suit, proceeding or controversy, which may at any time be pending, or which may by law be brought before any public official, in such official's official capacity, or in such official's place of trust or profit." As the Senate Report notes, this definition reflects Congress' intent to "include any decision or action taken by a public official in his capacity as such." S.Rep. No. 87-2213, (1962), reprinted in 1962 U.S.C.C.A.N. 3852, 3856.

However, the Supreme Court has clarified that official acts that violate an official's duty are not limited to those within the official's specific authority. United States v. Birdsall, 233 U.S. 223, 231 (1914). In Birdsall, the Court held that duties not completely defined by written rules are established by settled practice, and action taken in the course of their performance must be regarded as within the provisions of the statutes against bribery. Id. 

The Sixth Circuit has also held that an official's actions can be considered a violation of their official duty even if they are not within their specific authority. United States v. Gjieli, 717 F.2d 968, 972 (6th Cir. 1983). However, the government must still prove that Mrs. Parker's actions were taken in her official capacity and were related to a matter that could be brought before her or ALJ Aronson.

In this case, Mrs. Parker's duties included assisting ALJ Aronson and typing and mailing his decisions. Although she was not authorized to approve benefits or sign ALJ Aronson's name, her actions in forging ALJ Aronson's signature and demanding money for favorable treatment can be seen as a violation of her official duty. The fact that she had access to the office's computer system and was able to manipulate documents related to benefits claims further supports this conclusion.

Therefore, the district court did not err in convicting Mrs. Parker of conspiracy to commit public bribery and public bribery under 18 U.S.C. § 201(b)(2)(C).

II. THE CONVICTIONS OF MR. PARKER SHOULD BE REVERSED BECAUSE THE EVIDENCE WAS INSUFFICIENT TO PROVE HIS INVOLVEMENT IN THE CONSPIRACY AND PUBLIC BRIBERY.

The evidence presented at trial was insufficient to prove Mr. Parker's involvement in the conspiracy and public bribery. The only evidence linking Mr. Parker to the scheme was his presence at the meeting with Simmons where he accepted $500, and Mrs. Parker's statements implicating him. However, this evidence is circumstantial and does not directly prove Mr. Parker's involvement in the conspiracy or public bribery.

To sustain a conviction under 18 U.S.C. § 201(b)(2)(C), the government must prove that Mr. Parker knowingly participated in the bribery scheme. The government failed to meet this burden, and therefore, Mr. Parker's convictions should be reversed.

In conclusion, while Mrs. Parker's conviction was proper, Mr. Parker's convictions should be reversed due to insufficient evidence.
