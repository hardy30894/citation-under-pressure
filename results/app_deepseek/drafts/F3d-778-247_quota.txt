**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Search Warrant Failed to Establish a Nexus Between the Alleged Crimes and the Place to Be Searched.**

The Fourth Amendment requires that a warrant application establish probable cause to believe that evidence of a crime will be found at the specific location to be searched. *United States v. Feliz*, 182 F.3d 82, 87 (1st Cir. 1999). The affidavit here provided no information linking Joubert’s parents’ home to any criminal activity. While the affiant stated that persons engaged in child exploitation often retain evidence at their residences, *see United States v. Tanguay*, 787 F.3d 44, 52 (1st Cir. 2015), the affidavit lacked any “nexus between the places to be searched and the evidence sought.” *United States v. Werra*, 638 F.3d 326, 337 (1st Cir. 2011). SJ’s report that Joubert disassembled a computer at his parents’ home and stored components there did not establish that the computer contained contraband; it showed only that he destroyed a computer. Moreover, the affidavit’s boilerplate statement that child molesters “often maintain possession” of evidence is insufficient without particularized facts tying the place to criminal activity. *See United States v. McLellan*, 792 F.3d 200, 212 (1st Cir. 2015). The warrant was therefore invalid.

**II. The District Court Abused Its Discretion by Admitting Evidence of Uncharged Child Molestation.**

Federal Rule of Evidence 404(b) prohibits evidence of other crimes to prove character or propensity. *United States v. Varoudakis*, 233 F.3d 113, 118 (1st Cir. 2000). The district court admitted testimony from multiple witnesses about uncharged molestations—a classic propensity use. This evidence was not “specially probative” of any legitimate issue, such as intent or plan, because the charged conduct (sexual exploitation under § 2251(a)) required no proof of intent beyond the voluntary act of creating the images. *See United States v. Adams*, 375 F.3d 108, 113 (1st Cir. 2004). The testimony’s prejudicial effect far outweighed any probative value, violating Rule 403. *United States v. Marek*, 548 F.3d 147, 154 (1st Cir. 2008). The error was not harmless, as the government’s case heavily relied on this propensity evidence.

**III. The Commerce Clause Does Not Support Application of 18 U.S.C. § 2251(a) and § 2252A(a)(5)(B) to Joubert’s Intrastate, Noncommercial Conduct.**

Congress may regulate intrastate activity only if it substantially affects interstate commerce. *United States v. Lopez*, 514 U.S. 549, 558–59 (1995). Section 2251(a) criminalizes the production of child pornography, but the government presented no evidence that Joubert’s conduct—videotaping KC in a private home—involved any interstate commerce element. The VHS tape was never transported, sold, or transmitted across state lines. *See United States v. Morrison*, 529 U.S. 598, 613 (2000) (rejecting aggregation theory where noneconomic activity is at issue). While some circuits have upheld § 2251(a) under the Commerce Clause, *see, e.g., United States v. Galo*, 239 F.3d 572, 575 (3d Cir. 2001), this Court should follow the logic of *Lopez* and *Morrison*: purely intrastate, noncommercial production of child pornography does not substantially affect interstate commerce. Similarly, possession under § 2252A(a)(5)(B) requires a jurisdictional nexus—here, the material was not “produced using materials that have been shipped in interstate commerce,” as the government failed to prove. *United States v. Ramos*, 685 F.3d 120, 127 (1st Cir. 2012). The convictions must be reversed.

**IV. The 480-Month Sentence Is Substantively Unreasonable.**

This Court reviews substantive reasonableness for abuse of discretion. *United States v. Martin*, 520 F.3d 87, 91 (1st Cir. 2008). The 480-month sentence—40 years—is effectively a life sentence and far exceeds the Guidelines range, which was erroneously calculated. The district court failed to adequately consider the § 3553(a) factors, including Joubert’s age, lack of prior convictions, and the non-violent nature of the offense. *See United States v. Rivera*, 803 F.3d 60, 64 (1st Cir. 2015). The sentence is “grossly disproportionate” and constitutes an abuse of discretion. *United States v. Merced*, 603 F.3d 103, 106 (1st Cir. 2010).
