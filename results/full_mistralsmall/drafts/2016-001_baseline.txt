**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Patent Act’s Text and History Require That Damages for Design Patent Infringement Be Limited to the Portion of the Infringer’s Profits Attributable to the Infringed Design.**

The Patent Act provides that a design patent infringer “shall be liable to the owner to the extent of his total profit,” 35 U.S.C. § 289, but this Court has long held that the statute’s reference to “total profit” is not a command to award all profits from the entire product. *See* *Dobson v. Dornan*, 118 U.S. 10, 14 (1886) (limiting damages to the “increase of business” attributable to the infringement). The Court has repeatedly cautioned that damages must be tied to the value of the patented invention, not the entire product. *See* *General Motors Corp. v. Devex Corp.*, 461 U.S. 648, 654 (1983) (holding that prejudgment interest must be “limited to the infringement-related portion of the infringer’s profits”).

The Federal Circuit’s contrary rule—allowing damages for the entire product’s profits when only a component is patented—is inconsistent with the Patent Act’s text and this Court’s precedents. *See* *Apple Inc. v. Samsung Elec. Co.*, 786 F.3d 983, 996 (Fed. Cir. 2015) (awarding total profits from smartphones for infringement of a single design patent covering a front face). The Federal Circuit’s rule conflates the patented design with the entire product, ignoring that a design patent covers only an “ornamental” or “decorative” aspect of a product. *See* *Egyptian Goddess, Inc. v. Swisa, Inc.*, 543 F.3d 665, 678 (Fed. Cir. 2008) (en banc) (holding that design patents protect “the visual appearance” of an article of manufacture).

This Court should clarify that damages under § 289 must be limited to the portion of the infringer’s profits attributable to the infringed design. *See* *Dobson*, 118 U.S. at 14* (“The increase of business thus occasioned is the measure of damages.”); *Devex*, 461 U.S. at 654* (damages must be “limited to the infringement-related portion”).

**II. The Federal Circuit’s Rule Permits Windfalls to Patent Holders and Unjust Punishment of Infringers.**

The Federal Circuit’s rule allows patent holders to recover damages far exceeding the value of their patented designs, creating a windfall that Congress did not intend. *See* *Dobson*, 118 U.S. at 14* (limiting damages to the “increase of business” attributable to the infringement). It also punishes infringers disproportionately, as the profits from an entire product (e.g., a smartphone) vastly exceed the value of a single component (e.g., a front face design). *See* *Apple*, 786 F.3d at 996* (awarding $399 million for infringement of a design covering a fraction of a smartphone).

This Court has repeatedly rejected rules that permit disproportionate damages. *See* *Devex*, 461 U.S. at 654* (“[P]rejudgment interest must be limited to the infringement-related portion of the infringer’s profits.”). The Federal Circuit’s rule similarly permits disproportionate damages, as it allows recovery of the entire product’s profits for infringement of a single design. *See* *Apple*, 786 F.3d at 996*.

**III. The Federal Circuit’s Rule Is Inconsistent with This Court’s Precedents Limiting Damages to the Infringement-Related Portion of Profits.**

This Court has long held that damages must be limited to the portion of profits attributable to the infringement. *See* *Dobson*, 118 U.S. at 14* (“The increase of business thus occasioned is the measure of damages.”); *Devex*, 461 U.S. at 654* (“[P]rejudgment interest must be limited to the infringement-related portion of the infringer’s profits.”). The Federal Circuit’s rule permitting recovery of the entire product’s profits for infringement of a single design is inconsistent with these precedents. *See* *Apple*, 786 F.3d at 996*.

**IV. The Federal Circuit’s Rule Creates Uncertainty and Encourages Excessive Litigation.**

The Federal Circuit’s rule creates uncertainty by allowing damages to swing wildly depending on whether a design patent covers a component or the entire product. *See* *Apple*, 786 F.3d at 996* (awarding $399 million for infringement of a design covering a fraction of a smartphone). This uncertainty encourages excessive litigation, as parties are incentivized to fight over whether a design patent covers a component or the entire product. *See* *Dobson*, 118 U.S. at 14* (limiting damages to the “increase of business” attributable to the infringement).

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the Court should reverse the Federal Circuit’s judgment and hold that damages for design patent infringement must be limited to the portion of the infringer’s profits attributable to the infringed design.
