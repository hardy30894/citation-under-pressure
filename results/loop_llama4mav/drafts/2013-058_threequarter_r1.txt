The Seventh Circuit's decision in Clark v. Rameker correctly held that an inherited IRA is not exempt from a debtor's estate in bankruptcy under Section 522 of the U.S. Bankruptcy Code. The plain meaning of the statute, combined with a long line of judicial precedent, supports this conclusion.

The Bankruptcy Code exempts "retirement funds to the extent that those funds are in a fund or account that is exempt from taxation under section... 408 ... of the Internal Revenue Code of 1986." 11 U.S.C. §522(b)(3)(C). The key issue is whether an inherited IRA constitutes "retirement funds" for the beneficiary. As the Seventh Circuit correctly observed, an inherited IRA is not a retirement fund for the beneficiary because it is not held for their retirement.

The Supreme Court has long recognized that exemptions in bankruptcy law should be narrowly construed. In Crawford v. Burke, 195 U.S. 176 (1904), the Court discussed the principles guiding the interpretation of exemptions. The Court stated that exemptions are to be construed with reference to the context and purpose of the statute. Id. at 190.

Similarly, in Gleason v. Thaw, 236 U.S. 558 (1915), the Court emphasized the importance of adhering to the plain meaning of the statute when interpreting exemptions. The Court held that "the provisions of the Bankruptcy Act should be construed with reference to the well-known rules of equity jurisprudence." Id. at 562.

Other courts have also recognized the importance of narrowly construing exemptions. In Re Lippitt, 229 F. 854 (D.C. Ohio 1915), the court held that exemptions should be interpreted in a way that is consistent with their purpose and the overall scheme of the Bankruptcy Code. See also Schwartz v. Holzman, 69 F.2d 814 (2d Cir. 1934); Lockwood v. Exchange Bank, 190 U.S. 294 (1903); Chicago, B. & Q.R. Co. v. Hall, 229 U.S. 511 (1913).

In Chicago, B. & Q.R. Co. v. Hall, the Supreme Court noted that exemptions should be carefully considered in light of the statutory context. 229 U.S. at 516. Exempting inherited IRAs from the bankruptcy estate would be inconsistent with this goal, as it would allow debtors to shield assets that are not truly retirement funds.

The Seventh Circuit's decision is consistent with this long line of precedent. By holding that an inherited IRA is not exempt from the bankruptcy estate, the court ensured that the Bankruptcy Code is applied in a fair and equitable manner. The decision should be affirmed.

In conclusion, the Seventh Circuit's decision in Clark v. Rameker is supported by a long line of judicial precedent and is consistent with the plain meaning of the Bankruptcy Code. The decision should be affirmed, and inherited IRAs should not be exempt from a debtor's estate in bankruptcy.
