**ARGUMENT**

**I. Johnson's Invalidation of the Residual Clause Announced a Substantive Rule That Voids Petitioner's Sentence Ab Initio**

Since *Ex parte Siebold*, 100 U.S. 371 (1880), this Court has recognized that "[a]n unconstitutional law is void, and is as no law. An offence created by it is not a crime. A conviction under it is not merely erroneous, but is illegal and void, and cannot be a legal cause of imprisonment." *Id.* at 376–77. The residual clause of the Armed Career Criminal Act suffered from precisely this infirmity: like the statute condemned in *Connally v. General Construction Co.*, 269 U.S. 385 (1926), it required "men of common intelligence" to "guess at its meaning," *id.* at 391, leaving petitioner unable to know in advance whether his prior conviction would be deemed a "violent felony." That defect is not a mere trial error; it is a failure of the law itself to exist as law. As *Norton v. Shelby County*, 118 U.S. 425 (1886), explained of unconstitutional enactments generally, such a provision "confers no rights; it imposes no duties; it affords no protection; it creates no office; it is, in legal contemplation, as inoperative as though it had never been passed." *Id.* at 442. A fifteen-year mandatory minimum imposed under a legally nonexistent clause is void from the moment it is imposed, not merely voidable on appeal.

This distinguishes Johnson's rule from an ordinary procedural correction. The vagueness holding did not merely regulate the manner of proof; it stripped the residual clause of any authority to enhance a sentence at all. Under *Siebold*, a prisoner held pursuant to a sentence resting on a void enactment "cannot be legally detained," 100 U.S. at 377, because "the question is not one of mere error, but of the power of the court to render judgment at all," *id.* at 375–76. Petitioner's sentence rests on exactly that kind of void authority: the residual clause never validly reached his prior Florida conviction, because the clause never validly reached anything.

**II. Retroactivity Doctrine Confirms That Rules Affecting the Very Power to Punish Must Be Given Retroactive Effect**

This Court's retroactivity jurisprudence has never treated all new constitutional rules alike. In *Linkletter v. Walker*, 381 U.S. 618 (1965), the Court held that "the Constitution neither prohibits nor requires retrospective effect" for new rules, *id.* at 629, and directed courts to weigh, as crystallized in *Stovall v. Denno*, 388 U.S. 293 (1967), "(a) the purpose to be served by the new standards, (b) the extent of the reliance by law enforcement authorities on the old standards, and (c) the effect on the administration of justice of a retroactive application of the new standards." *Id.* at 297. Each factor favors retroactivity here.

First, the purpose served by Johnson's rule is the vindication of due process's most basic guarantee—fair notice of what conduct the law punishes. See *Lanzetta v. New Jersey*, 306 U.S. 451, 453 (1939) ("No one may be required at peril of life, liberty or property to speculate as to the meaning of penal statutes."). A rule vindicating that guarantee bears directly on the fairness of the proceeding that produced the sentence, because it determines whether the sentencing court possessed any lawful basis to enhance punishment at all. In *Tehan v. United States ex rel. Shott*, 382 U.S. 406 (1966), this Court distinguished rules that "go[] to the fairness of the trial process itself" from rules that merely regulate the mechanics of proof, recognizing that the former warrant fuller retroactive application. *Id.* at 416–17.

Second, reliance interests cannot justify continued enforcement of sentences resting on a clause this Court has condemned as unconstitutionally vague. Reliance by sentencing courts on the residual clause is not a legitimate interest the retroactivity inquiry protects, because, as *Siebold* teaches, a void law "affords no protection" to the judgments entered under it. 118 U.S. at 442. Reliance on an admittedly void source of authority does not become a reason to preserve its consequences.

Third, the effect on the administration of justice favors retroactivity: unlike rules requiring wholesale retrial of guilt, correcting an ACCA enhancement requires only resentencing of a discrete and identifiable class of prisoners whose sentences rest on the same infirm clause. That modest administrative burden cannot outweigh the injustice of leaving individuals imprisoned beyond the term the law actually authorizes.

**III. Continued Detention Under a Void Enhancement Offends Due Process**

Finally, the due process concerns that motivated the vagueness holding in *Connally* and *Lanzetta* apply with full force to continued imprisonment. A statute that fails to give fair warning of what it punishes cannot be salvaged retroactively merely because a defendant's sentence became final before this Court identified the defect. See *Connally*, 269 U.S. at 391. To hold otherwise would perpetuate confinement based on the fiction that the residual clause once validly authorized what it never had power to authorize at all. As *Norton* makes clear, a void enactment is "as inoperative as though it had never been passed," 118 U.S. at 442—a principle that applies with equal force whether the challenge arrives on direct appeal or, as here, on collateral review.

For these reasons, the rule announced in *Johnson* applies retroactively to cases, like petitioner's, on collateral review.
