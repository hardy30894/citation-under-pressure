**ARGUMENT**

**I. The ADEA Does Not Apply to Appointed State Judges Because They Are “Appointee[s] on the Policymaking Level.”**

The Age Discrimination in Employment Act defines “employee” to exclude “any person chosen by such officer to be on such officer’s personal staff, or an appointee on the policymaking level.” 29 U.S.C. § 630(f). Appointed state judges fall squarely within this exclusion.

Judges indisputably make policy. As the Eighth Circuit recognized, “policymaking is indisputably a part of the function of judging to the extent that judging involves lawmaking to fill the interstices of authority found in constitutions, statutes, and precedents.” *EEOC v. Massachusetts*, 858 F.2d 52, 55 (1st Cir. 1988). When judges decide cases, they “establish either general standards or specific rules of decision that may be relied upon in the future.” *Gregory v. Ashcroft*, 898 F.2d 598, 602 (8th Cir. 1990). The Missouri Supreme Court, for example, has adopted a system of comparative negligence, *Gustafson v. Benda*, 661 S.W.2d 11 (Mo. 1983) (en banc), and resolved profound ethical questions regarding the withdrawal of life-sustaining treatment, *Cruzan v. Harmon*, 760 S.W.2d 408 (Mo. 1988) (en banc). These are quintessentially policymaking functions.

Moreover, Missouri judges exercise substantial supervisory and administrative policymaking authority. The Chief Justice of the Missouri Supreme Court, the Chief Judges of the court of appeals, and the Presiding Judges of the circuit courts each have “general supervisory powers.” Mo. Const. art. V, § 4. The Missouri Supreme Court adopts rules governing judicial and attorney conduct and resolves disputes within the judicial hierarchy. *See In re Rules of Circuit Court for Twenty-First Judicial Circuit*, 702 S.W.2d 457 (Mo. 1985) (en banc). These responsibilities confirm that judges operate “on the policymaking level.”

Petitioners’ argument that the “policymaking level” exception applies only to executive and legislative appointees is unavailing. If that were true, “none of the four categories of exemptions from the coverage of the ADEA would apply to employees in the judicial branch in those states where judges are appointed.” *Gregory*, 898 F.2d at 603. Congress could not have intended to exclude elected judges entirely from ADEA coverage while providing no exemption for appointed judges. *Id.* The more natural reading is that appointed judges, who exercise the same powers and responsibilities as elected judges, are “appointee[s] on the policymaking level.”

Because Congress did not “explicitly and unequivocally manifest[] its intent to preempt state law” in this traditionally sensitive area of state sovereignty, *United States v. Bass*, 404 U.S. 336, 349 (1971), the ADEA does not override Missouri’s constitutional retirement provision.

**II. Missouri’s Mandatory Retirement Provision Does Not Violate the Equal Protection Clause.**

The Fourteenth Amendment’s Equal Protection Clause requires only that a classification bear a rational relationship to a legitimate state interest when, as here, it does not burden a suspect class or fundamental right. *Massachusetts Bd. of Retirement v. Murgia*, 427 U.S. 307, 312 (1976) (per curiam). Age is not a suspect classification, *id.* at 313-14, and there is no fundamental right to continued public employment, *id.* at 313.

Missouri’s mandatory retirement age of 70 for judges satisfies rational basis review. The state has legitimate interests in ensuring that judges possess the “mental and physical stamina” necessary for the demanding work of judging, *Murgia*, 427 U.S. at 315 n.6, and in “eliminat[ing] the unpleasantness of selectively removing aged . . . judges,” *Malmed v. Thornburgh*, 621 F.2d 565, 572 (3d Cir. 1980). Mandatory retirement also “reduces delays in the administration of justice caused by death or disabling illness of sitting judges,” *Trafelet v. Thompson*, 594 F.2d 623, 629 (7th Cir.), *cert. denied*, 444 U.S. 906 (1979), and “eliminates the anguish, time, delay, expense, and embarrassment of the supervision and removal of older judges of failing competence,” *Apkin v. Treasurer and Receiver Gen.*, 517 N.E.2d 141, 146 (Mass. 1988). These are all rational bases for the classification.

Petitioners may disagree with the wisdom of these rationales, but that is irrelevant. Under rational basis review, a court may not “sit as a superlegislature to judge the wisdom or desirability of legislative policy determinations.” *Murgia*, 427 U.S. at 316 (quoting *San Antonio School Dist. v. Rodriguez*, 411 U.S. 1, 35 (1973)). Because Missouri’s mandatory retirement provision is rationally related to legitimate state interests, it does not violate the Equal Protection Clause.

**CONCLUSION**

The judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
