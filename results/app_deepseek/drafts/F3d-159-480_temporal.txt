## ARGUMENT

### A. Congress Lacked Constitutional Authority to Enact 18 U.S.C. § 1951 as Applied to This Case

The Hobbs Act, 18 U.S.C. § 1951, criminalizes robbery that “in any way or degree” affects interstate commerce. As applied to Dominic Pearson’s conviction for robbing a single local restaurant in Wichita, Kansas, this statute exceeds Congress’s power under the Commerce Clause. The robbery was a purely intrastate crime with only an attenuated and speculative connection to interstate commerce. Under this Court’s pre-1970 precedents, such a minimal nexus is insufficient to sustain federal jurisdiction.

The Commerce Clause empowers Congress to regulate only those intrastate activities that have a “substantial effect” on interstate commerce. *Schechter Poultry Corp. v. United States*, 295 U.S. 495, 546 (1935) (striking down federal regulation of intrastate poultry sales because they did not “directly” affect interstate commerce). In *Carter v. Carter Coal Co.*, 298 U.S. 238, 308 (1936), the Court held that mining—a local activity—could not be regulated under the commerce power because its effect on interstate commerce was “indirect” and “remote.” And in *United States v. E.C. Knight Co.*, 156 U.S. 1, 12 (1895), the Court declared that “manufacturing is not commerce” and thus lies beyond Congress’s reach. These cases establish that the Commerce Clause does not extend to every local act that may *incidentally* touch interstate markets.

The government’s evidence here falls far short of showing a substantial effect. Mr. Goodcents is a local franchise that purchases some goods from out-of-state suppliers. The stolen money, the owner testified, “would have been used to purchase goods produced outside of Kansas.” That is pure speculation. The robbery did not close the business, disrupt supply chains, or otherwise impede interstate commerce. At most, the government proved that the restaurant was part of a national chain—a fact true of countless local businesses. If that alone satisfied the Hobbs Act, Congress would have “the power to regulate any local activity that has any connection, however remote, to interstate commerce.” That is precisely the kind of overreach the Court rejected in *Schechter* and *Carter Coal*.

The government may rely on *United States v. Green*, 350 U.S. 415 (1956), which upheld the Hobbs Act as applied to extortion that forced a business to close, thereby directly affecting interstate commerce. But *Green* did not hold that every robbery of a business that deals in interstate goods falls within the Act. To the contrary, the Court emphasized that the extortion “actually and realistically” interfered with interstate commerce. *Id.* at 420. Here, the robbery had no such concrete effect. The restaurant remained open; the stolen cash was never shown to have been destined for interstate purchases. The government’s theory—that any robbery of a business that buys out-of-state goods affects commerce—would make every local theft a federal crime. That cannot be squared with the limited, enumerated powers of Congress.

Moreover, the Hobbs Act’s “in any way or degree” language cannot expand Congress’s authority beyond constitutional limits. As the Court stated in *United States v. Five Gambling Devices*, 346 U.S. 441, 448 (1953) (plurality opinion), “the power of Congress to regulate commerce among the several States is not unlimited.” The Act must be construed to avoid constitutional doubt. When applied to a purely local robbery with only a de minimis effect on interstate commerce, the statute violates the Tenth Amendment’s reservation of powers to the states. *See Hammer v. Dagenhart*, 247 U.S. 251, 275 (1918) (holding that Congress cannot “in the exercise of the power to regulate commerce” invade “the reserved power of the States”), *overruled on other grounds by United States v. Darby*, 312 U.S. 100 (1941). Even after *Darby*, the principle remains that Congress may not regulate “purely local” activities that lack a “substantial effect” on interstate commerce. *Darby*, 312 U.S. at 119–20.

Here, the robbery of a single Wichita restaurant is a quintessentially local crime. The government’s evidence of interstate commerce—the restaurant’s out-of-state purchases and the speculative use of stolen funds—is too attenuated to satisfy the substantial-effect test. Accordingly, 18 U.S.C. § 1951, as applied to Mr. Pearson, exceeds Congress’s commerce power, and his convictions must be reversed.
