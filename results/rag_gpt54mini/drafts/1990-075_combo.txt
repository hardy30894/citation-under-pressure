The Controlled Substances Act does not unconstitutionally delegate legislative power. The Act supplies an intelligible principle, detailed substantive criteria, and procedural safeguards that cabin the Attorney General’s discretion. See J.W. Hampton, Jr., & Co. v. United States, 276 U.S. 394, 409 (1928); Yakus v. United States, 321 U.S. 414, 423-24 (1944); Carlson v. Landon, 342 U.S. 524, 542-44 (1952); Fahey v. Mallonee, 332 U.S. 245, 250 (1947). Congress’s power to regulate dangerous drugs is especially broad in the public-health sphere. See Minnesota Ex Rel. Whipple v. Martinson, 256 U.S. 41 (1921).

The statute here is far more definite than the delegations this Court has repeatedly sustained. Congress identified the scheduling framework, specified the factors to be considered, required a binding scientific and medical evaluation from HHS, and mandated APA notice-and-comment procedures before permanent scheduling. 21 U.S.C. § 811(a)-(c). This is precisely the kind of delegation upheld where Congress “enjoin[s] upon” the agency “a certain course of procedure and certain rules of decision.” Wichita Railroad & Light Co. v. Public Utilities Commission of Kansas, 260 U.S. 48 (1922). The Act resembles the regulatory schemes sustained in Yakus, 321 U.S. at 423-27, and Carlson, 342 U.S. at 542-44, where broad discretion was limited by statutory standards and judicially manageable criteria. It is also consistent with Mistretta v. United States, 488 U.S. 361, 372, 374-79 (1989), which reaffirmed that Congress may delegate when it provides an intelligible principle and meaningful constraints.

Petitioners’ reliance on Panama Refining Co. v. Ryan, 293 U.S. 388 (1935), and A.L.A. Schechter Poultry Corp. v. United States, 295 U.S. 495 (1935), is misplaced. Those cases involved delegations without adequate policy, standards, or bounds. Panama Refining, 293 U.S. at 415; Schechter Poultry, 295 U.S. at 541-42. Here, by contrast, Congress gave the Attorney General a tightly circumscribed emergency authority to address “an imminent hazard to the public safety.” That is a classic example of delegation to fill in details and respond to changing conditions in a technical field. See Yakus, 321 U.S. at 425-26; Mistretta, 488 U.S. at 372-75.

The nondelegation challenge is even weaker because the temporary scheduling authority is limited and transient. Congress enacted a one-year order, extendable only for six months, and required notice and consultation. Such temporary administrative measures fall well within accepted constitutional bounds. See Flemming v. Florida Citrus Exchange, 358 U.S. 153, 163-64 (1958); Abbott Laboratories v. Gardner, 387 U.S. 136, 151-52 (1967); Gardner v. Toilet Goods Assn., Inc., 387 U.S. 167, 170-71 (1967). Congress may also rely on administrative expertise where, as here, the regulated subject is highly technical and fast-moving. See Fahey, 332 U.S. at 250; Mistretta, 488 U.S. at 372.

Nor did the Attorney General exceed his authority by delegating temporary scheduling to the DEA Administrator. Congress expressly authorized the Attorney General to delegate functions under the Controlled Substances Act. 21 U.S.C. § 871(a). When Congress gives the head of an agency authority to act, a subdelegation to a responsible subordinate is ordinarily permissible absent a contrary indication. See District of Columbia v. John R. Thompson Co., 346 U.S. 100, 105-06 (1953); Cudahy Packing Co. v. Holland, 315 U.S. 357, 361-63 (1942). Congress knows how to forbid delegation when it wishes to do so. Cudahy Packing, 315 U.S. at 361-63. Nothing in § 811(h) or § 871(a) withholds that ordinary authority.

Indeed, the structure of the Act confirms the propriety of subdelegation. The Attorney General remains responsible for the regulatory judgment; the DEA Administrator merely acts as the Attorney General’s delegate within the Department. That is a familiar administrative arrangement. See John R. Thompson Co., 346 U.S. at 105-06; Honolulu Rapid Transit & Land Co. v. Territory of Hawaii Ex Rel. Hemenway, 211 U.S. 282, 294-95 (1908). Petitioners identify no text, history, or precedent requiring the Attorney General personally to perform every temporary scheduling act.

Because Congress supplied an intelligible principle and because the Attorney General was authorized to delegate within his Department, the judgment below should be affirmed.
