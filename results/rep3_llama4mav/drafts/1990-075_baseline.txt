The Controlled Substances Act (CSA) does not unconstitutionally delegate legislative power to the Attorney General. The non-delegation doctrine, as reaffirmed by the Supreme Court in Mistretta v. United States, 488 U.S. 361 (1989), allows Congress to delegate authority to a coordinate branch when it lays down "an intelligible principle" to guide the delegatee's actions. Id. at 372. The CSA provides such a principle by specifying the factors the Attorney General must consider when temporarily scheduling a substance, including its history, current pattern, and significance of abuse, as well as the risk it poses to public health. 21 U.S.C. § 811(h)(1), (3).

The CSA's delegation is further constrained by the requirement that the Attorney General publish notice of the temporary scheduling in the Federal Register and consider comments from the Secretary of Health and Human Services. 21 U.S.C. § 811(h)(4). These procedural safeguards, combined with the substantive guidelines, ensure that the Attorney General's authority is not unbridled. The Supreme Court has upheld delegations with similarly broad language, see, e.g., Yakus v. United States, 321 U.S. 414 (1944), and the CSA's delegation is therefore constitutional.

The Attorney General's sub-delegation of temporary scheduling authority to the Drug Enforcement Agency (DEA) is also authorized. The CSA explicitly permits the Attorney General to delegate his functions to other officials, 21 U.S.C. § 871(a), and the Attorney General has done so through a valid regulation, 28 C.F.R. § 0.100(b). This sub-delegation is consistent with the CSA's overall scheme, which allows for efficient and effective enforcement of the statute.

The Third Circuit correctly held that the CSA's delegation is constitutional and that the Attorney General's sub-delegation to the DEA is authorized. The court properly applied the "intelligible principle" test and considered the relevant factors, including the complexity of the field and the rapidity of changes in the matter being regulated. See Mistretta, 488 U.S. at 372-73. The Toubys' challenge to the CSA's delegation should be rejected, and their convictions should be affirmed.

The Third Circuit's decision is consistent with the decisions of other courts that have considered the constitutionality of the CSA's delegation. See, e.g., United States v. Emerson, 846 F.2d 541 (9th Cir. 1988); United States v. Spain, 825 F.2d 1426 (10th Cir. 1987). These courts have recognized that the CSA's delegation is not without limits and that the Attorney General's authority is guided by the statute's substantive and procedural requirements.

In sum, the CSA's delegation of authority to the Attorney General is constitutional, and the Attorney General's sub-delegation to the DEA is authorized. The Toubys' challenge should be rejected, and their convictions should be affirmed.
