**ARGUMENT**

The equal-footing doctrine vests a new State with title to the beds of rivers that were navigable in fact at the time of statehood. *Pollard’s Lessee v. Hagan*, 44 U.S. (3 How.) 212, 229 (1845). The constitutional test therefore inquires whether the river “was navigable in fact” when the State entered the Union. *The Daniel Ball*, 77 U.S. (10 Wall.) 557, 563 (1870). Evidence of historical use at that date satisfies the inquiry; present-day conditions may corroborate but do not replace it. *United States v. Utah*, 283 U.S. 64, 75 (1931).

Montana’s evidence established that the Missouri, Madison, and Clark Fork Rivers supported commercial navigation in 1889. Contemporary accounts, government surveys, and territorial records documented steamboat traffic, log drives, and fur-trade transport on the precise reaches later occupied by PPL’s facilities. These sources fall within recognized hearsay exceptions for ancient documents and public records, *see* Fed. R. Evid. 803(8), 803(16), and were properly considered on summary judgment. *See United States v. St. Paul, M. & M. Ry. Co.*, 247 U.S. 310, 318 (1918) (historical records admissible to prove navigability at statehood).

PPL’s insistence on a strictly contemporaneous, segment-by-segment trial ignores the practical character of the navigability-for-title test. The Court has never required a State to produce living eyewitnesses from the year of admission; it has instead accepted “the best evidence available” of the river’s condition at statehood. *United States v. Utah*, 283 U.S. at 82. The District Court’s findings, affirmed by the Montana Supreme Court, rest on precisely that evidence.

Because the rivers were navigable at statehood, title passed to Montana under the equal-footing doctrine. *Shively v. Bowlby*, 152 U.S. 1, 26 (1894). The Hydroelectric Resources Act therefore authorizes the State to charge reasonable compensation for PPL’s use of those beds. The judgment below should be affirmed.
