**ARGUMENT**

The Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment requires a trial court to apply a heightened “reasoned choice” standard when determining whether a defendant is competent to waive counsel or enter a guilty plea. The Ninth Circuit correctly held that the state court failed to satisfy this constitutional obligation in Richard Allan Moran’s case. The record reveals substantial evidence of mental impairment—suicidal ideation, severe depression, and the use of four psychotropic medications—combined with monosyllabic responses and a desire to prevent mitigation, all of which should have triggered a good-faith doubt about Moran’s capacity to make a reasoned choice. The state court’s reliance on psychiatric evaluations conducted under the lower standard for competency to stand trial, and its failure to conduct a hearing under the proper standard, violated due process. This Court should affirm.

**I. The Constitution Requires a “Reasoned Choice” Standard for Waiver of Counsel and Guilty Pleas**

A defendant may not validly waive constitutional rights unless he possesses the capacity for “reasoned choice” among the alternatives available to him. *Harding v. Lewis*, 834 F.2d 853, 856 (9th Cir. 1987), cert. denied, 488 U.S. 871, 109 S.Ct. 182, 102 L.Ed.2d 151 (1988). This standard is distinct from the lesser requirement of competency to stand trial, which demands only a “rational and factual understanding of the proceedings” and the ability to assist counsel. *Drope v. Missouri*, 420 U.S. 162, 172, 95 S.Ct. 896, 904, 43 L.Ed.2d 103 (1975); *Dusky v. United States*, 362 U.S. 402, 402, 80 S.Ct. 788, 788, 4 L.Ed.2d 824 (1960) (per curiam). The “reasoned choice” standard demands more: the defendant must understand the nature and consequences of his decisions, including the risks of self-representation and the absence of mitigation at sentencing. *Chavez v. United States*, 656 F.2d 512, 518 (9th Cir. 1981); *Evans v. Raines*, 800 F.2d 884, 887 (9th Cir. 1986) (Evans II).

This higher standard is not merely procedural—it is rooted in the fundamental right to due process. As this Court has held, “the right to counsel is not a mere matter of form, but a vital component of the fair trial guaranteed by the Constitution.” *McKaskle v. Wiggins*, 465 U.S. 168, 177, 104 S.Ct. 944, 950, 79 L.Ed.2d 122 (1984). When a defendant waives this right, the court must ensure that the waiver is knowing, intelligent, and voluntary. *Boykin v. Alabama*, 395 U.S. 238, 243, 89 S.Ct. 1709, 1712, 23 L.Ed.2d 274 (1969). The “utmost solicitude” of the courts is required in such cases. *Sieling v. Eyman*, 478 F.2d 211, 214 (9th Cir. 1973). The failure to apply the correct standard renders the waiver constitutionally invalid.

**II. A Good-Faith Doubt Triggered the Duty to Conduct a Hearing**

Due process requires a court to initiate a competency hearing—on its own motion if necessary—whenever it has or should have a good-faith doubt about a defendant’s capacity to waive constitutional rights. *Sieling*, 478 F.2d at 214; *Evans I*, 705 F.2d 1479, 1480 (9th Cir. 1973); *Harding*, 834 F.2d at 856. A good-faith doubt arises when there is “substantial evidence of incompetence.” *Harding*, 834 F.2d at 856 (citing *United States v. Veatch*, 674 F.2d 1217, 1223 (9th Cir. 1981), cert. denied, 456 U.S. 946, 102 S.Ct. 2013, 72 L.Ed.2d 469 (1982)). In Moran’s case, such evidence was overwhelming.

Moran had attempted suicide just months before the plea hearing. He was still recovering from a self-inflicted gunshot wound to the abdomen and had slashed his wrists. He was taking four medications—*Inderal*, *Dilantin*, *Phenobarbitol*, and *Vistaril*—all of which affect cognition, mood, and motor function. *Inderal* is a beta-blocker that can cause fatigue and depression; *Dilantin* and *Phenobarbitol* are anticonvulsants with sedative effects; *Vistaril* is an anxiolytic that impairs judgment. *Physicians’ Desk Reference* 650–68, 1543–45, 1625–26, 1667–68 (42d ed. 1988). The court was informed that Moran was on medication, yet made no inquiry into its effects. This failure to investigate constitutes a clear breach of due process.

Moreover, Moran’s conduct at the hearing was profoundly inconsistent with reasoned choice. His responses were monosyllabic, and he repeatedly refused to engage in meaningful dialogue. He stated he wanted to fire his attorney to prevent any mitigating evidence from being presented. This decision—made in the context of a death penalty case—was not a rational choice but a self-destructive act, inconsistent with the kind of deliberate decision-making required
