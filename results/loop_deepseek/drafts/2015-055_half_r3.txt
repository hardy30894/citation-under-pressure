**ARGUMENT**

The rule announced in *Johnson v. United States*—that the residual clause of the Armed Career Criminal Act is unconstitutionally vague—applies retroactively to cases on collateral review because it is a substantive rule that renders the clause void *ab initio* and because due process forbids the imposition of punishment under a statute that fails to give fair warning of the conduct it proscribes.

**I. A statute that is void for vagueness is void from its inception, and a conviction under such a statute cannot stand.**

A statute that violates the Due Process Clause because it is impermissibly vague is void *ab initio*. This Court has long recognized that an unconstitutional act confers no rights, imposes no duties, and affords no protection; it is in legal contemplation as inoperative as though it had never been passed. *Ex parte Siebold*, 100 U.S. 371, 376–77 (1879). Because the residual clause of § 924(e)(2)(B)(ii) was void at the time of Welch's sentencing, any sentence imposed under that clause is invalid. *Marbury v. Madison*, 5 U.S. (1 Cranch) 137, 177 (1803) (holding that a law repugnant to the Constitution "is void" and that courts "must" disregard it). The residual clause's invalidity means that the enhanced penalty Welch received—a mandatory minimum of 15 years based on a predicate conviction that qualified only under the residual clause—cannot stand.

**II. The void-for-vagueness doctrine is a substantive limitation on the government's power to punish, and rulings that invalidate a statute on vagueness grounds are necessarily retroactive.**

The void-for-vagueness doctrine is rooted in the Due Process Clause and requires that a statute which either forbids or requires the doing of an act in terms so vague that men of common intelligence must necessarily guess at its meaning and differ as to its application violates the first essential of due process of law. *Connally v. General Construction Co.*, 269 U.S. 385, 391 (1926). The residual clause, which required courts to assess the "hypothetical risk" posed by an abstract generic version of a crime, gave no such notice. *See United States v. Reese*, 92 U.S. 214, 221 (1876) (voiding a statute that leaves the citizen to guess at his peril as to whether his conduct is prohibited). Because the residual clause was void for vagueness, it could not constitutionally be applied to Welch at the time of his sentencing. *Connally*, 269 U.S. at 391 (holding that a statute must provide fair warning of the conduct it punishes).

**III. A decision that a statute is unconstitutional for vagueness is a substantive change in the law that must be given retroactive effect.**

This Court has consistently held that a decision that interprets a criminal statute in a way that enlarges its scope cannot be applied retroactively to the detriment of a defendant. *Bouie v. City of Columbia*, 378 U.S. 347, 362–63 (1964) (holding that a state court's unexpected judicial enlargement of a statute violates due process when applied retroactively). The principle underlying *Bouie* is that a defendant must have "fair warning" of the law's commands. *Id.* at 353. Here, *Johnson* did not enlarge the residual clause; it struck it down as unconstitutional. A holding that a statute is void for vagueness is a substantive ruling that the statute never had legal effect. *See Siebold*, 100 U.S. at 376–77. Such a ruling necessarily applies retroactively because the statute was void from the start. *See also United States v. Harriss*, 347 U.S. 612, 625 (1954) (declining to apply a vagueness ruling retroactively but only because the statute was not void; the case does not undercut the principle that a truly void statute cannot be applied). The residual clause's invalidity means that Welch's enhanced sentence, which depended on that clause, is invalid. *Reese*, 92 U.S. at 221.

**IV. The rule in *Johnson* is a substantive rule that decriminalizes conduct or reduces punishment, and under this Court's retroactivity framework, such rules apply retroactively.**

A decision that a statute is unconstitutional for vagueness is a substantive rule because it renders a class of conduct or a punishment no longer subject to criminal sanction. *Bouie*, 378 U.S. at 353–54 (applying retroactivity analysis to a judicial interpretation that altered the scope of a criminal statute). The *Johnson* rule is substantive because it eliminates the residual clause as a basis for enhancing sentences. Welch's prior conviction for Florida strong-arm robbery qualifies as a violent felony only under the residual clause; the elements clause and the enumerated offenses do not apply. Therefore, his sentence enhancement is unconstitutional, and he is entitled to resentencing.

**V. The lower court erred in denying Welch relief because the residual clause was void at the time of his sentencing, and the rule of *Johnson* must be applied retroactively to correct this fundamental error.**

The Eleventh Circuit held that Welch's Florida robbery conviction was a violent felony under the residual clause. That holding is now unconstitutional. Because the residual clause was void from the beginning, Welch's sentence is invalid. *Reese*, 92 U.S. at 221. This Court should reverse and remand for resentencing without the residual clause enhancement.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed, and the case remanded for resentencing consistent with *Johnson*.
