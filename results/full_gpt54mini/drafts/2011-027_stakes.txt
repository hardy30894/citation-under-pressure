## Argument

The Court should answer the question presented in the affirmative. Title navigability is not an abstract inquiry into whether a river is or ever became useful for commerce; it is a statehood inquiry. Under the equal-footing doctrine, the States received title to riverbeds only if the waters were navigable when the State entered the Union. The Supreme Court has repeatedly held that “navigability for title purposes is determined at the time of statehood,” and that later changes in condition do not create or destroy title. *PPL Montana, LLC v. Montana*, 565 U.S. 576, 592 (2012); *United States v. Utah*, 283 U.S. 64, 75 (1931); *United States v. Oregon*, 295 U.S. 1, 14-15 (1935).

That rule follows from the Constitution’s equal-footing doctrine. When a State enters the Union, it receives the same sovereignty over submerged lands as the original States, but only as to those waters that were navigable in fact at statehood. *Pollard v. Hagan*, 44 U.S. (3 How.) 212, 230-31 (1845); *Shively v. Bowlby*, 152 U.S. 1, 57 (1894). The Court in *Shively* explained that title to the beds of navigable waters “passed to the several States” on admission, but the federal government retained title where waters were not navigable. 152 U.S. at 57-58. Thus, the relevant legal question is whether the river segment was navigable “at the time of statehood,” not whether it has since been altered by natural forces or human development. *PPL Montana*, 565 U.S. at 592.

The Court’s precedents also require segment-by-segment analysis. Navigability for title is not established by proof that a river, as a whole, was generally useful for commerce somewhere, sometime. Rather, the inquiry focuses on “the segment of the river” whose bed is claimed, and the segment must be shown navigable in its natural and ordinary condition at statehood. *United States v. Utah*, 283 U.S. at 76-77; *Oklahoma v. Texas*, 258 U.S. 574, 586-87 (1922); *PPL Montana*, 565 U.S. at 592-94. The Court has rejected efforts to aggregate nonnavigable reaches with navigable ones or to rely on modern improvements and reservoirs to supply title navigability. *United States v. Appalachian Elec. Power Co.*, 311 U.S. 377, 406-08 (1940); *PPL Montana*, 565 U.S. at 596-97.

Petitioner’s contrary theory would untether title from statehood and collapse the constitutional distinction between title navigability and regulatory navigability. The Court has long recognized that navigability for commerce under the Commerce Clause is broader than navigability for title. *The Daniel Ball*, 77 U.S. (10 Wall.) 557, 563 (1870); *United States v. Appalachian Elec. Power Co.*, 311 U.S. at 406. But that broader regulatory concept does not determine who owns the riverbed. *PPL Montana*, 565 U.S. at 593-94. If post-statehood conditions could establish title, a State’s ownership would vary with later dams, dredging, or episodic use, contrary to the settled rule that title vested, if at all, at the moment of statehood. *United States v. Utah*, 283 U.S. at 75-77.

That statehood rule is especially important here because the State’s claimed rights rest on the equal-footing doctrine. The doctrine is grounded in equal sovereignty among the States, not in later evidence of utility. *Pollard*, 44 U.S. at 228-31; *Shively*, 152 U.S. at 57-58. The Court in *Montana v. United States* likewise treated title to the bed of a navigable river as turning on navigability when the State entered the Union. 450 U.S. 544, 551-52 (1981). And where the Court has found state title, it has done so only after concluding that the particular river or segment was navigable at statehood. *United States v. Utah*, 283 U.S. at 75-77; *Utah v. United States*, 403 U.S. 9, 10-11 (1971).

The Montana Supreme Court’s contrary approach therefore cannot stand. By focusing on later conditions and by treating historical use evidence as sufficient without tying it to statehood-era navigability of the specific segments at issue, the court departed from the controlling federal standard. *PPL Montana*, 565 U.S. at 593-97. The federal test asks a simple but constitutionally decisive question: was the relevant river segment navigable in fact at statehood? If not, the United States retained title, and no state-law claim predicated on state ownership can survive. *Pollard*, 44 U.S. at 230-31; *Shively*, 152 U.S. at 57-58; *United States v. Utah*, 283 U.S. at 75-77.

For those reasons, the Court should hold that navigability for title purposes must be determined as of statehood and on a segment-specific basis, and it should reverse the judgment below.
