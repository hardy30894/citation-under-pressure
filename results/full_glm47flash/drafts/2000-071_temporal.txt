**ARGUMENT**

**I. Milford’s policy creates a limited public forum for community use.**

Milford Central School’s Community Use Policy authorizes district residents to utilize school facilities for "social, civic and recreational meetings" and other activities pertaining to the welfare of the community. By opening its facilities for these specific purposes, the School created a limited public forum. The government may not, by a general regulation, exclude the use of a public forum for a particular purpose because of the subject matter of the speech. *United States v. Rumely*, 345 U.S. 41, 50 (1953). This principle was reaffirmed in *United States v. CIO*, where the Court held that a public park is a public forum and the government may not exclude the use of a forum for a particular purpose because of the subject matter of the speech. 335 U.S. 106, 116 (1948). Milford’s policy explicitly permits the use of its facilities for "moral and character development" by secular groups. Therefore, the School cannot exclude the Good News Club simply because the subject matter of its speech is religious.

**II. The exclusion constitutes viewpoint discrimination.**

The Second Circuit erred in characterizing the Good News Club’s activities as "quintessentially religious" subject matter rather than viewpoint discrimination. The Club’s activities—singing songs, hearing Bible lessons, and memorizing scripture—are presented as "moral and character development" from a religious perspective. The School allows other groups to engage in this exact same subject matter from a secular perspective. By excluding the Club solely because of its religious viewpoint, Milford discriminates based on the viewpoint rather than the subject matter. The right of free speech is not a mere luxury which a tolerant society can dispense with in times of peace. *Edwards v. South Carolina*, 372 U.S. 229, 236 (1963). Furthermore, it can hardly be argued that either students or teachers shed their constitutional rights to freedom of speech or expression at the schoolhouse gate. *Tinker v. Des Moines Independent Community School District*, 393 U.S. 503, 506 (1969). When a government entity opens a forum for a specific subject, it must allow all viewpoints on that subject.

**III. The Establishment Clause does not justify the exclusion.**

Milford argues that excluding the Good News Club is necessary to avoid violating the Establishment Clause. However, the Establishment Clause forbids legislation either preferring one religion to another or preferring religion to nonreligion. *Walz v. Tax Commission*, 397 U.S. 664, 674 (1970). Milford’s policy does the opposite; it prefers nonreligion by allowing secular moral instruction while excluding religious moral instruction. The First Amendment has erected a wall between church and state. That wall must be kept high and impregnable. *Everson v. Board of Education*, 330 U.S. 1, 18 (1947). Allowing the Good News Club to meet in a private capacity after hours does not constitute state sponsorship of religion. The School is not endorsing the Club’s message; it is merely providing access to a limited public forum. Therefore, Milford’s concern for the Establishment Clause does not justify the exclusion of the Club.
