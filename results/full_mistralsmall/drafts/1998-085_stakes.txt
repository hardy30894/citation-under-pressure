**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE TEXT AND STRUCTURE OF § 1981a DO NOT IMPOSE AN “EGREGIOUSNESS” REQUIREMENT INDEPENDENT OF THE STATUTORY MENTAL STATE.**

The plain language of § 1981a(b)(1) authorizes punitive damages where the plaintiff “demonstrates that the [defendant] engaged in a discriminatory practice ... with malice or with reckless indifference to the federally protected rights of an aggrieved individual.” 42 U.S.C. § 1981a(b)(1) (emphasis added). Congress did not add any additional textual element such as “egregious” or “outrageous” conduct. This Court has repeatedly held that “[w]hen the terms of a statute are unambiguous, this Court must enforce the statute according to its plain terms.” *United States v. Ron Pair Enters., Inc.*, 489 U.S. 235, 241 (1989); *see also Connecticut Nat'l Bank v. Germain*, 503 U.S. 249, 253-54 (1992) (same). The absence of any “egregiousness” qualifier in the statute compels the conclusion that Congress did not intend to import such a requirement. *Cf. BMW of N. Am., Inc. v. Gore*, 517 U.S. 559, 568 (1996) (noting that punitive damages awards must comport with statutory limits and due process, but declining to impose judicially created “egregiousness” tests where statute is silent).

The structure of § 1981a further confirms this reading. The statute expressly defines the mental state required for punitive damages—“malice” or “reckless indifference”—and separately caps damages based on employer size. 42 U.S.C. § 1981a(b)(1), (b)(3). If Congress had intended to impose an additional “egregiousness” threshold, it would have done so explicitly, as it did in other contexts. *See, e.g., Smith v. Wade*, 461 U.S. 30, 56 (1983) (noting that in § 1983 cases, punitive damages require “conduct that is outrageous, because of the defendant’s evil motive or his reckless indifference to the rights of others”); *cf. Kolstad v. Am. Dental Ass’n*, 139 F.3d 953, 960 (D.C. Cir. 1998) (en banc) (Tatel, J., concurring in part and dissenting in part) (acknowledging that some courts have conflated “egregiousness” with the statutory mental state). The failure to include such language here is dispositive. *See Lamie v. U.S. Tr.,* 540 U.S. 526, 534 (2004) (“[W]here Congress includes particular language in one section of a statute but omits it in another, it is generally presumed that Congress acts intentionally in the disparate inclusion or exclusion.”).

**II. THE LEGISLATIVE HISTORY DOES NOT SUPPORT AN “EGREGIOUSNESS” REQUIREMENT.**

The legislative history cited by petitioner is ambiguous at best and does not overcome the statute’s plain meaning. *See Chevron U.S.A. Inc. v. Natural Res. Def. Council, Inc.*, 467 U.S. 837, 842-43 (1984) (requiring deference to agency interpretations only where statute is ambiguous). Senator Dole’s statement that punitive damages should be available only in “extraordinarily egregious cases” was part of a colloquy and does not reflect the enacted text. *See* 137 Cong. Rec. S15473 (Oct. 30, 1991). Representative Edwards, by contrast, stated that punitive damages should be available “to the same extent and under the same standards” as under § 1981. *Id.* at H9527 (Nov. 7, 1991). This Court has cautioned against relying on isolated statements in legislative history where they conflict with the statute’s text. *See, e.g., Exxon Mobil Corp. v. Allapattah Servs., Inc.*, 545 U.S. 546, 568 (2005) (“[W]e do not resort to legislative history to cloud a statutory text that is clear.”).

Moreover, the House Report’s reference to a “higher standard” for punitive damages was tied to the *House version* of the bill, which included the word “callous” in the mental state requirement. *See* H.R. Rep. No. 102-40(I), at 12 (1991). The final version omitted this language, and the Conference Report did not incorporate the “higher standard” language. *See* H.R. Conf. Rep. No. 102-81, at 72 (1991). This Court has repeatedly refused to import provisions from rejected legislative drafts. *See, e.g., Russello v. United States*, 464 U.S. 16, 23-24 (1983) (declining to rely on rejected Senate bill language).

**III. THIS COURT’S PRECEDENT RECOGNIZES THAT PUNITIVE DAMAGES UNDER § 1981a TURN SOLELY ON THE STATUTORY MENTAL STATE.**

In *Kolstad v. American Dental Ass’n*, 527 U.S. 526 (1999), this Court held that punitive damages under § 1981a are available where the employer acts with “malice” or “reckless indifference,” without requiring additional “egregious” conduct. *Id.* at 535-36. The Court expressly rejected the argument that the statute imposed a heightened threshold, noting that the “text of § 1981a(b)(1) makes clear that the statute permits punitive damages when an employer discriminates ... with malice or reckless indifference.” *Id.* at 535. The Court further observed that the statute’s structure—linking punitive damages to the employer’s mental state rather than the severity of its conduct—“reflects Congress’s intent to tie punitive damages to the employer’s culpability, not to a judicial assessment of the conduct’s egregiousness.” *Id.* at 536.

Petitioner’s reliance on *Smith v. Wade*, 461 U.S. 30 (1983), is misplaced. *Smith* interpreted § 1983, not § 1981a, and involved a common-law tort regime where “egregiousness” was a traditional factor in punitive damages analysis. *Id.* at 56. By contrast, § 1981a expressly defines the mental state required for punitive damages, displacing any judicially created “egregiousness” inquiry. *See also Cooper Indus., Inc. v. Leatherman Tool Grp., Inc.*, 532 U.S. 424, 432 (2001) (distinguishing between statutory mental states and common-law punitive damages standards).

**IV. AN “EGREGIOUSNESS” REQUIREMENT WOULD UNDERMINE CONGRESS’S GOAL OF DETERRENCE AND CONFLICT WITH THE STATUTE’S TEXTUAL LIMITS.**

Congress enacted § 1981a to strengthen remedies for intentional discrimination and harassment in the workplace. *See* 42 U.S.C. § 1981a(a)(1). Imposing an “egregiousness” requirement would frustrate this purpose by allowing employers to escape punitive damages for intentional discrimination so long as their conduct was not “outrageous” enough. *See BMW of N. Am., Inc. v. Gore*, 517 U.S. at 568 (warning against judicial limitations that “in practical effect ... amount to a judicial determination that the jury’s award is excessive and unreasonable”). The statute’s caps on punitive damages already cabin potential liability, 42 U.S.C. § 1981a(b)(3), and no further judicial limitation is warranted.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment below should be affirmed. The text, structure, and legislative history of § 1981a do not impose an “egregiousness” requirement independent of the statutory mental state. This Court’s precedent confirms that punitive damages are available where the employer acts with “malice” or “reckless indifference,” without further judicial scrutiny of the conduct’s severity.
