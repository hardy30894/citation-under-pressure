# ARGUMENT

## I. Article III Confines Federal Courts to Live, Concrete Disputes, and a Bare Claim for a Token Sum Cannot Revive a Controversy Already Resolved.

The judicial power extends only to genuine "cases" and "controversies," and this Court has long refused to render judgments that would amount to advisory opinions on questions no longer affecting the parties before it. *Muskrat v. United States*, 219 U.S. 346 (1911). In *Mills v. Green*, 159 U.S. 651 (1895), this Court explained that "the duty of this court, as of every other judicial tribunal, is limited to determining rights of persons or of property, which are actually controverted in the particular case before it." *Id.* at 653. Once Georgia Gwinnett College rescinded the Freedom of Expression Policy and the challenged Student Code provision, no live dispute remained over the validity of rules that no longer governed anyone's conduct.

The touchstone for what remains justiciable after such intervening events is whether the case presents a "real and substantial controversy admitting of specific relief through a decree of a conclusive character, as distinguished from an opinion advising what the law would be upon a hypothetical state of facts." *Aetna Life Insurance Co. v. Haworth*, 300 U.S. 227, 241 (1937). As this Court further explained, "[t]he controversy must be definite and concrete, touching the legal relations of parties having adverse legal interests." *Id.* at 240. A demand for a nominal sum, unmoored from any pleaded actual injury, is precisely the sort of abstraction *Aetna* forecloses: it identifies no continuing legal relationship between the parties and seeks no relief beyond a symbolic label attached to a policy that GGC has already repealed.

## II. Claims Too Insubstantial to Reflect a Genuine Stake in the Outcome Do Not Preserve Jurisdiction Once the Underlying Dispute Is Resolved.

This Court has repeatedly dismissed cases as moot where the only thing technically remaining was a residual claim too slight to embody a genuine, ongoing dispute between adverse parties. In *California v. San Pablo & Tulare Railroad Co.*, 149 U.S. 308 (1893), the State's suit to recover back taxes became moot once the legislature released the underlying obligation, notwithstanding a nominally surviving claim, because nothing of practical consequence remained for the courts to decide between the parties. *Id.* at 314. And in *United States v. Alaska Steamship Co.*, 253 U.S. 113 (1920), this Court held that once intervening events destroy the practical significance of a dispute, the case is moot even though some technical vestige of the controversy persists on paper. *Id.* at 116.

The same logic controls here. Petitioners' request for nominal damages is exactly such a technical vestige. It survives the repeal of the challenged policies only in form, not in substance, because it identifies no concrete injury and seeks no relief capable of altering the parties' legal relationship going forward. A claim that exists only to keep litigation alive, rather than to redress an actual injury, is not the sort of "definite and concrete" controversy that satisfies Article III. *Aetna*, 300 U.S. at 240.

## III. GGC's Voluntary Revision of the Challenged Policies Mooted This Case, and the Residual Nominal-Damages Request Cannot Restore a Live Controversy.

Where a defendant voluntarily ceases the challenged conduct and there is no reasonable expectation that the same controversy will recur, the case is moot. *Golden v. Zwickler*, 394 U.S. 103 (1969). There, this Court dismissed a challenge to an anonymous-handbill statute as moot because the legislator whose prior criticism had prompted the suit no longer held office, and the prospect that the plaintiff would again confront the same restriction was speculative at best. *Id.* at 108–10. The Eleventh Circuit correctly held—and petitioners do not contest—that GGC's revision of the Freedom of Expression Policy and Student Code of Conduct mooted their claims for declaratory and injunctive relief. That same voluntary and complete abandonment of the offending provisions likewise deprives the residual nominal-damages request of any live footing, because petitioners face no continuing or future enforcement of policies that no longer exist.

*St. Pierre v. United States*, 319 U.S. 41 (1943), confirms that a technically surviving claim does not, by itself, preserve jurisdiction. There, this Court dismissed a contempt appeal as moot once the sentence had been served, notwithstanding lingering collateral consequences, because no substantial and continuing stake in the outcome remained. *Id.* at 42–43. By contrast, in *Liner v. Jafco, Inc.*, 375 U.S. 301 (1964), this Court declined to find mootness because a genuine, substantial dispute over costs survived the completion of the enjoined construction project—an interest bearing directly on a real economic stake between the parties. *Id.* at 306 n.3. Nominal damages untethered from any pleaded actual injury are the inverse of the substantial cost dispute preserved in *Liner*; they are indistinguishable from the empty formalism this Court declined to entertain in *St. Pierre*.

Finally, this Court's settled practice when a civil case becomes moot during the course of litigation—vacating the underlying judgment rather than adjudicating a controversy the parties no longer have a stake in resolving—reflects the same principle. *United States v. Munsingwear, Inc.*, 340 U.S. 36, 39–40 (1950). That principle applies with full force here: because the Prior Policies no longer exist, and because petitioners have alleged no actual injury flowing from them, a request for a token sum cannot substitute for the "real and substantial controversy" that Article III demands. *Aetna*, 300 U.S. at 241.

## Conclusion

For these reasons, the judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
