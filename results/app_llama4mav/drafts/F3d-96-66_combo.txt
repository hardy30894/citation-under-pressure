## Argument

The district court erred in granting summary judgment to Bora Corp., Ltd. ("the ship") because there are genuine issues of material fact regarding the ship's negligence under section 5(b) of the Longshore and Harbor Workers' Compensation Act, 33 U.S.C. § 905(b). Specifically, the ship breached its "active operations" duty to protect longshoremen like John Serbin from hazards related to the ship's operations.

The "active operations" duty, as developed in pre-1970 case law, requires a shipowner to exercise reasonable care to prevent injuries to longshoremen when the ship is actively involved in the cargo operations. In The Max Morris, 283 U.S. 520 (1931), the Supreme Court established that a shipowner has a duty to protect longshoremen from hazards related to the ship's operations. The Court held that the shipowner's duty is not limited to providing a safe workplace, but also extends to exercising reasonable care during cargo operations (The Max Morris, 283 U.S. at 527).

In Alaska S.S. Co. v. Petterson, 347 F.2d 478 (9th Cir. 1965), the Ninth Circuit applied this principle, holding that a shipowner's failure to properly secure a winch boom constituted negligence (Alaska S.S. Co. v. Petterson, 347 F.2d at 481). Similarly, in Grillea v. United States, 232 F.2d 919 (2d Cir. 1956), the Second Circuit held that a shipowner's failure to properly stow cargo created a hazardous condition that led to a longshoreman's injury (Grillea v. United States, 232 F.2d at 922).

Here, the ship's crew was responsible for moving the snatch blocks, including the one that Serbin attempted to move. The crew's failure to return the snatch block to the up position created a hazardous condition that Serbin attempted to remedy. As in Petterson and Grillea, the ship's failure to properly secure the snatch block constitutes negligence.

Furthermore, the ship's crew was actively involved in the cargo operations, as they were responsible for opening and closing the hatch covers using the block and pulley system. In The Admiral Peoples, 295 U.S. 649 (1935), the Supreme Court held that a shipowner's duty to protect longshoremen extends to situations where the ship is actively involved in cargo operations (The Admiral Peoples, 295 U.S. at 653-54). The ship's active involvement in the cargo operations, combined with its failure to properly secure the snatch block, creates a genuine issue of material fact regarding the ship's negligence.

Additionally, in Imperial Oil, Limited v. Drlik, 234 F.2d 4 (6th Cir. 1956), the Sixth Circuit held that a shipowner's failure to warn longshoremen of a hazardous condition constituted negligence (Imperial Oil, Limited v. Drlik, 234 F.2d at 8). Here, the ship's crew failed to warn Serbin of the hazardous condition created by the snatch block, further supporting a finding of negligence.

The ship's argument that Serbin assumed the risk by moving the snatch block is unpersuasive. In Socony-Vacuum Oil Co. v. Smith, 305 U.S. 141 (1938), the Supreme Court held that a longshoreman's assumption of risk is not a complete bar to recovery, but rather a factor to be considered in determining the shipowner's liability (Socony-Vacuum Oil Co. v. Smith, 305 U.S. at 148-49). In Pope & Talbot, Inc. v. Hawn, 346 U.S. 406 (1953), the Supreme Court reaffirmed this principle, holding that a longshoreman's contributory negligence is not a bar to recovery, but rather a factor to be considered in determining damages (Pope & Talbot, Inc. v. Hawn, 346 U.S. at 408-09).

Moreover, in Lahde v. United States, 194 F.2d 43 (9th Cir. 1952), the Ninth Circuit held that a shipowner's negligence can be established even if the longshoreman took some action that contributed to the injury (Lahde v. United States, 194 F.2d at 45). Here, Serbin's decision to move the snatch block was a reasonable response to the hazardous condition created by the ship's crew.

Finally, in Seas Shipping Co. v. Sieracki, 328 U.S. 85 (1946), the Supreme Court held that a shipowner's duty to protect longshoremen is not limited to providing a safe workplace, but also extends to exercising reasonable care during cargo operations (Seas Shipping Co. v. Sieracki, 328 U.S. at 94-95). The ship's failure to exercise reasonable care in this case creates a genuine issue of material fact regarding its negligence.

In conclusion, the district court erred in granting summary judgment to the ship. The ship's breach of its "active operations" duty, combined with its failure to properly secure the snatch block and warn Serbin of the hazardous condition, creates a genuine issue of material fact regarding the ship's negligence. This Court should reverse the district court's order and remand the case for further proceedings.
