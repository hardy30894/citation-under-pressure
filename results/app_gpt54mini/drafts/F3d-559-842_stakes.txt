**Argument**

The district court erred in granting summary judgment to Rand because, on this record, a reasonable jury could find that Rand violated TILA and HOEPA in ways that extended the Mouas’ right to rescind from three days to three years. Under 15 U.S.C. § 1635(a), a borrower’s right to rescind extends to three years if the creditor fails to deliver the disclosures required by TILA. The Supreme Court has explained that TILA rescission is triggered by nondelivery of the required disclosures, and that the right continues for three years when those disclosures are not provided. *Beach v. Ocwen Federal Bank*, 523 U.S. 410, 412, 417-18 (1998); *Jesinoski v. Countrywide Home Loans, Inc.*, 574 U.S. 259, 262-63 (2015). HOEPA likewise requires heightened disclosures for high-cost mortgages, and those disclosures must be made clearly and conspicuously before consummation. 15 U.S.C. § 1639(b); *In re Jones*, 298 B.R. 451, 459-60 (Bankr. D. Kan. 2003).

I. **A genuine dispute exists whether Rand failed to provide the required disclosures and notice.**

The record, viewed in the Mouas’ favor, supports the conclusion that Rand did not satisfy its disclosure obligations. The Mouas testified that they did not receive the three-day advance HOEPA disclosures before closing. That testimony alone creates a genuine issue of material fact because TILA compliance turns on whether the lender actually delivered the required information. See *Barrett v. JP Morgan Chase Bank, N.A.*, 445 F.3d 874, 882-83 (6th Cir. 2006) (strict compliance required; creditor bears burden of proving disclosure).

The same is true for the Notice of Right to Cancel. TILA requires the creditor to deliver a clear and conspicuous notice of the right to rescind. 15 U.S.C. § 1635(a); 12 C.F.R. § 226.23(b)(1). Courts construe this obligation strictly. *Rodash v. AIB Mortgage Co.*, 16 F.3d 1142, 1144-45 (11th Cir. 1994), overruled on other grounds by statute; *Handy v. Anchor Mortgage Corp.*, 464 F.3d 760, 764-65 (7th Cir. 2006). Here, the Mouas produced evidence that the “Confirmation” section was signed at the closing, before the rescission period expired, and that they did not understand what they were being asked to sign. A jury could find that the “Confirmation” document did not constitute a valid waiver or that its use obscured, rather than clarified, the rescission right.

II. **Any purported waiver of the three-day advance HOEPA disclosure period was ineffective on this record.**

HOEPA’s advance-disclosure requirement protects borrowers in high-cost mortgage transactions from hurried decision-making. 15 U.S.C. § 1639(b); *Jones v. Countrywide Home Loans, Inc.*, 504 F. Supp. 2d 134, 140-41 (S.D. Tex. 2007). A waiver in this setting must be knowing, voluntary, and tied to a bona fide personal financial emergency. See 12 C.F.R. § 226.23(e); *Mills v. Equicredit Corp.*, 172 F. App’x 504, 507-08 (4th Cir. 2006). Rand relied on a handwritten statement prepared at closing, allegedly copied by Manisy at the closer’s direction. On this record, a factfinder could conclude the purported waiver was not voluntary and not made with understanding.

That conclusion is reinforced by the alleged circumstances of the closing: the Mouas were in foreclosure, were told to sign a statement they did not understand, and were presented with documents only at the closing itself. TILA and HOEPA are designed to prevent precisely this sort of pressure-driven, opaque transaction. *Mourning v. Family Publications Service, Inc.*, 411 U.S. 356, 364-65 (1973); *Powers v. Sims & Levin*, 542 F.2d 1216, 1219-20 (4th Cir. 1976).

III. **The alleged prepayment penalty provides an independent HOEPA violation and supports rescission.**

The Mouas also contend the note included a prepayment penalty prohibited by HOEPA. HOEPA prohibits certain prepayment penalties in high-cost mortgage transactions. 15 U.S.C. § 1639(c). Where a loan term violates HOEPA’s substantive limits, rescission is an available remedy. *Shepeard v. Quality Siding & Window Factory, Inc.*, 730 F. Supp. 1295, 1299-1300 (D. Del. 1990). Rand disputes the characterization of the provision, but that dispute is factual, not legal. The note’s terms and their effect should have been evaluated by the trier of fact rather than resolved on summary judgment.

IV. **Because the evidence permits a finding of TILA/HOEPA violations, the rescission period remained open.**

When disclosure violations occur, the borrower’s rescission right continues for up to three years. *Beach*, 523 U.S. at 417-18. The borrowers need not sue within that period to preserve the right; written notice suffices. *Jesinoski*, 574 U.S. at 262-63. The Mouas’ evidence, including the alleged disclosure failures, the contested waiver, and the challenged “Confirmation” signature, is enough to create a triable issue that their rescission right had not expired when they sought to cancel.

For these reasons, the judgment should be reversed and the case remanded for trial.
