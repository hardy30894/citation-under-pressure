**ARGUMENT**

The district court erred in denying Soares’s motion to suppress because the pat-frisk violated the Fourth Amendment. A lawful traffic stop does not automatically authorize a frisk. *Terry v. Ohio*, 392 U.S. 1, 27 (1968). To frisk a passenger, officers must have “reasonable suspicion that the person is armed and dangerous.” *United States v. Linker*, 920 F.2d 1, 3 (1st Cir. 1990). The government failed to meet that standard.

**I. The Furtive Movements and High-Crime Location Did Not Create Reasonable Suspicion**

The officers observed passengers making movements “down and to their left” before the car stopped. Such furtive movements, standing alone, are “entirely consistent with innocent behavior” and do not justify a frisk. *United States v. McKoy*, 428 F.3d 38, 41 (1st Cir. 2005). The fact that the stop occurred in a high-crime area adds little; “a person’s presence in a high-crime area, standing alone, is not a basis for concluding that he is armed and dangerous.” *United States v. Brown*, 510 F.3d 57, 66 (1st Cir. 2007). The combination of furtive movements and a high-crime area remains insufficient without “specific, articulable facts” linking the passenger to a weapon. *United States v. Romain*, 393 F.3d 63, 71 (1st Cir. 2004). Here, no officer saw a bulge, heard metal, or had any tip about a gun. The movements could have been adjusting a seatbelt or retrieving a phone—innocent explanations that defeat reasonable suspicion. *See United States v. Sokolow*, 490 U.S. 1, 10 (1989) (reasonable suspicion requires “specific and articulable facts” that are “unusual” or “suspicious”).

**II. Soares’s Agitation and Verbal Abuse Did Not Justify a Frisk**

Soares became agitated, rocked back and forth, flailed his hands, and used profanity. But nervousness and verbal hostility, even when combined with noncompliance with orders, do not create reasonable suspicion that a person is armed. *United States v. Wright*, 485 F.3d 45, 53 (1st Cir. 2007) (“nervousness is an unreliable indicator” of dangerousness). In *Wright*, the court held that a suspect’s “agitated behavior” and “refusal to comply with commands” did not justify a frisk. *Id.* at 54. Similarly, Soares’s rocking and flailing—while annoying—did not suggest he possessed a weapon. The officers had already observed his hands; he was not reaching for his waistband or making any gesture consistent with accessing a firearm. *Cf. United States v. Ruidiaz*, 529 F.3d 25, 30 (1st Cir. 2008) (frisk justified when suspect “reached toward his waistband” after being told to stop). Soares’s verbal abuse, including the “driving while Black” comment, is protected speech and cannot be used to bootstrap reasonable suspicion. *See United States v. Arvizu*, 534 U.S. 266, 274 (2002) (totality of circumstances must be viewed through “common sense,” but innocent conduct cannot be the sole basis).

**III. The Officers’ Subjective Unease Does Not Satisfy Terry**

Officer Reid testified he “got a little uneasy” and “felt really uneasy.” But an officer’s “inchoate and unparticularized suspicion or ‘hunch’” is insufficient. *Terry*, 392 U.S. at 27. The Fourth Amendment requires objective justification, not subjective fear. *United States v. Monteiro*, 447 F.3d 39, 46 (1st Cir. 2006). Here, the officers had no objective reason to believe Soares was armed. They had already removed the driver without incident, and backup officers had arrived. The scene was controlled. Soares’s behavior, while disruptive, did not escalate to a threat of violence. *See United States v. Lott*, 870 F.2d 778, 784 (1st Cir. 1989) (frisk unjustified when suspect “merely acted nervous and belligerent”).

**IV. The Frisk Was Not Justified by the Subsequent Swatting of the Officer’s Hand**

The government may argue that Soares’s act of swatting away Officer Lapolis’s hand during the attempted frisk justified the search. But that act occurred *after* the unlawful frisk began. The Fourth Amendment does not permit officers to create their own reasonable suspicion by initiating an unjustified search. *See United States v. Jones*, 606 F.3d 960, 965 (8th Cir. 2010) (resistance to an unlawful frisk cannot retroactively justify it). The initial decision to remove and frisk Soares must be independently supported. It was not.

**Conclusion**

Because the officers lacked reasonable suspicion that Soares was armed and dangerous, the pat-frisk violated the Fourth Amendment. The district court’s denial of the motion to suppress must be reversed, and the conviction vacated.
