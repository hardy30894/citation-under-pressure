The National Labor Relations Board's (NLRB) "good-faith reasonable doubt" standard for polling employees on union support is not rational and consistent with the National Labor Relations Act (NLRA). Furthermore, the NLRB's factual determination that Allentown Mack Sales, Inc. lacked such doubt is not supported by substantial evidence in the record.

The NLRA does not explicitly govern employee polling, and the NLRB has discretion to develop and apply national labor policy in this area (NLRB v. Curtin Matheson Scientific, Inc., 494 U.S. 775, 786 (1990)). However, the NLRB's current standard for polling, which requires "objective indications raising a reasonable doubt about the union's majority status," is too strict and renders polling "marginally useful" to employers (Texas Petrochemicals Corp., 296 N.L.R.B. 1057, 1061-62 (1989)). Three circuits have rejected this standard, holding that an employer may poll employees if it has "substantial, objective evidence of a loss of union support" (A.W. Thompson, Inc. v. NLRB, 651 F.2d 1141, 1145 (5th Cir. 1981); Mingtree Restaurant, Inc. v. NLRB, 736 F.2d 1295, 1299 (9th Cir. 1984); Thomas Indus., Inc. v. NLRB, 687 F.2d 863, 867 (6th Cir. 1982)).

The NLRB's standard is inconsistent with the NLRA's goal of stable collective-bargaining relationships. By making it difficult for employers to poll employees, the NLRB's standard may lead to employers withdrawing recognition from a union without certainty about the union's majority status. In contrast, the "substantial, objective evidence" standard adopted by the Fifth, Sixth, and Ninth Circuits provides a more balanced approach, allowing employers to poll employees while protecting the union's interests.

In this case, the NLRB's determination that Allentown Mack lacked a good-faith reasonable doubt about the union's majority status is not supported by substantial evidence. The Administrative Law Judge (ALJ) and the NLRB ignored or discounted significant evidence of employee dissatisfaction with the union, including statements from multiple employees. The NLRB's conclusion is arbitrary and capricious, as it fails to consider the totality of the evidence (Thomas Indus., Inc., 687 F.2d at 868).

The court should defer to the reasoning of the Fifth, Sixth, and Ninth Circuits and hold that the NLRB's "good-faith reasonable doubt" standard is not rational and consistent with the NLRA. The court should also find that the NLRB's factual determination is not supported by substantial evidence and reverse the NLRB's order.
