I. The U.S. Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit Lacks Jurisdiction to Review the Merit Systems Protection Board’s Dismissal of Anthony Perry’s Mixed Case for Lack of Jurisdiction.

The Civil Service Reform Act of 1978 (CSRA) governs judicial review of Merit Systems Protection Board (MSPB or Board) decisions. 5 U.S.C. § 1101 et seq. As a general matter, the CSRA channels review of MSPB decisions to the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit. Id. § 7703(b)(1)(A). However, an exception exists for "cases of discrimination," also known as "mixed cases," which are subject to review in federal district court. Id. § 7703(b)(2); Kloeckner v. Solis, 568 U.S. 41, 44 (2012). A mixed case is defined as one in which an employee "has been affected by an action which [she] may appeal to the Merit Systems Protection Board, and alleges that a basis for the action was discrimination prohibited by" certain federal laws. 5 U.S.C. § 7702(a)(1).

The question presented is whether a mixed case dismissed by the MSPB for lack of jurisdiction falls within the exception to Federal Circuit review. The D.C. Circuit, in Powell v. Department of Defense, 158 F.3d 597 (D.C. Cir. 1998), held that it does not. However, the Supreme Court's subsequent decision in Kloeckner v. Solis effectively overruled Powell.

In Kloeckner, the Court held that when the MSPB dismisses a mixed case on procedural grounds, judicial review lies in district court, not the Federal Circuit. 568 U.S. at 56. Although Kloeckner did not directly address jurisdictional dismissals, its reasoning is equally applicable to such cases. The Court's analysis turned on the statutory text, specifically the definition of a mixed case under 5 U.S.C. § 7702(a)(1). The Court observed that a mixed case is one in which an employee "has been affected by an action which [she] may appeal to the" MSPB. Kloeckner, 568 U.S. at 44 (quoting 5 U.S.C. § 7702(a)(1)(A)). The Court's emphasis was on the nature of the claim brought before the MSPB, not the MSPB's ultimate decision on jurisdiction.

Perry's argument that Kloeckner eviscerates the distinction between procedural and jurisdictional dismissals is persuasive. The statutory language does not support a distinction between the two. See Conforto v. Merit Sys. Prot. Bd., 713 F.3d 1111, 1118 n.1 (Fed. Cir. 2013) (Newman, J., concurring in part and dissenting in part). In both cases, the employee alleges discrimination and an adverse action appealable to the MSPB. The MSPB's dismissal, whether on procedural or jurisdictional grounds, does not change the fact that the employee brought a mixed case.

Furthermore, the Federal Circuit's own precedent acknowledges that the distinction between procedural and jurisdictional dismissals is not meaningful in this context. See Conforto, 713 F.3d at 1118 n.1 (noting that the Board's authority to waive procedural defects, but not jurisdictional defects, is a "distinction without a difference" in this context).

The practical implications of sending jurisdictional dismissals to the Federal Circuit, while sending procedural dismissals to district court, would create uncertainty and inconsistency. Perry's case illustrates this point. He brought a mixed case to the MSPB, alleging discrimination and challenging the voluntariness of his retirement and suspension. The MSPB dismissed his appeal for lack of jurisdiction, finding that his actions were voluntary. Under the D.C. Circuit's holding in Powell, review of this decision would lie in the Federal Circuit. However, if the MSPB had dismissed Perry's appeal as untimely, review would lie in district court, pursuant to Kloeckner.

In conclusion, the Supreme Court's decision in Kloeckner v. Solis undermines the D.C. Circuit's holding in Powell v. Department of Defense. A mixed case dismissed by the MSPB for lack of jurisdiction is subject to judicial review in district court, not the Federal Circuit. Therefore, this Court should reverse the D.C. Circuit's decision transferring Perry's case to the Federal Circuit and hold that the proper forum for review is a federal district court.
