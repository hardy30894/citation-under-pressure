## ARGUMENT

The reinstatement statute, 8 U.S.C. § 1231(a)(5), became effective on April 1, 1997. Petitioner Fernandez-Vargas reentered the United States illegally in 1982—fifteen years before the statute existed. The statute cannot apply to him, and even if it could, retroactive application would be impermissible.

### I. The Statute Does Not Apply to Pre-Enactment Reentries

This Court has long held that no statute shall be given retrospective operation unless its language is "so clear, plain, and unequivocal that they admit of no other meaning." *United States v. Heth*, 3 Cranch 399, 413 (1806). Section 1231(a)(5) speaks entirely in the present tense: the Attorney General may reinstate a prior order when he "finds that an alien has reentered." Nothing in this text manifests an intent to reach reentries completed before the statute's enactment. Congress could have written "at any time" or "regardless of when" to express retroactive intent. It did not.

This ambiguity must be resolved in favor of the alien, for "deportation is a drastic measure," and "statutes imposing it must be narrowly construed." *Mahler v. Eby*, 264 U.S. 32, 40 (1924). Indeed, this Court has repeatedly instructed that deportation statutes "must be construed in favor of the alien when doubts remain." *Fong Haw Tan v. Phelan*, 333 U.S. 6, 10 (1948). The statute therefore applies only to reentries occurring after its effective date.

### II. Retroactive Application Would Be Impermissibly Retroactive

Even if the statute could be read to encompass pre-1997 reentries, applying it to petitioner would attach new legal consequences to past conduct and violate the bedrock "presumption against retroactive legislation." *Union Pacific R. Co. v. Laramie Stock Yards Co.*, 231 U.S. 190, 199 (1913). A statute "is not to be given retroactive effect unless that purpose is clearly expressed." *United States v. St. Louis, S.F. & T. Ry. Co.*, 270 U.S. 1, 3 (1926). No such clear expression appears here.

When petitioner reentered in 1982, the law permitted him to later apply for adjustment of status if he married a United States citizen. *Barber v. Gonzales*, 347 U.S. 637, 642 (1954) (recognizing that "the law accords the alien a full and fair hearing" and opportunities for relief). The reinstatement statute eliminated that possibility entirely, barring any form of relief. This new bar alters the legal consequences of a reentry completed long before the statute's enactment. Such a change is impermissibly retroactive because it "impairs rights a party possessed when he acted." *Greene v. United States*, 376 U.S. 149, 160 (1964) (quoting *Union Pacific*, 231 U.S. at 199). Petitioner's reentry in 1982 carried with it the prospect of eventual adjustment. The 1997 statute stripped that prospect away.

This Court has expressly refused to apply deportation statutes retroactively to reach past conduct. In *Delgadillo v. Carmichael*, 332 U.S. 388, 391 (1947), the Court declined to apply a deportation ground to an alien who had returned before the ground's enactment, holding that "the law must be construed to be prospective only unless a contrary intent is clearly expressed." The same principle governs here. The reinstatement statute imposes a harsh new consequence—foreclosing all relief—on petitioner's pre-1997 reentry. As *Claridge v. Evans*, 137 F.2d 347, 349 (7th Cir. 1943), teaches, "it is a fundamental rule that statutes are to be construed as prospective only unless a contrary intention is clearly expressed."

The Tenth Circuit erred in holding that petitioner lacked a "protectable expectation" of adjusting his status. An alien who reenters illegally does not forfeit all future claims to relief. *See Fong Haw Tan*, 333 U.S. at 10 (the "grave nature of deportation" requires that "the statute be construed in favor of the alien"). The expectation of applying for adjustment under then-existing law was a legitimate right that the government could not retroactively extinguish without clear congressional command. *Heth*, 3 Cranch at 413. No such command appears in Section 1231(a)(5).

Retroactive application also offends due process by upsetting settled reliance interests. When petitioner returned in 1982, the legal landscape permitted him to later seek adjustment if he married a citizen. The 1997 statute retroactively destroyed that avenue, making his earlier conduct the basis for permanent exclusion from relief. This is precisely the kind of retrospective change the presumption against retroactivity is designed to prevent. *St. Louis, S.F. & T. Ry. Co.*, 270 U.S. at 3.

For these reasons, Section 1231(a)(5) does not apply to aliens who reentered before its effective date. Even if it did, applying it retroactively would be impermissible. The petition for review should be granted.
