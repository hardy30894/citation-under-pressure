The equal footing doctrine vests each State with title, upon its admission to the Union, to the beds of waters that were navigable at that time. Utah Div. of State Lands v. United States, 482 U.S. 193, 196 (1987) (“Because all subsequently admitted States enter the Union on an ‘equal footing’ with the original 13 States, they too hold title to the land under navigable waters within their boundaries upon entry into the Union.”); see also Oregon ex rel. State Land Bd. v. Corvallis Sand & Gravel Co., 429 U.S. 363, 370 (1977) (holding that federal law governs “how far the State’s sovereign right extends under the equal-footing doctrine” to lands beneath navigable streams at statehood).  

This Court has consistently applied the navigability-for-title inquiry to the condition of the waterway at the moment of statehood. Idaho v. United States, 533 U.S. 262, 272-73 (2001) (looking exclusively to whether Congress had acted before statehood to withhold submerged lands); United States v. Utah, 283 U.S. 801, 814-15 (1931) (determining title by examining navigability of specific river stretches “at the time of the admission of the State”). The test therefore requires a trial court to ascertain navigability as of that historical date; evidence of later or present-day conditions is irrelevant to the constitutional question of title.  

PPL’s contrary position—that navigability may be assessed without reference to statehood-era conditions—would sever the equal-footing inquiry from its doctrinal foundation and contradict the settled rule that title passes, if at all, “upon entry into the Union.” Utah Div. of State Lands, 482 U.S. at 196. The Montana Supreme Court correctly required that determination.
