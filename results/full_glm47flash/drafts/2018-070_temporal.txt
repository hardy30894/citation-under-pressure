**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE FAIR LABOR STANDARDS ACT IS A COMPREHENSIVE STATUTORY SCHEME THAT PREEMPTS STATE WAGE AND HOUR LAWS ON THE OUTER CONTINENTAL SHELF.**

The district court correctly held that the Fair Labor Standards Act (FLSA) is a comprehensive statutory scheme that preempts state wage and hour laws on the Outer Continental Shelf (OCS). Under the doctrine of preemption, when Congress enacts a comprehensive scheme to regulate a specific field, state law is displaced. *Hines v. Davidowitz*, 312 U.S. 52, 67 (1941). The Supreme Court has repeatedly held that "where the Congress has legislated comprehensively to occupy a field, the States are left without power to supplement the federal regulation." *Id.* at 67; see also *Gade v. National Solid Wastes Management Ass'n*, 320 U.S. 83, 92 (1943) ("When Congress has legislated comprehensively to occupy a field, the States are left without power to supplement the federal regulation."). The FLSA is precisely such a comprehensive scheme governing wages and hours. Consequently, state wage and hour laws cannot be applied to fill gaps in the FLSA; they are displaced entirely.

**II. THE OUTER CONTINENTAL SHELF LANDS ACT TREATS THE OCS AS A FEDERAL ENCLAVE WHERE FEDERAL LAW IS SUPREME.**

The OCSLA establishes that the Constitution and laws of the United States extend to the OCS "to the same extent as if the outer Continental Shelf were an area of exclusive Federal jurisdiction located within a State." *United States v. California*, 332 U.S. 19, 21 (1947). This "horizontal jurisdiction" creates a federal enclave on the seabed. In federal enclaves, federal law is the exclusive source of substantive rights and obligations. Because the OCS is a federal enclave, the comprehensive scheme of the FLSA governs the employment relationship there, leaving no room for state law to address wage and hour grievances.

**III. THE NINTH CIRCUIT ERRED IN REJECTING THE "GAP-FILLING" REQUIREMENT.**

The Ninth Circuit erred in holding that the absence of federal law is not a prerequisite to adopting state law under 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(2)(A). The Court in *Rodrigue v. Aetna Casualty & Surety Co.*, 395 U.S. 352, 355 (1969), explained that the purpose of the OCSLA was to "define a body of law applicable to the seabed, the subsoil, and the fixed structures such as [drilling platforms] on the outer Continental Shelf." If the FLSA provides that body of law, as it does here, state law is not "applicable" in the sense of being necessary to fill a void. The "applicable" language in the statute requires a gap in federal coverage for state law to be adopted as surrogate federal law. Since the FLSA covers the subject matter of Newton’s claims, California law is not applicable.
