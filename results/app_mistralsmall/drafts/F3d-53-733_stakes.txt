**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN ADMITTING THE EXPERT OPINION TESTIMONY OF AMERICAN STEAMSHIP’S WITNESS REGARDING THE CAUSE OF THE LINE’S PARTING.**

The district court abused its discretion in admitting the testimony of American Steamship’s expert, Mr. Harold Jensen, regarding the cause of the line’s parting. *Daubert v. Merrell Dow Pharms., Inc.*, 509 U.S. 579, 589 (1993) (holding that the trial court acts as a "gatekeeper" to ensure that expert testimony is both reliable and relevant). Jensen’s opinion was not based on any recognized scientific or technical methodology but was instead a mere speculation unsupported by any empirical data or testing. *See Kumho Tire Co. v. Carmichael*, 526 U.S. 137, 147 (1999) (requiring expert testimony to be grounded in "scientific knowledge").

Jensen testified that the line parted due to "fatigue failure," yet he admitted that he did not perform any metallurgical analysis, stress testing, or microscopic examination of the line to confirm this theory. *See id.* at 150 (finding expert testimony unreliable where it "lacked sufficient foundation"). The line was not preserved for inspection, and no expert was retained to examine it before it was discarded. Jensen’s opinion was thus based on conjecture rather than reliable principles and methods. *See id.* at 156-57 (holding that expert testimony must be "derived by the scientific method").

Moreover, Jensen’s opinion was irrelevant because the line’s parting was not the cause of Cook’s injury. The evidence showed that the line was properly rigged and that the bos’n’s chair was functioning as intended when Cook fell. *See Fed. R. Evid. 401* (defining relevant evidence as that which "has any tendency to make a fact more or less probable"). The district court’s admission of Jensen’s testimony was prejudicial and warrants reversal.

**II. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN DENYING COOK’S MOTION FOR JUDGMENT AS A MATTER OF LAW ON THE ISSUE OF UNSEAWORTHINESS.**

The district court erred in denying Cook’s motion for judgment as a matter of law on the issue of unseaworthiness. *See Miles v. Melrose Metro. Dist. Corp.*, 882 F.2d 976, 981 (6th Cir. 1989) (holding that a shipowner’s duty to provide a seaworthy vessel is non-delegable and absolute). The evidence established that the American Steamship Company failed to maintain the bos’n’s chair and its supporting equipment in a reasonably safe condition.

First, the boom was improperly maintained. The crew failed to use winter grease in the bearings, causing the boom to freeze and preventing it from swinging outboard. *See *Gutierrez v. Waterman S.S. Corp.*, 372 U.S. 205, 214 (1963) (holding that a shipowner is liable for unseaworthy conditions caused by its failure to maintain equipment). Second, the line used to support the bos’n’s chair was improperly stored and maintained. The line had been left rigged during summer months, exposing it to moisture and wear, and it was not inspected for defects before use. *See *Mitchell v. Trawler Racer, Inc.*, 362 U.S. 539, 550 (1960) (finding unseaworthiness where the shipowner failed to inspect and maintain equipment).

The district court’s denial of Cook’s motion for judgment as a matter of law on the issue of unseaworthiness was error. The evidence overwhelmingly established that the vessel was unseaworthy, and the jury should not have been permitted to second-guess this conclusion. *See *Pope & Talbot, Inc. v. Hawn*, 346 U.S. 406, 409 (1953) (holding that the issue of unseaworthiness is for the court, not the jury, to determine).

**III. THE DISTRICT COURT DID NOT ERR IN ADMITTING EVIDENCE OF COOK’S ALCOHOL-RELATED AILMENTS.**

The district court did not err in admitting evidence of Cook’s alcohol-related ailments under Federal Rule of Evidence 404(b). *See Huddleston v. United States*, 485 U.S. 681, 685 (1988) (holding that Rule 404(b) permits the admission of prior bad acts to prove motive, opportunity, intent, preparation, plan, knowledge, identity, or absence of mistake). Cook’s alcohol-related ailments were relevant to the issue of comparative negligence, as they may have impaired his ability to perform his duties safely. *See *McCorstin v. United States*, 8 F.3d 804, 807 (6th Cir. 1993) (holding that evidence of a plaintiff’s prior injuries is admissible to show comparative negligence).

The district court properly weighed the probative value of this evidence against its prejudicial effect under Rule 403. *See *United States v. Johnson*, 27 F.3d 1186, 1190 (6th Cir. 1994) (holding that the district court’s Rule 403 balancing is reviewed for abuse of discretion). The evidence was not unduly prejudicial, as it was not offered to show that Cook was a "bad person" but rather to establish that his alcohol-related ailments may have contributed to his injury. *See *Huddleston*, 485 U.S. at 691-92 (holding that Rule 404(b) evidence is not unduly prejudicial where it is probative of a material issue).

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the district court should be vacated and the case remanded for a new trial.
