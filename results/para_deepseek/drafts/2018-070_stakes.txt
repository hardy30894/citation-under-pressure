**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Plain Text and Structure of the OCSLA Require a Gap-Filling Role for State Law**

The Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act ("OCSLA"), 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(2)(A), provides that state law applies on the outer Continental Shelf "[t]o the extent that [state law is] applicable and not inconsistent with this subchapter or with other Federal laws." This language does not authorize state law whenever it pertains to the subject matter of a lawsuit; instead, it contemplates state law serving as surrogate federal law only where federal law is absent. This Court's decisions interpreting analogous statutes confirm that assimilation provisions require a gap in federal law before state law may be adopted.

In *Rodrigue v. Aetna Casualty & Surety Co.*, 395 U.S. 352 (1969), this Court explained that OCSLA was designed to "define a body of law applicable to the seabed, the subsoil, and the fixed structures" on the OCS. *Id.* at 355. Congress recognized that federal law "was never designed to be a complete body of law in and of itself," 99 Cong. Rec. 6963 (1953), and therefore borrowed state law to fill gaps. The OCSLA's structure—borrowing state law only to fill the interstices of federal law—reflects the settled principle that state law cannot apply where federal law already provides comprehensive coverage.

**II. The FLSA Is a Comprehensive Federal Scheme That Leaves No Gap for State Law**

The Fair Labor Standards Act ("FLSA"), 29 U.S.C. § 201 *et seq.*, establishes a comprehensive federal regime governing wages, hours, and overtime. This Court has recognized that the FLSA is "a comprehensive remedial scheme" that occupies the field of wage and hour regulation for employees covered by its provisions. *See Christensen v. Harris County*, 529 U.S. 576, 585 (2000). The FLSA contains detailed standards for minimum wage, overtime, recordkeeping, and enforcement, and it expressly permits more protective state law only as to minimum wage and overtime standards. *See* 29 U.S.C. § 218(a).

Where federal law provides a comprehensive scheme, state law cannot apply as surrogate federal law under OCSLA because there is no "gap" for state law to fill. The Fifth Circuit's test, articulated in *Continental Oil Co. v. London S.S. Owners' Mutual Insurance Ass'n*, 417 F.2d 1030 (5th Cir. 1969), correctly requires that state law be necessary "to fill a significant void or gap" in federal law. *Id.* at 1036. The FLSA's comprehensive coverage of wages and hours leaves no such void. The FLSA's savings clause, 29 U.S.C. § 218(a), permits states to apply their own wage laws to employees within their territorial jurisdiction. But the OCS is not within California's territorial jurisdiction. *See* 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(1) (treating OCS as "an area of exclusive Federal jurisdiction"). The savings clause therefore does not authorize California to impose its wage and hour laws on the OCS.

**III. The Ninth Circuit's Interpretation Contradicts OCSLA's Text and Federalism Principles**

The Ninth Circuit held that state law applies on the OCS whenever it is "applicable and not inconsistent" with federal law, regardless of whether federal law leaves a gap. That interpretation misreads the statutory phrase "[t]o the extent that they are applicable and not inconsistent." 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(2)(A). The phrase "[t]o the extent that" is a limitation, not an authorization. It cabins the application of state law to those situations where federal law is not already speaking to the issue. This Court has construed analogous language in the Assimilative Crimes Act, 18 U.S.C. § 13(a), to require a gap in federal law before state law may be adopted. *See United States v. Sharpnack*, 355 U.S. 286, 292-93 (1958) (federal law must be "deficient" before state law applies). The same logic governs OCSLA.

The Ninth Circuit's approach also undermines the uniformity that OCSLA was designed to achieve. Allowing each adjacent state's wage and hour laws to apply on OCS platforms would subject a single industry to a patchwork of conflicting state regulations. Congress could not have intended such a result when it created "exclusive Federal jurisdiction" over the OCS. 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(1).

**IV. The FLSA Preempts California's Meal-Period and Other Wage Laws**

Even if California's wage and hour laws could apply on the OCS, they are "inconsistent" with the FLSA's regulatory scheme. 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(2)(A). The FLSA does not require employers to provide meal periods, and its regulations permit employers to structure workweeks flexibly. *See* 29 C.F.R. § 785.19. California's requirement of a 30-minute meal period for every five hours worked, Cal. Lab. Code § 226.7, imposes an affirmative obligation not found in federal law. While the FLSA's savings clause permits states to supplement federal minimum standards, the OCSLA's "not inconsistent" requirement is broader: it bars state law that conflicts with federal law, not merely state law that federal law preempts. California's meal-period law is inconsistent with the FLSA's decision not to mandate such breaks.

**CONCLUSION**

The judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
