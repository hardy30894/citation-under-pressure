**Argument**

The Equal Protection Clause does not provide a free-floating federal tort remedy for every claim of unfairness by a single property owner. It forbids a State from denying any person the equal protection of the laws, but equal protection doctrine has always been concerned with discrimination against persons who are treated differently because they belong to an identifiable class. *Yick Wo v. Hopkins*, 118 U.S. 356 (1886); *Gulf, Colorado & Santa Fe Ry. Co. v. Ellis*, 165 U.S. 150 (1897). The Court has repeatedly held that the Constitution does not require that all persons be treated alike in every circumstance, only that classifications rest on a rational basis and not on forbidden grounds. *Cotting v. Kansas City Stock Yards Co.*, 183 U.S. 79 (1901); *F.S. Royster Guano Co. v. Virginia*, 253 U.S. 412 (1920).

Petitioner’s claim fails because respondent alleges neither membership in a class nor discrimination based on class status. The complaint asserts only that the Village demanded a larger easement from respondent than from others similarly situated. But differential treatment, standing alone, does not state an equal protection violation. In *Missouri v. Lewis*, 101 U.S. 22 (1880), the Court explained that equal protection is satisfied so long as the law operates alike upon all persons within the same class. Likewise, in *Heath & Milligan Manufacturing Co. v. Worst*, 207 U.S. 338 (1907), the Court upheld differing treatment where the classification was not arbitrary. The Constitution does not constitutionalize ordinary disputes over municipal administration.

Nor may respondent recast an individualized grievance as a “class of one” claim untethered to any recognized constitutional principle. This Court has rejected equal protection challenges where the asserted injury is merely the product of discretionary administration absent a showing of invidious classification. In *Lindsley v. Natural Carbonic Gas Co.*, 220 U.S. 61 (1911), the Court held that a classification will be upheld if any state of facts reasonably may be conceived to justify it, and that those attacking the classification bear the burden of negating every conceivable basis that might support it. See also *Chicago, Burlington & Quincy R.R. Co. v. McGuire*, 219 U.S. 549 (1911). Respondent alleges at most that the Village made a mistaken or excessive demand. That does not negate the existence of a rational basis for the Village’s action.

The Court’s equal protection decisions also confirm that selective enforcement is not unconstitutional merely because it is uneven. The evil targeted by the Clause is arbitrary discrimination, not every deviation from uniform treatment. *Snowden v. Hughes*, 321 U.S. 1 (1944), held that unequal application of a law, without intentional or purposeful discrimination, does not violate equal protection. And in *Tigner v. Texas*, 310 U.S. 141 (1940), the Court reiterated that states may draw distinctions so long as the line is not wholly arbitrary. Respondent alleges no discrimination based on race, national origin, or any other suspect classification, and no denial of a fundamental right. Her claim therefore falls outside established equal protection doctrine.

To recognize a “class of one” cause of action here would also convert federal courts into superintendents of routine land-use and utility disputes. This Court has long refused to constitutionalize every alleged municipal unfairness. In *Salsburg v. Maryland*, 346 U.S. 545 (1954), the Court upheld a state’s selective evidentiary rule because legislatures retain wide latitude in line drawing. In *McGowan v. Maryland*, 366 U.S. 420 (1961), the Court explained that a statutory classification must be upheld if any reasonably conceivable state of facts could provide a rational basis. Those principles foreclose respondent’s attempt to litigate an ordinary easement disagreement as a federal constitutional case.

Finally, the Seventh Circuit’s “spiteful effort to get him” formulation cannot be squared with this Court’s precedents. Equal protection is not a generalized anti-bad-faith guarantee. Its function is to police unconstitutional classifications, not to remedy every allegedly ill-motivated act by local officials. *Snowden*, 321 U.S. 1; *Lindsley*, 220 U.S. 61; *McGowan*, 366 U.S. 420. If respondent believes the Village acted improperly, state law supplies remedies for arbitrary municipal conduct. But the Fourteenth Amendment does not create a cause of action simply because one homeowner contends she was singled out for unfavorable treatment.

The judgment should be reversed.
