**ARGUMENT**

This Court should affirm the judgment below because Carol Anne Bond, an individual criminal defendant, lacks standing to challenge her conviction on the theory that 18 U.S.C. § 229 exceeds Congress's enumerated powers and offends the Tenth Amendment. That Amendment protects the sovereign prerogatives of the States as governmental entities; it does not create a personal right that a private litigant may wield to escape a validly enacted federal criminal statute.

**I. Standing Is a Threshold Jurisdictional Question That Must Be Resolved Before Any Consideration of the Merits.**

Before reaching Bond's constitutional arguments, this Court must first determine whether she may raise them at all. As this Court has explained, when jurisdictional and merits questions are intertwined, a court must resolve "the inquiry as to standing" at the threshold rather than assume it and proceed to the merits. *Steel Co. v. Citizens for a Better Env't*, 523 U.S. 83, 732 (1998) (explaining that a court must determine "whether the statute in question authorizes review at the behest of the plaintiff" before addressing the underlying claim). That principle governs here. Bond does not merely argue that her own liberty interest was violated by an unconstitutional prosecution; she argues that Congress lacked the constitutional authority, vis-à-vis the States, to enact § 229 at all. That is a federalism claim belonging to the States, not to Bond, and this Court should not reach its merits unless and until it first concludes that Bond is a proper party to assert it.

**II. The Tenth Amendment Protects the Reserved Powers of the States, and Only the States or Their Officers May Invoke That Protection.**

This Court's decision in *Tennessee Electric Power Co. v. Tennessee Valley Authority*, 306 U.S. 118 (1939), forecloses Bond's claim of standing. There, private utility companies argued that the federal government's sale of electric power invaded a domain reserved to the States under the Tenth Amendment. This Court rejected that argument on standing grounds, holding that the private companies, "absent the states or their officers, have no standing in this suit to raise any question under the [Tenth] Amendment." *Id.* at 144. The logic of that holding is straightforward: the Tenth Amendment "protects the sovereign rights of States, not the personal rights of individuals," and a private party's interest in avoiding federal regulation—or, as here, federal prosecution—is not the same as the State's sovereign interest in maintaining the constitutional balance of power. Bond's situation is functionally identical to that of the utility companies in *Tennessee Electric Power*. She, like they, seeks to invoke the structural protections of federalism to serve a purely private end: avoiding conviction. But the Tenth Amendment does not exist to protect her personal liberty; it exists to protect the States' reserved sovereignty, and only the States—or officers acting on their behalf—may complain that Congress has invaded it.

This structural understanding of federalism as belonging to the States is reinforced by this Court's recognition that "[u]nder our federal system the administration of criminal justice rests with the States except as Congress, acting within the scope of [its] delegated powers, has created offenses against the United States." *Screws v. United States*, 325 U.S. 91, 109 (1945). That principle describes a relationship between two sovereigns—the States and the National Government—not a personal entitlement running to individual defendants. If Congress has, in fact, transgressed the limits of its delegated powers by legislating in a field reserved to the States, the remedy lies with the States themselves, through litigation brought by a State or its officers, or through the political process. It is the States, not scattered individual defendants prosecuted under a validly enacted statute, who are positioned to vindicate the structural balance the Tenth Amendment protects.

**III. Permitting Any Convicted Defendant to Raise a Tenth Amendment Objection Would Convert a Structural Safeguard into a Freestanding Defense Available to Every Federal Defendant.**

Bond's approach would transform the Tenth Amendment from a guarantee of the States' reserved sovereignty into a universal affirmative defense available to any person prosecuted under any federal criminal statute that arguably touches on subject matter also regulated by the States. Nothing in this Court's precedents supports so sweeping an expansion. To the contrary, this Court has been careful to police the threshold jurisdictional question of whether a particular litigant is the right party to raise a particular constitutional claim, resolving that question before ever reaching the merits. *Steel Co.*, 523 U.S. at 732. Bond's dispute is with Myrlinda Haynes, not with the constitutional order between the federal government and the Commonwealth of Pennsylvania. Pennsylvania has raised no objection to her prosecution under § 229, and it is Pennsylvania—not Bond—whose sovereign interests the Tenth Amendment was designed to protect.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should hold that Bond lacks standing to challenge her conviction on Tenth Amendment grounds and should affirm the judgment of the Court of Appeals.
