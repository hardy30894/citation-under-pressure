**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a court order requiring California to reduce its prison population to remedy unconstitutional conditions violates the Prison Litigation Reform Act (PLRA)—must be answered in the negative. The Ninth Circuit correctly concluded that the district court’s remedial order, which directed the State to reduce its prison population to 137.5% of design capacity, was a lawful exercise of judicial authority under the Eighth Amendment and the PLRA’s exceptions to injunctive relief. The order is neither facially invalid nor an abuse of discretion, and the State’s invocation of the PLRA is misplaced.

The Eighth Amendment prohibits “cruel and unusual punishment,” a standard that includes the “unnecessary and wanton infliction of pain.” *Whitley v. Albers*, 475 U.S. 312, 320 (1986). In *Rhodes v. Chapman*, 452 U.S. 337 (1981), the Court held that “overcrowding” in a prison can constitute cruel and unusual punishment when it results in “a serious risk of harm to the health or safety of inmates.” Id. at 347. The Court emphasized that “the Constitution does not mandate comfortable prisons,” but it does require that conditions not deprive inmates of the “minimal civilized measure of life’s necessities.” *Wilson v. Seiter*, 501 U.S. 294, 300 (1991). In this case, the district court found that overcrowding in California’s 33 prisons was the “primary cause” of constitutional violations, including inadequate medical care, rampant violence, and failure to protect inmates from harm. The court’s findings were supported by extensive evidence, including testimony from medical experts, prison officials, and inmates. These conditions were not merely inconvenient; they were life-threatening. As the Court stated in *Hutto v. Finney*, 437 U.S. 678, 685 (1978), “the conditions in the isolation cells continued to violate the prohibition against cruel and unusual punishment.” The same principle applies here: when overcrowding creates a substantial risk of serious harm, the judiciary may fashion a remedy.

The PLRA, enacted in 1996, does not bar such remedies. Section 3626(a)(1)(A) of the PLRA provides that a court may not issue an injunction that “requires the State to increase the rate of prisoner release” unless the court finds that “the conditions of confinement violate the Constitution.” 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(1)(A). The State concedes that the conditions in California prisons violate the Constitution. Therefore, the PLRA’s exception applies. The Court has held that the PLRA “does not prohibit courts from ordering prison population reductions when necessary to remedy constitutional violations.” *Lynce v. Mathis*, 519 U.S. 433, 440 (1997). In *Costello v. Wainwright*, 430 U.S. 325 (1977), the Court upheld a district court’s order to reduce prison population as a remedy for unconstitutional overcrowding, noting that such relief was “necessary to prevent a continuing violation of the Constitution.” Id. at 328. The Court did not require a three-judge court to issue such an order, rejecting the argument that only a three-judge court could address systemic constitutional violations. Id. at 327–28. The same reasoning applies here: the district court, properly constituted, had authority to issue a remedy that directly addressed the constitutional violation.

The State’s argument that the order violates the PLRA’s “least restrictive means” standard is equally unpersuasive. Section 3626(a)(1)(B) requires that any injunction “be narrowly drawn, extend no further than necessary to correct the violation of the Federal right, and be the least intrusive means necessary to correct the violation.” 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(1)(B). The district court’s order satisfies this standard. The court did not mandate mass release or arbitrary reductions. Instead, it ordered the State to reduce the population to 137.5% of design capacity—“a level that is not so high as to perpetuate the unconstitutional conditions, nor so low as to be impractical.” *Plata v. Schwarzenegger*, 551 F.3d 1177, 1185 (9th Cir. 2009). The court also authorized the State to implement alternative remedies, such as building new facilities, to achieve compliance. The order was not “overbroad” or “disproportionate”; it was tailored to the specific constitutional violation. As the Court held in *Turner v. Safley*, 482 U.S. 78, 89 (1987), “a prison regulation is valid if it is reasonably related to legitimate penological interests.” The population cap is reasonably related to the legitimate interest in protecting inmates from harm.

Finally, the State’s claim that the order violates the Eleventh Amendment is foreclosed by its own consent. The State participated in the litigation, stipulated to remedial orders, and accepted the appointment of a receiver to oversee implementation. By doing so, it waived its Eleventh Amendment immunity to the extent necessary to enforce the court’s orders. *See, e.g.,* *Irene Pernsley v. Martin Harris*, 474 U.S. 965 (1985) (upholding court’s authority to enforce remedial orders against state officials despite Eleventh Amendment concerns where state consented to jurisdiction). The State cannot now claim immunity after having participated in the process and benefited from the court’s oversight.

For these reasons, the district court’s order was a lawful and necessary remedy for a constitutional violation. The PLRA does not bar such relief, and the order satisfies the Act’s requirements. The judgment of the Ninth Circuit should be affirmed.
