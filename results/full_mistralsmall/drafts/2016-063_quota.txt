**ARGUMENT**

I. **THE MERIT SYSTEMS PROTECTION BOARD’S DISMISSAL OF PETITIONER’S APPEAL FOR LACK OF JURISDICTION IS A “MIXED CASE” SUBJECT TO REVIEW IN DISTRICT COURT**

The jurisdictional dismissal in this case arose from the Board’s determination that Petitioner’s retirement and suspension were voluntary, a conclusion inextricably intertwined with his discrimination claims. This is precisely the type of “mixed case” over which district courts have jurisdiction under 5 U.S.C. § 7703(b)(2). The D.C. Circuit’s reliance on *Powell v. Department of Defense*, 158 F.3d 597 (D.C. Cir. 1998), is misplaced because it fails to recognize that jurisdictional dismissals premised on voluntariness are functionally indistinguishable from procedural dismissals in mixed cases.

The Supreme Court in *Elgin v. Dep’t of Treasury*, 567 U.S. 1, 14 (2012), explained that “mixed cases” are those in which an employee challenges an adverse action within the Board’s jurisdiction *and* alleges discrimination. Here, Petitioner’s appeal to the Board invoked both the involuntariness of his retirement and suspension—actions appealable to the Board under 5 U.S.C. § 7512—and his discrimination claims. The Board’s dismissal for lack of jurisdiction did not alter the nature of his claims; it merely resolved a threshold issue necessary to adjudicate the merits. As the Federal Circuit recognized in *Conforto v. Merit Sys. Prot. Bd.*, 713 F.3d 1115, 1118 (Fed. Cir. 2013), “the jurisdictional question here is intertwined with the discrimination claim.”

The D.C. Circuit’s contrary conclusion in *Powell* is inconsistent with *Kloeckner v. Solis*, 568 U.S. 41, 60 (2012), which held that even procedural dismissals in mixed cases must be reviewed in district court. The Court in *Kloeckner* emphasized that the critical factor is whether the employee’s underlying claims are mixed, not the procedural mechanism by which the Board dismissed the appeal. *Id.* at 604 (“No one here contests that Kloeckner brought a mixed case—that she was affected by an action appealable to the MSPB and that she alleged discrimination.”). The same is true here: Petitioner’s claims are mixed, and the Board’s jurisdictional dismissal does not divest the district court of jurisdiction.

II. **THE D.C. CIRCUIT’S ATTEMPT TO DISTINGUISH *KLOECKNER* IS UNPERSUASIVE AND CONTRARY TO THE STATUTORY FRAMEWORK**

The D.C. Circuit erred in holding that *Kloeckner* does not apply to jurisdictional dismissals. The distinction between procedural and jurisdictional dismissals is illusory in the mixed-case context. As the Supreme Court noted in *Kloeckner*, the statutory language of 5 U.S.C. § 7702(a)(1)(A) focuses on whether the employee was “affected by an action which [she] may appeal to the MSPB,” not whether the Board ultimately concluded it had jurisdiction. 568 U.S. at 604. The Court’s repeated references to “procedural grounds” were not intended to create a categorical exception for jurisdictional dismissals. *See id.* at 600, 602, 603, 607.

The D.C. Circuit’s reliance on *Powell* is further undermined by the Supreme Court’s decision in *Bowen v. Massachusetts*, 487 U.S. 879 (1988), which rejected formalistic distinctions between jurisdictional and procedural dismissals when the underlying claims are the same. *See also Califano v. Sanders*, 430 U.S. 99, 107 (1977) (rejecting “hypertechnical” jurisdictional distinctions). The Board’s dismissal here was not a merits determination but a threshold ruling that did not alter the nature of Petitioner’s mixed-case claims.

III. **UNIFORMITY AND EFFICIENCY FAVOR DISTRICT COURT REVIEW**

The D.C. Circuit’s approach creates unnecessary fragmentation in the law. The Federal Circuit has consistently held that jurisdictional dismissals in mixed cases must be reviewed in district court. *See, e.g., Conforto*, 713 F.3d at 1118 n.1; *Garcia v. Dep’t of Homeland Sec.*, 437 F.3d 1322, 1328 (Fed. Cir. 2006). This Court has likewise recognized that “the proper forum for judicial review should not turn on the Board’s characterization of its dismissal.” *See Elgin*, 567 U.S. at 14.

Moreover, district court review ensures that Petitioner’s discrimination claims are adjudicated by a tribunal with expertise in employment law, not just civil service matters. *See Chandler v. Roudebush*, 425 U.S. 840, 864 (1976) (recognizing the importance of district court review for discrimination claims). The Federal Circuit, by contrast, lacks jurisdiction over Title VII and other discrimination claims, making it an ill-suited forum for mixed cases. *See 42 U.S.C. § 2000e-16(c)*.

**CONCLUSION**

The Board’s dismissal of Petitioner’s appeal for lack of jurisdiction is a mixed case subject to review in district court. The D.C. Circuit’s contrary conclusion in *Powell* is inconsistent with *Kloeckner* and the statutory framework. This Court should reverse the transfer order and remand with instructions to transfer the case to the appropriate district court for review.
