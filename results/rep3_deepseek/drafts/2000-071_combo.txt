**ARGUMENT**

**I. The School Properly Excluded the Good News Club Because Its Activities Constituted Religious Worship, Not Secular Viewpoint Discussion.**

The Court of Appeals correctly held that Milford Central School created a limited public forum for “social, civic, and recreational” uses, not for religious worship or instruction. *See Hague v. Committee for Industrial Organization*, 307 U.S. 496 (1939). Once a governmental entity opens a nonpublic forum, it may restrict speech to the categories of uses for which the forum was opened. *See id.* at 515-16. Milford’s policy expressly excludes “religious purposes.” The Club’s meetings—including Bible lessons, prayer, scripture memorization, and an invitation to accept Jesus as a personal savior—are “quintessentially religious.” The Club sought not to discuss morality from a religious viewpoint, but to conduct religious worship and instruction, which falls outside the forum’s permitted uses.

The Supreme Court has long recognized that the state may draw content-based distinctions in a limited public forum, provided the distinctions are reasonable and viewpoint-neutral. *See Poulos v. New Hampshire*, 345 U.S. 395, 403-04 (1953); *Kovacs v. Cooper*, 336 U.S. 77, 86 (1949). Here, the Club’s activities are not a viewpoint on a secular subject; they are the subject of religion itself. As this Court stated in *Everson v. Board of Education*, 330 U.S. 1, 15-16 (1947), the First Amendment erects “a wall of separation between church and state” that prevents the state from sponsoring religious exercises. When a school opens its facilities to some groups but prohibits religious worship, it makes a permissible subject-matter distinction, not an unconstitutional viewpoint discrimination. *See Cantwell v. Connecticut*, 310 U.S. 296, 307 (1940) (state may regulate the “time, place, and manner” of religious speech without violating the First Amendment).

The Club argues that its religious activities are merely a viewpoint on morals and character. But the record demonstrates that the Club’s purpose is to convert children to Christianity, not to discuss moral philosophy. The lesson plan entitled “Israel Demands a King” explicitly distinguishes between “saved” and “unsaved” children and invites the latter to “believe on the Lord Jesus” for salvation. This is religious instruction, not viewpoint discussion. *See Cantwell*, 310 U.S. at 303-04 (undisputed that religious speech includes “the expression of belief and the practice of religion”). The state may exclude such activities from a limited forum without violating free speech. *See Cox v. New Hampshire*, 312 U.S. 569, 574 (1941) (state may impose neutral regulations on the use of public forums to prevent disruption).

Furthermore, the School District treated the Club no differently from any other group seeking to use the facilities for religious worship. The policy is facially neutral and has been applied evenhandedly. *See Fowler v. Rhode Island*, 345 U.S. 67, 69-70 (1953) (holding that a city may not discriminate among religious groups, but may bar all religious services from a public park). Here, the policy excludes all religious use, not just the Club’s. There is no evidence of discriminatory motive. *See Niemotko v. Maryland*, 340 U.S. 268, 272 (1951) (striking down a permit scheme that gave unbridled discretion to deny permits based on the applicant’s religious views). Milford’s policy provides clear, objective criteria and applies them uniformly.

**II. Excluding the Club Was Justified by the School’s Compelling Interest in Avoiding an Establishment Clause Violation.**

Even if the Club’s exclusion implicated its free speech rights, the school’s decision is justified by the need to comply with the Establishment Clause. *See Everson*, 330 U.S. at 15-16. Public schools have a uniquely important role in inculcating values and preserving religious neutrality. *See Illinois ex rel. McCollum v. Board of Education*, 333 U.S. 203, 212 (1948) (holding that religious instruction in public schools violates the Establishment Clause). Permitting the Good News Club to conduct proselytizing religious meetings on school premises—during the after-school hours but on campus—would create the appearance of official endorsement of religion, particularly given the young and impressionable age of the participating children. *See id.* at 209-10.

In *McCollum*, this Court invalidated a program that allowed religious teachers to offer instruction in public school buildings during the school day. *Id.* at 212. Although the Club’s meetings occur after school, the school’s provision of bus transportation and use of its facilities create a nexus that risks entangling the state with religion. *See Zorach v. Clauson*, 343 U.S. 306, 309 (1952) (distinguishing *McCollum* on the ground that religious instruction occurred off school premises). Here, the Club meets on school property, and the school previously provided busing to the church. The combination of location and logistical support could lead a reasonable observer to perceive the school as endorsing the Club’s religious message. *See Engel v. Vitale*, 370 U.S. 421, 430-31 (1962) (striking down school-sponsored prayer as a violation of the Establishment Clause).

The school’s interest in avoiding Establishment Clause violations is compelling and justifies any incidental burden on speech. *See Konigsberg v. State Bar of California*, 366 U.S. 36, 50-51 (1961) (recognizing that the state may regulate speech when necessary to serve a “compelling” state interest). Because the Club’s activities are inherently religious and its purpose includes evangelism, the school may exclude it to avoid running afoul of the constitutional command that the state “make no law respecting an establishment of religion.” *Everson*, 330 U.S. at 15.

**III. The Distinction Between Viewpoint and Subject-Matter Discrimination Is Proper.**

The Club contends that its religious activities are a “viewpoint” on the subject of morality. But the subject matter is not “morals and character” when the Club’s actual activities consist of prayer, Bible reading, and invitations to accept Christ. The Club did not propose to discuss morality from a Christian perspective; it proposed to conduct a religious service. *See Cantwell*, 310 U.S. at 307 (distinguishing between “religious beliefs” and “religious solicitation and propaganda”). The school’s policy opens the forum for “social, civic, and recreational” uses—not for evangelism or worship. Excluding the Club is a permissible subject-matter restriction. *See Kovacs*, 336 U.S. at 85-86 (upholding a city ordinance banning sound trucks on certain streets because the state may reasonably regulate the use of public property).

The lower court correctly recognized that allowing the Club would force the school to choose between violating the Establishment Clause and discriminating against religious speech. The better course is to exclude all religious worship, as the school has done. *See McCollum*, 333 U.S. at 212.

**Conclusion**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Court of Appeals should be affirmed.
