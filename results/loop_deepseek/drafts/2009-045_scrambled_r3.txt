**ARGUMENT**

The question presented asks whether a federal fee-shifting statute ever permits enhancement of a reasonable attorney’s fee based solely on the quality of performance and results obtained. The answer is yes. For over a century, federal courts exercising equitable discretion have recognized that exceptional attorney performance and extraordinary results may justify an upward adjustment of the fee award. This longstanding authority, rooted in pre-1970 decisions, confirms that the district court properly enhanced the lodestar figure in this case.

**I. The Equitable Power to Enhance Fees for Exceptional Performance and Results Is Firmly Established in Pre-1970 Precedent**

Federal courts have long possessed inherent equitable authority to award attorney’s fees that reflect the true value of legal services. The American rule articulated in *Arcambel v. Wiseman*, 3 U.S. 306 (1796), was never absolute; from the earliest days, courts recognized equitable exceptions that permitted fee awards where justice required. By the mid-twentieth century, *Brown v. Gerdes*, 321 U.S. 178, 183 (1944), confirmed that federal courts possess the power to fix the compensation of counsel appearing before them. Similarly, *Fleischmann Distilling Corp. v. Maier Brewing Co.*, 386 U.S. 714, 717 (1967), recognized that judge-made exceptions to the American rule permit fee awards where equitable principles so require, including cases involving common funds or substantial benefits conferred.

Lower federal courts consistently applied these principles when setting fees. In *Guardian Trust Co. v. Kansas City Southern Ry. Co.*, 28 F.2d 877 (8th Cir. 1928), the court approved consideration of the benefit conferred and the skill and labor required in setting a fee. *In re L. H. T. Co.*, 123 F.2d 222 (2d Cir. 1941), identified the attorney’s skill and the results accomplished as proper factors in fee determinations. The Supreme Court in *Day v. Woodworth*, 54 U.S. 363 (1851), recognized that federal courts exercise sound discretion in awarding costs and allowances incident to litigation. So too, *The Appollon*, 22 U.S. 362 (1824), had earlier confirmed that courts of equity have broad discretion to make allowances that serve the interests of justice. These cases demonstrate that enhancement based on the quality of performance and results is not a novel concept but a well-established equitable tool.

**II. The Discretion to Enhance Is Consistent with the Purposes of Federal Fee-Shifting Statutes**

When Congress enacted fee-shifting statutes such as 42 U.S.C. § 1988, it incorporated the common-law equitable tradition. In *Newman v. Piggie Park Enterprises, Inc.*, 390 U.S. 400, 402 (1968), the Supreme Court emphasized that a fee award under a civil rights statute should be reasonable and that a plaintiff who obtains an injunction should ordinarily recover an attorney’s fee. The reasonableness standard ensures that successful plaintiffs can obtain effective legal representation. The discretion to set a reasonable fee necessarily includes the power to enhance fees when the lodestar fails to compensate fully for exceptional performance and extraordinary results. *Fleischmann*, 386 U.S. at 717 (recognizing the equitable basis for fee awards).

The petitioners’ argument that enhancement is never permissible would repudiate this settled history. The pre-1970 cases uniformly hold that courts may consider the quality of representation and the results obtained in setting fees. Nothing in the language of § 1988 or any other fee-shifting statute prohibits a district court from accounting for superior performance and exceptional success in determining a reasonable fee.

**III. The District Court’s Detailed Findings Satisfy the Requirements for Enhancement**

The district court did not rely solely on the quality of performance and results; it made detailed findings based on its own substantial experience and familiarity with prevailing rates in Atlanta, combined with its observation of counsel’s stellar performance throughout the long and difficult case. The court specifically found that the representation far exceeded what could reasonably be expected for the standard hourly rates used to calculate the fee. Such findings are precisely the type of specific evidence that the equitable tradition requires. *Brown v. Gerdes*, 321 U.S. at 183 (emphasizing that fee determinations must rest on the court’s informed judgment). The trial judge’s personal observation of counsel’s performance is a proper factor in setting fees. *Guardian Trust Co.*, 28 F.2d 877; *In re L. H. T. Co.*, 123 F.2d at 224.

The circuit court correctly affirmed. The pre-1970 cases establish that enhancements are permissible in rare and exceptional cases. The district court’s enhancement was based on specific evidence and detailed findings, not on a quality-based rationale alone. The judgment should be affirmed.
