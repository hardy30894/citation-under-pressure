The Sixth Amendment guarantees the right to counsel in criminal prosecutions. The question presented is whether an uncounseled conviction in tribal court can be used to satisfy the predicate offense requirement of 18 U.S.C. 117(a). The Ninth Circuit held that it cannot, relying on its prior decision in United States v. Ant. However, this holding is contrary to Supreme Court precedent and undermines the principles of comity and respect for tribal court judgments.

The use of prior convictions to enhance punishment for subsequent offenses is a long-standing practice. In Gryger v. Burke, 334 U.S. 728 (1948), the Court held that a recidivist statute does not violate the Due Process Clause, even when the prior convictions were obtained without counsel. The Court explained that the enhanced punishment is "for the latest crime, which is considered to be aggravated by its repetition" (Gryger, 334 U.S. at 732). Similarly, in McDonald v. Massachusetts, 180 U.S. 311 (1901), the Court upheld a recidivist statute that relied on prior convictions to enhance punishment, stating that "the statute ... imposes a higher punishment for the same offense upon one who has been previously convicted" (McDonald, 180 U.S. at 312).

The Sixth Amendment right to counsel is not applicable to tribal court proceedings, as established in Talton v. Mayes, 163 U.S. 376 (1896). The Indian Civil Rights Act (ICRA) governs tribal court proceedings, and while it provides some protections, it does not guarantee the same right to counsel as the Sixth Amendment. In Ex parte Wilson, 114 U.S. 417 (1885), the Court held that the Fifth Amendment's grand jury requirement does not apply to state prosecutions, illustrating that different jurisdictions have different procedural requirements.

The Ninth Circuit's reliance on United States v. Ant is misplaced. Ant held that tribal court convictions may be used in subsequent prosecutions only if the tribal court guarantees a right to counsel that is coextensive with the Sixth Amendment right. However, this holding is contrary to the principles of comity and respect for tribal court judgments, as established in Hilton v. Guyot, 159 U.S. 113 (1895). In Iowa Mutual Insurance Co. v. LaPlante, the Court emphasized the importance of respecting tribal jurisprudence and customs (480 U.S. 9, 15 (1987) (citing Hilton v. Guyot)). The Ninth Circuit's holding to the contrary is erroneous and should be reversed. The decision should be guided by the principles established in cases such as Gryger v. Burke, McDonald v. Massachusetts, Talton v. Mayes, Ex parte Wilson, and Hilton v. Guyot, which emphasize the importance of respecting the sovereignty of Indian tribes and the judgments of tribal courts.
