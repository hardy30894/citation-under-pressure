**ARGUMENT**

**I. The District Court Abused Its Discretion by Certifying a Class Without Requiring Admissible Evidence That Damages Could Be Measured and Quantified on a Class-Wide Basis.**

A district court must conduct a “rigorous analysis” before certifying a class under Rule 23(b)(3). *Wal-Mart Stores, Inc. v. Dukes*, 564 U.S. 338, 350-51 (2011). This analysis requires the plaintiff to “affirmatively demonstrate” that common questions predominate over individual ones. *Id.* at 350. The predominance inquiry “entail[s] some overlap with the merits of the plaintiff’s underlying claim.” *Id.* at 351. Accordingly, a district court may not “lightly assume” that common issues predominate; it must “assess the evidence” and “resolve factual disputes” to ensure that certification is appropriate. *Comcast Corp. v. Behrend*, 569 U.S. 27, 34 (2013).

Where damages cannot be measured and quantified on a class-wide basis through common evidence, predominance fails. *Id.* at 35. In *Comcast*, this Court reversed the Third Circuit’s affirmance of class certification because the plaintiffs’ damages model “did not measure damages attributable solely to” the plaintiffs’ accepted theory of antitrust impact. *Id.* at 36. The expert’s model calculated damages based on a comparison of Comcast’s prices to prices in other markets, but that model “assumed the validity of [multiple] theories of antitrust impact,” not just the lone theory the district court accepted. *Id.* at 34, 36-37. Because the model could not isolate damages from the accepted theory, it could not “justify class certification” because it did not “demonstrate that the element of damages is capable of measurement on a classwide basis.” *Id.* at 34. “Such a model cannot possibly establish that damages are susceptible of measurement across the entire class.” *Id.* at 35.

Here, the district court certified the class based on the same fatally flawed model this Court rejected in *Comcast*. The plaintiffs’ expert, Dr. James McClave, did not design a damages model that measured damages attributable solely to the overbuilder-deterrence theory. Instead, as this Court recognized, Dr. McClave’s model purported to calculate “anticompetitive overcharges” but “did not isolate” the effect of the overbuilder theory from other alleged anticompetitive conduct. *Comcast*, 569 U.S. at 36. The model assumed that “any price increase” above the benchmark was attributable to “Comcast’s anticompetitive conduct” generally, rather than to the specific overbuilder theory. *Id.* at 37. As a result, the model could not “show that the damages stemmed from the particular antitrust theory on which the class was certified.” *Id.*

The district court’s error was compounded by its failure to require that Dr. McClave’s model be “consistent with [the] class-wide theory of impact.” *Comcast*, 569 U.S. at 35. The court accepted the model even though it had “no intention of considering causation” at the certification stage. *Behrend v. Comcast Corp.*, 264 F.R.D. 150, 180 (E.D. Pa. 2010). That approach is irreconcilable with *Comcast*, which held that “a model purporting to serve as evidence of damages in a class action must measure only those damages attributable to that theory.” 569 U.S. at 35.

The Third Circuit’s affirmance incorrectly reasoned that the district court “properly satisfied” Rule 23’s predominance requirement by finding that the model provided “a common methodology to quantify damages on a class-wide basis.” Pet. App. 4a. But a methodology that cannot link damages to the plaintiffs’ accepted theory of liability is not a valid class-wide methodology. *Comcast*, 569 U.S. at 35. The Third Circuit’s reliance on the district court’s factual findings under a clearly erroneous standard does not salvage the certification decision. The district court applied an erroneous legal standard by failing to require that the damages model actually “measure damages” attributable to the accepted theory. *Id.* at 36. That legal error is reviewed *de novo* and requires reversal. *In re Hydrogen Peroxide Antitrust Litig.*, 552 F.3d 305, 312 (3d Cir. 2008).

Moreover, the district court’s factual finding that Dr. McClave’s model was a “common methodology” was itself clearly erroneous. As this Court recognized, the model did not “confine itself to the overbuilder theory” and instead incorporated “other alleged anticompetitive conduct.” *Comcast*, 569 U.S. at 37. A model that includes damages from multiple theories cannot satisfy Rule 23(b)(3)’s predominance requirement because it would require individualized inquiries to determine which damages are attributable to the accepted theory. *Id.* at 34. The Third Circuit’s affirmance thus rests on a finding “completely devoid of minimum evidentiary support” and “bears no rational relationship to the supportive evidentiary data.” *Krasnov v. Dinan*, 465 F.2d 1298, 1302 (3d Cir. 1972).

**II. The District Court’s Failure to Require a Proper Damages Model Undermines the Predominance Requirement.**

The predominance requirement of Rule 23(b)(3) is designed to “ensure that a class action would be economical and efficient.” *Amgen Inc. v. Connecticut Ret. Plans & Tr. Funds*, 568 U.S. 455, 470 n.6 (2013). When damages cannot be measured on a class-wide basis, individual inquiries will predominate, making class treatment inappropriate. *Comcast*, 569 U.S. at 34. The district court’s acceptance of a model that could not link damages to the sole theory of liability guaranteed that individualized fact-finding would be required at trial. That alone defeats predominance.

The Third Circuit’s contrary conclusion improperly diluted the predominance inquiry. By deferring to the district court’s factual findings without scrutinizing the legal adequacy of the damages model, the Third Circuit allowed certification based on evidence that could not, as a matter of law, satisfy Rule 23(b)(3). This Court should reverse and remand with instructions to decertify the class.
