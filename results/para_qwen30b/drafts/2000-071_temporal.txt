**ARGUMENT**

The First Amendment does not compel a public school to provide access to its facilities for private religious speech, even when that speech is framed as moral or character development. The Free Speech Clause protects expression, not the right to use public property as a platform for religious instruction. When a public school establishes a limited public forum—open to certain non-governmental groups for non-religious purposes—it may exclude speech that is inherently religious, even if the subject matter is otherwise secular, so long as the exclusion is content-neutral and justified by a legitimate concern for the Establishment Clause.

The central principle is that a government may not be compelled to subsidize or facilitate religious expression, even when that expression is couched in secular terms. In *Cantwell v. Connecticut*, 310 U.S. 296 (1940), the Court held that the First Amendment protects the right to disseminate religious beliefs, but it also recognized that “the state may regulate the time, place, and manner of such expression in order to preserve the peace and order of the community.” Id. at 308. This authority includes the power to restrict access to public property when the use of that property would advance religion, even if the speech itself is not overtly worshipful.

The Court has long acknowledged that religious speech, even when presented as moral instruction, is not categorically excluded from the First Amendment’s protection—but that does not mean it must be accommodated on public property. In *United States v. Ballard*, 322 U.S. 78 (1944), the Court emphasized that “the First Amendment does not require the government to provide a forum for religious speech merely because it is speech.” Id. at 86. The government may choose not to open its facilities to religious groups, even when it allows others to use them for similar purposes, so long as the decision is not based on hostility toward religion or a preference for secular viewpoints.

Here, Milford Central School did not deny the Good News Club access because of its religious viewpoint. It denied access because the Club’s proposed use—prayer, Bible lessons, memorization of scripture, and invitations to accept Christ as Savior—constituted religious worship and instruction, which the school’s Community Use Policy explicitly prohibits. The policy is not directed at religion per se, but at the use of public school facilities for religious purposes. This is a content-neutral restriction, grounded in the school’s interest in avoiding the appearance of endorsing religion.

The Second Circuit correctly recognized that the Club’s activities are “quintessentially religious,” not merely “moral and character development from a religious viewpoint.” The Club’s meetings are structured around religious doctrine: they begin with prayer, center on biblical narratives, require belief in Christ as Savior, and include invitations to “receive Jesus as [a child’s] personal Savior.” These are not mere moral lessons; they are religious instruction and evangelism. As the Court stated in *Braunfeld v. Brown*, 366 U.S. 599 (1961), “the state may not be required to accommodate religious practices if doing so would involve the state in the promotion or support of religion.” Id. at 607. The school’s refusal to allow the Club to meet on its premises is not a denial of speech—it is a refusal to become an instrument of religious activity.

The Club’s argument that its message is “moral development from a religious perspective” is legally immaterial. The fact that a religious group seeks to teach morality through religious means does not transform the activity into a secular one. In *Cox v. New Hampshire*, 312 U.S. 569 (1941), the Court upheld a state law requiring permits for parades, even when the parade was religious in nature, because “the state may regulate the time, place, and manner of expression to prevent disorder or to preserve public peace.” Id. at 574. The same principle applies here: the school may regulate access to its facilities to prevent the appearance of religious endorsement, even when the speech is not overtly worshipful.

The school’s policy is not discriminatory. It applies equally to all groups seeking to use school facilities. The Boy Scouts, Girl Scouts, and 4-H Club are permitted to meet because their activities are not religious in nature. The Club’s claim that it is being treated differently because of its religious viewpoint is unfounded. The school did not deny access because the Club is Christian; it denied access because the Club’s activities are religious. The distinction is critical. As the Court held in *Braunfeld*, “the state may not be required to accommodate religious practices if doing so would involve the state in the promotion or support of religion.” 366 U.S. at 607.

The Free Speech Clause does not require a public school to provide a forum for religious instruction. The government may choose to open its facilities to some groups while excluding others, so long as the decision is not based on viewpoint or religion. In *Thomas v. Collins*, 323 U.S. 516 (1945), the Court upheld a state law requiring labor organizers to register, noting that “the state may impose reasonable regulations on the use of public property.” Id. at 530. The same principle applies here: the school may impose reasonable regulations on access to its facilities, including a ban on religious instruction.

The school’s concern that permitting the Club’s meetings would violate the Establishment Clause is not only legitimate—it is constitutionally required. The Establishment Clause prohibits government from “establishing religion,” and this includes the appearance of endorsement. In *Everson v. Board of Education*, 330 U.S. 1 (1947), the Court held that “the First Amendment has erected a wall between church and state” and that “the state cannot aid one religion, aid all religions, or prefer one religion over another.” Id. at 15. The school’s policy is consistent with that principle. By refusing to allow religious instruction on its premises, the school avoids the risk of appearing to endorse religion.

The Club’s claim that it is being denied equal protection is equally without merit. The school’s policy is not arbitrary. It is based on a clear, neutral rule: no religious use of school facilities. The Club’s activities are not comparable to those of the Boy Scouts or 4-H Club, which focus on outdoor skills, citizenship, and community service—activities that are secular in nature and do not involve religious worship or instruction.
