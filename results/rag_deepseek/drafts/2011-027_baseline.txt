**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Equal Footing Doctrine Requires That Navigability for Title Be Determined as of the Moment of Statehood.**

The equal footing doctrine provides that States admitted to the Union after 1789 possess the same sovereign rights and title to lands under navigable waters as the original Thirteen Colonies. *Utah Division of State Lands v. United States*, 482 U.S. 193, 195-96 (1987). Under this doctrine, title to the beds of navigable waters passes to a State upon its admission to the Union. *Id.* at 196. The critical inquiry is whether a river was navigable at the time of statehood. *United States v. Utah*, 283 U.S. 801, 804 (1931). This Court has consistently held that the navigability determination must be made as of the moment of statehood, not at some later date. *Id.*; *see also Idaho v. United States*, 533 U.S. 262, 272 (2001) (looking to events at the time of statehood to determine title).

The Montana Supreme Court correctly applied this test. It examined historical evidence of the Missouri, Madison, and Clark Fork Rivers' navigability in 1889, when Montana entered the Union. The court considered contemporaneous records, including steamboat traffic, log drives, and other commercial uses, to conclude that these rivers were navigable at statehood. This fact-intensive inquiry is precisely what the equal footing doctrine requires.

**II. The Navigability for Title Test Is a Federal Question Governed by Federal Law.**

The determination of navigability for title purposes is a federal question. *United States v. Appalachian Electric Power Co.*, 311 U.S. 377, 404 (1941). Federal law defines navigability as the capacity for use as a highway for commerce, either in the natural condition of the waterway or as susceptible of reasonable improvement. *Id.* at 407-08. The Montana Supreme Court applied this federal standard, considering evidence of actual commercial use and the rivers' susceptibility to improvement at statehood.

PPL's argument that the trial court improperly relied on historical evidence is unavailing. This Court has long recognized that historical records—including newspaper accounts, government surveys, and other contemporaneous documents—are admissible to prove navigability at statehood. *United States v. Utah*, 283 U.S. at 805-06. The Montana Supreme Court properly admitted such evidence under established evidentiary rules, including the ancient documents and public records exceptions. The court's thorough review of the historical record demonstrates that the rivers were used for commercial navigation in 1889.

**III. The Federal Power Act Does Not Preempt State Ownership or Compensation Claims.**

The Federal Power Act (FPA) establishes a "dual system" of control between the states and the federal government. *First Iowa Hydro-Electric Cooperative v. Federal Power Commission*, 328 U.S. 152, 167-68 (1946). The FPA preserves state law with respect to "proprietary rights" in riverbeds and water rights. *Id.* at 175-76. Section 9(b) of the FPA requires license applicants to demonstrate compliance with state law regarding "beds and banks and to the appropriation, diversion, and use of water for power purposes." 16 U.S.C. § 802(b). This provision explicitly recognizes state authority over property interests in riverbeds.

Moreover, the FPA does not preempt state compensation claims. *Federal Power Commission v. Union Electric Co.*, 381 U.S. 90, 98-99 (1965). The Act contemplates that licensees must compensate landowners for the use of their property. *Jordan v. Randolph Mills, Inc.*, 716 F.2d 1053, 1055 (4th Cir. 1983). Montana's Hydroelectric Resources Act (HRA) simply requires PPL to pay fair market value for its use of state-owned riverbeds—a requirement entirely consistent with the FPA's framework.

**IV. The Federal Navigational Servitude Does Not Bar State Compensation.**

The federal navigational servitude is a power of Congress to ensure that navigable waters remain open to commerce. *United States v. Chicago, Milwaukee, St. Paul & Pacific Railroad Co.*, 312 U.S. 592, 596-97 (1941). However, the exercise of this servitude requires clear authorization from Congress. *Federal Power Commission v. Niagara Mohawk Power Corp.*, 347 U.S. 239, 249-51 (1954). The FPA does not provide such authorization. To the contrary, the FPA recognizes state property rights and permits compensation for their use. *First Iowa*, 328 U.S. at 175-76.

PPL's reliance on the federal navigational servitude is misplaced. The servitude does not extinguish state ownership of riverbeds; it merely allows Congress to regulate navigation without compensating the State. *United States v. West Virginia*, 295 U.S. 463, 470-71 (1935). Here, Montana is not seeking to obstruct navigation. It is simply seeking compensation for PPL's commercial use of state property—a right that the FPA expressly preserves.

**V. The Montana Supreme Court's Factual Findings Are Entitled to Deference.**

The navigability determination is a factual question. *United States v. Utah*, 283 U.S. at 805. The Montana Supreme Court conducted a thorough review of the historical record, including evidence of steamboat traffic, log drives, and other commercial uses on the Missouri, Madison, and Clark Fork Rivers at statehood. The court's findings are supported by substantial evidence and should not be disturbed.

**CONCLUSION**

The judgment of the Montana Supreme Court should be affirmed. The equal footing doctrine requires that navigability for title be determined as of the time of statehood, and the court correctly applied this test. The FPA does not preempt state ownership or compensation claims, and the federal navigational servitude does not bar Montana from seeking fair compensation for PPL's use of state-owned riverbeds.
