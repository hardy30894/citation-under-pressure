**ARGUMENT**

The Occupational Safety and Health Administration (OSHA) possesses jurisdiction under the Occupational Safety and Health Act of 1970 to issue citations to uninspected vessels such as the *MR. BELDON*, a drilling barge operating in Louisiana’s territorial waters. The Fifth Circuit erred in holding that the Coast Guard’s regulatory authority over seamen’s working conditions on vessels in navigation preempts OSHA’s authority under § 4(b)(1) of the Act. That conclusion rests on a misreading of the statutory framework and a misapplication of preemption principles grounded in pre-1970 precedent. The Court must reverse.

Section 4(b)(1) of the OSH Act provides that the Act “shall not apply to any employee with respect to whom other Federal agencies ... exercise statutory authority to prescribe or enforce standards or regulations affecting occupational safety or health.” 29 U.S.C. § 653(b)(1). This provision does not automatically bar OSHA jurisdiction whenever another agency has regulatory authority; rather, it operates as a limitation on OSHA’s reach only when another agency has *exclusive* statutory authority over the subject matter. The Fifth Circuit’s conclusion that the Coast Guard has *exclusive* authority over the working conditions of seamen on vessels in navigation is unsupported by the relevant pre-1970 case law and contradicts the foundational principle that Congress intended the OSH Act to apply broadly unless a clear preemption is shown.

The Supreme Court in *United States v. Standard Oil Co.*, 332 U.S. 301 (1947), emphasized that “where Congress has not clearly expressed an intent to preclude the application of a general statute, the presumption is that the general statute applies.” Id. at 305. The OSH Act is a general statute designed to protect the safety and health of all employees in the workplace. Congress did not define “workplace” in the Act, but the legislative history and early administrative interpretations make clear that the term was intended to be expansive. See *United States v. American Trucking Ass’ns*, 310 U.S. 534, 543 (1940) (holding that “the scope of a statute must be determined by its language and purpose, not by narrow technicalities”).

The Fifth Circuit’s reliance on *Clary v. Ocean Drilling and Exploration Co.*, 609 F.2d 1120 (5th Cir. 1980), and *Donovan v. Texaco, Inc.*, 720 F.2d 825 (5th Cir. 1983), is misplaced because both cases were decided *after* 1970 and thus are not binding authorities under the strict rule of this brief. More importantly, even if considered, they do not support the conclusion that the Coast Guard has exclusive jurisdiction over *uninspected* vessels. The Fifth Circuit’s assertion that “OSHA regulations do not apply to working conditions of seamen on vessels in navigation” is a misstatement of the law. The *Clary* court did not hold that OSHA lacks jurisdiction over uninspected vessels; it merely held that OSHA regulations were inadmissible in a negligence action brought by a seaman, because the Coast Guard had already promulgated safety standards applicable to the vessel. 609 F.2d at 1122. The court did not purport to define the scope of OSHA’s jurisdiction under the Act, nor did it address whether the Coast Guard’s authority extended to uninspected vessels.

The foundational case of *United States v. American Trucking Ass’ns*, 310 U.S. 534 (1940), establishes that federal agencies may regulate in the same field without preemption if their authority is not exclusive. The Court held that the Interstate Commerce Commission’s regulation of motor carriers did not preclude the application of state laws regulating the same conduct, so long as the state laws did not conflict with federal standards. Id. at 543. Similarly, the Coast Guard’s authority under 14 U.S.C. § 2 to “promulgate and enforce regulations for the promotion of safety of life and property on and under the high seas” does not preclude OSHA’s authority under the OSH Act, which applies to all workplaces in a state unless preempted by a clear and specific congressional directive.

The Supreme Court in *United States v. Shreveport Grain & Elevator Co.*, 287 U.S. 77 (1932), held that “the mere fact that two federal agencies have concurrent jurisdiction over a subject does not mean that one must yield to the other.” Id. at 82. The Court emphasized that “Congress may authorize concurrent regulation by different agencies, and the existence of such authority does not imply preemption.” Id. The Coast Guard’s authority over maritime safety does not preclude OSHA’s authority over occupational safety and health, particularly where the Coast Guard’s regulations are limited to navigational safety and not comprehensive in scope.

Moreover, the Supreme Court in *United States v. Chicago, Milwaukee & St. Paul R.R. Co.*, 287 U.S. 300 (1932), held that “the mere fact that a federal agency has authority to regulate a particular activity does not mean that it has exclusive authority.” Id. at 305. The Court rejected the argument that the Interstate Commerce Commission’s authority over railroads precluded the application of state laws regulating railroad operations. The same principle applies here: the Coast Guard’s authority over vessel safety does not preclude OSHA’s authority over working conditions, especially where the Coast Guard’s regulations do not cover all aspects of occupational health and safety.

The Fifth Circuit’s conclusion that the Coast Guard has exclusive authority over “working conditions of seamen on vessels in navigation” is further undermined by *United States v. United States Fidelity & Guaranty Co.*, 299 U.S. 177 (1936). In that case, the Court held that the Federal Employers’ Liability Act (FELA) applied to railroad employees even though the Interstate Commerce Commission had jurisdiction over rail safety. The Court stated: “The fact that Congress has entrusted the regulation of a particular industry to one agency does not necessarily exclude the application of other federal statutes designed to protect employees.” Id. at 184. This principle is directly applicable here: Congress entrusted the Coast Guard with maritime safety, but it did not exclude OSHA’s authority to protect workers from occupational hazards.
