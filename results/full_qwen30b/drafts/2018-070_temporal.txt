**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether the Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act (OCSLA) permits the application of state law only when there is a gap in federal law, or whenever state law pertains to the subject matter and is not preempted by inconsistent federal law—requires this Court to interpret the OCSLA’s choice-of-law provision in light of its original text, legislative history, and the settled principles of federal preemption that governed the law at the time of its enactment. The Ninth Circuit erred in holding that California’s wage and hour laws may be applied on the outer Continental Shelf as “surrogate federal law” simply because they are “applicable and not inconsistent” with federal law. That interpretation disregards the foundational principle that federal law preempts state law when it occupies the field, and it misreads the OCSLA’s purpose and structure. The correct rule, grounded in pre-1970 precedent, is that state law applies on the OCS only when federal law is silent or inadequate—i.e., when a gap exists—and not as a matter of course whenever state law is not inconsistent with federal law.

The OCSLA’s choice-of-law provision, 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(2)(A), provides that “the civil and criminal laws of each adjacent State, now in effect or hereafter adopted, amended, or repealed are declared to be the law of the United States for that portion of the subsoil and seabed of the outer Continental Shelf... to the extent that they are applicable and not inconsistent with this subchapter or with other Federal laws and regulations of the Secretary now in effect or hereafter adopted.” This language, enacted in 1953, was not intended to create a general rule of concurrent state-federal jurisdiction. Rather, Congress designed the OCSLA to fill a jurisdictional vacuum left by the absence of a comprehensive federal statutory scheme for the outer Continental Shelf. As the legislative history makes clear, Congress rejected the idea of extending state law to the OCS in a blanket fashion, fearing that such an extension might constitute an unconstitutional delegation of legislative power. See *S. Rep. No. 83-411, at 33 (1953)*. To avoid that constitutional concern, Congress limited the incorporation of state law to laws that were “in effect” at the time of the Act’s passage. This narrow construction was essential to preserve the constitutional integrity of the federal legislative power.

The Supreme Court’s decision in *United States v. Standard Oil Co.*, 332 U.S. 301 (1947), confirms that federal preemption is not a mere matter of inconsistency but a doctrine rooted in the supremacy of federal authority over areas where Congress has acted. In *Standard Oil*, the Court held that federal law preempted state regulation of oil pipelines where Congress had enacted a comprehensive regulatory scheme. The Court emphasized that “where Congress has acted in a field, the States may not impose regulations that conflict with federal policy or that frustrate the objectives of federal law.” Id. at 314. The same principle applies here. The Fair Labor Standards Act (FLSA), enacted in 1938, is a comprehensive statutory scheme that governs minimum wage, overtime, and meal period requirements for employees engaged in interstate commerce. See *29 U.S.C. § 201 et seq.* The FLSA contains a savings clause—29 U.S.C. § 218—that expressly permits states to impose more protective wage and hour standards. But that clause does not create a right to apply state law in the absence of a gap; it merely preserves state law where it is more protective. It does not override the principle that federal law governs when it is complete and self-sufficient.

The Ninth Circuit’s interpretation of the OCSLA—under which state law applies whenever it is “not inconsistent” with federal law—would render the FLSA’s comprehensive structure meaningless. If state law could be applied as a matter of course, even when federal law is fully operative, then the FLSA would be reduced to a floor, not a ceiling. That result is inconsistent with the settled doctrine of preemption established in *Hill v. Wallace*, 259 U.S. 44, 50 (1922), where the Court held that “where the federal law is complete in itself, and leaves no room for state legislation, the state law is excluded.” The FLSA, with its detailed provisions, enforcement mechanisms, and regulatory framework, is complete in itself. The Department of Labor’s regulations, which interpret and implement the FLSA, are binding and authoritative. See *United States v. American Trucking Ass’ns, Inc.*, 310 U.S. 534, 543 (1940). These regulations, promulgated under the FLSA’s authority, define when employees are entitled to meal periods, how hours are counted, and what constitutes compensable work. They are not mere guidelines; they are the law of the land.

The Ninth Circuit’s reliance on *Continental Oil Co. v. London S.S. Owners’ Mut. Ins. Ass’n*, 417 F.2d 1030 (5th Cir. 1969), is misplaced. That case, decided after the OCSLA’s 1975 amendment, is not binding precedent and was decided in a context where the OCSLA’s choice-of-law provision had been expanded to include future state laws. More importantly, *Continental Oil* itself recognized that state law applies only when federal law is “inadequate” or “inapplicable.” Id. at 1036. The court held that “the absence of federal law is not a prerequisite to the application of state law,” but it did not hold that state law applies whenever it is not inconsistent. Rather, the court emphasized that state law may be applied only when federal law “leaves a gap.” Id. This is consistent with the principle that federal law preempts state law when it is complete. The Ninth Circuit’s reading of *Continental Oil* as permitting state law to apply in the absence of inconsistency, without regard to whether federal law fills the field, contradicts the very holding of that case.
