## ARGUMENT

The district court erred in denying Perkins's petition for a writ of habeas corpus by requiring him to demonstrate reasonable diligence in addition to making a credible claim of actual innocence. This Court has previously held that a credible claim of actual innocence is sufficient to warrant equitable tolling of AEDPA's statute of limitations. See Souter v. Jones, 395 F.3d 577, 602 (6th Cir. 2005) (holding that "where an otherwise untimely habeas petitioner can demonstrate that it is more likely than not that no reasonable juror would have found him guilty beyond a reasonable doubt in light of new evidence of innocence, the petitioner is entitled to have his otherwise barred habeas petition reach the merits"). The Supreme Court's decision in Pace v. DiGuglielmo, 544 U.S. 408 (2005), does not impose an additional burden of reasonable diligence on petitioners seeking equitable tolling based on actual innocence.

In Pace, the Supreme Court held that a petitioner seeking equitable tolling must demonstrate that he has been pursuing his rights diligently. 544 U.S. at 418. However, the Court did not address the specific issue of whether a petitioner claiming actual innocence must also demonstrate reasonable diligence. See id. at 419 (noting that the petitioner did not make a claim of actual innocence). The Supreme Court has since clarified that the Pace diligence requirement is not applicable to all equitable tolling claims. See Holland v. Florida, 130 S. Ct. 2549, 2562 (2010) (holding that equitable tolling is available to a petitioner who has been pursuing his rights diligently, but noting that the Court was not addressing the issue of actual innocence).

This Court's decision in Souter v. Jones remains controlling on the issue of whether a credible claim of actual innocence is sufficient to warrant equitable tolling. See 395 F.3d at 602. The Supreme Court's decision in Schlup v. Delo, 513 U.S. 298 (1995), which established the standard for evaluating claims of actual innocence, does not require a showing of diligence. See id. at 327-28 (holding that a petitioner must demonstrate that it is more likely than not that no reasonable juror would have found him guilty beyond a reasonable doubt in light of new evidence). Similarly, the Court's decision in House v. Bell, 547 U.S. 518 (2006), reaffirmed the Schlup standard and did not impose an additional diligence requirement. See id. at 538.

Other circuits have also held that a credible claim of actual innocence is sufficient to warrant equitable tolling, without requiring a showing of diligence. See, e.g., Lee v. Lampert, 653 F.3d 929, 934-35 (9th Cir. 2011) (en banc) (holding that a credible claim of actual innocence is sufficient to warrant equitable tolling); Lopez v. Trani, 628 F.3d 1228, 1230-31 (10th Cir. 2010) (same). The Seventh Circuit has also recognized that a credible claim of actual innocence can warrant equitable tolling, although it has not directly addressed the issue of diligence. See Escamilla v. Jungwirth, 426 F.3d 868, 871-72 (7th Cir. 2005).

In McQuiggin v. Perkins, the Supreme Court recently held that "actual innocence, if proved, serves as a gateway through which a petitioner may pass" to overcome AEDPA's statute of limitations. 133 S. Ct. 1924, 1928 (2013). Although the Court noted that "a federal habeas court, faced with an actual-innocence gateway claim, should count unjustified delay on the timeliness question," id. at 1935, it did not impose a separate diligence requirement. Instead, the Court emphasized that "the miscarriage of justice exception... survived AEDPA's passage," id. at 1933, and that "untimeliness, although not an unyielding ground for dismissal of a petition... remains an affirmative defense," id. at 1934 (citing Day v. McDonough, 547 U.S. 198, 208 (2006)).

Perkins has made a credible claim of actual innocence, supported by new evidence that was not available at trial. The district court erred in requiring him to demonstrate reasonable diligence in addition to making this claim. This Court should reverse the district court's judgment and remand the case for consideration of Perkins's habeas petition on the merits.
