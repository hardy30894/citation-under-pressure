**ARGUMENT**

**I. CLEMENCY PROCEEDINGS CREATE A PROTECTED LIBERTY INTEREST UNDER THE DUE PROCESS CLAUSE**

The Sixth Circuit erred in concluding that petitioner Woodard lacks a constitutionally protected liberty interest in clemency proceedings. This Court has long recognized that clemency is an integral part of the criminal justice system, not a mere act of grace. *See Ohio Adult Parole Auth. v. Woodard*, 523 U.S. 272, 281 (1998) (plurality opinion) ("Clemency is deeply rooted in our constitutional structure."). The Due Process Clause protects against arbitrary deprivations of liberty, and clemency decisions directly affect a prisoner’s fundamental right to freedom. *See Dumschat*, 452 U.S. at 464 (Stevens, J., dissenting) ("The Due Process Clause applies to each step in the state’s deprivation of liberty, including clemency.").

The Sixth Circuit’s reliance on *Connecticut Bd. of Pardons v. Dumschat*, 452 U.S. 458 (1981), is misplaced. *Dumschat* addressed whether a state-created liberty interest existed in clemency, not whether clemency itself implicates due process protections. *Id.* at 465. Here, the clemency process is not merely a statutory creation—it is a constitutional safeguard against excessive punishment. *See Ex parte Grossman*, 277 U.S. 498, 506 (1928) ("The power of clemency is part of the judicial function in the broadest sense.").

**II. THE FIFTH AMENDMENT PROHIBITS COMPELLING PARTICIPATION IN CLEMENCY INTERVIEWS**

The Sixth Circuit’s suggestion that the Fifth Amendment is not implicated because the clemency interview is voluntary is legally unsound. The Fifth Amendment protects against any governmental action that compels self-incrimination, even indirectly. *See Lefkowitz v. Cunningham*, 431 U.S. 801, 806 (1977) ("The Fifth Amendment prohibits the government from forcing a person to choose between incriminating himself and forfeiting a constitutional right.").

Ohio’s clemency process presents a classic "Hobson’s choice": Petitioner must either waive his Fifth Amendment rights by participating in an interview or risk appearing uncooperative in the eyes of the parole board. *See Baxter v. Palmigiano*, 425 U.S. 308, 316 (1976) ("A state may not impose penalties for asserting constitutional rights."). The Sixth Circuit’s reliance on *Baxter* is misplaced because *Baxter* involved prison disciplinary proceedings, not clemency. *Id.* at 324.

The Court should hold that conditioning clemency on participation in an interview violates the Fifth Amendment. *See Lefkowitz*, 431 U.S. at 808 ("The Fifth Amendment forbids the imposition of penalties for exercising constitutional rights.").

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should reverse the Sixth Circuit’s decision and hold that clemency proceedings implicate due process protections and that Ohio’s clemency interview process violates the Fifth Amendment.
