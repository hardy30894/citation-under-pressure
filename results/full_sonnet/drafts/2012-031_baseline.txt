# ARGUMENT

## I. RULE 23(b)(3) REQUIRES ADMISSIBLE EVIDENCE ESTABLISHING A CLASSWIDE METHODOLOGY FOR MEASURING DAMAGES, AND THE THIRD CIRCUIT FAILED TO REQUIRE IT

Rule 23(b)(3) permits certification only where "questions of law or fact common to class members predominate over any questions affecting only individual members." This Court has made clear that certification is not a mere formality but demands rigorous judicial scrutiny of the evidence. In *Wal-Mart Stores, Inc. v. Dukes*, 564 U.S. 338 (2011), this Court held that "[a] party seeking class certification must affirmatively demonstrate his compliance with the Rule," and that certification is proper only after "rigorous analysis" confirms that Rule 23's prerequisites are satisfied. *Id.* at 350-51. Critically, this Court recognized that "[f]requently that 'rigorous analysis' will entail some overlap with the merits of the plaintiff's underlying claim," and that district courts must resolve factual disputes—even merits-related ones—when necessary to determine whether certification requirements are met. *Id.* at 351.

The Third Circuit paid lip service to this standard while eviscerating it in practice. The court below acknowledged that *Hydrogen Peroxide* requires courts to "determine that the requirements of Rule 23 have been met" through "a thorough examination of the factual and legal allegations," which "may include a preliminary inquiry into the merits." Pet. App. 12a (citing *In re Hydrogen Peroxide Antitrust Litig.*, 552 F.3d 305, 317 (3d Cir. 2008)). Yet when confronted with Comcast's central argument—that Plaintiffs' damages model, created by Dr. James McClave, failed to isolate damages attributable to the sole surviving theory of antitrust impact (overbuilder deterrence) from three other theories the district court had rejected—the Third Circuit declined to test the model's admissibility or reliability under *Daubert v. Merrell Dow Pharmaceuticals, Inc.*, 509 U.S. 579 (1993).

This omission is fatal. *Daubert* requires that expert testimony be both relevant and reliable before a court may rely upon it. *Id.* at 589. An expert damages model that calculates damages using a methodology untethered to the certified theory of liability is not "relevant" in any meaningful sense; it does not "fit" the case. *Id.* at 591 (requiring "a valid scientific connection to the pertinent inquiry as a precondition to admissibility"). Here, Dr. McClave's regression model calculated damages by comparing actual cable prices to a hypothetical benchmark reflecting the presence of overbuilder competition, *satellite* competition, *municipal* competition, and *traditional cable* competition combined—even though only the overbuilder theory survived class certification. Pet. App. 45a-46a. The model thus measured harm from four theories of anticompetitive conduct using data that could not disaggregate which portion of the calculated overcharge, if any, was attributable to the one theory the class was permitted to pursue.

The Third Circuit brushed aside this defect, reasoning that "an antitrust plaintiff need not tie each theory of antitrust impact into an exact calculation of damages" at the class-certification stage. Pet. App. 46a. That holding cannot be reconciled with *Dukes*, which forecloses certification premised on speculative or unverified common proof. This Court in *Dukes* rejected statistical evidence of classwide discrimination because it could not reliably demonstrate that "all the employees' Title VII claims can productively be litigated at once." 564 U.S. at 359-60. The same infirmity dooms the McClave model: if damages cannot be reliably attributed to the single theory of classwide impact, then individual damages calculations—and individualized proof of what portion of any overcharge stemmed from lack of overbuilder competition specifically—will predominate over common questions, defeating Rule 23(b)(3)'s core requirement.

## II. THE THIRD CIRCUIT'S REFUSAL TO SCRUTINIZE THE DAMAGES METHODOLOGY CONFLICTS WITH THIS COURT'S TEACHING THAT PREDOMINANCE REQUIRES COMMON PROOF OF DAMAGES, NOT MERELY COMMON PROOF OF LIABILITY

Predominance under Rule 23(b)(3) does not evaporate simply because common questions exist as to liability. This Court has long recognized that class treatment is appropriate only where common issues predominate over the *entire* claim, including damages. In antitrust cases particularly, where damages theories are complex and expert-driven, unadmitted or unreliable methodologies cannot substitute for the rigorous analysis *Dukes* demands.

The Third Circuit's own precedent in *Hydrogen Peroxide* recognized that "the district court's obligation to consider all relevant evidence and arguments extends to expert testimony, whether offered by a party seeking class certification or by a party opposing it." 552 F.3d at 323. Yet the panel majority below did not apply this obligation to test whether McClave's model was reliable evidence that damages *could* be measured on a classwide basis attributable to the narrowed theory of liability. Instead, the court merely accepted the district court's determination that "the models and analyses of Plaintiffs' damages expert... were common evidence available to measure and quantify damages on a classwide basis," Pet. App. 8a, without independently assessing whether that methodology was admissible or reliable in light of the mismatch between the model's inputs and the certified theory.

This deferential, rubber-stamp approach cannot be squared with the "clearly erroneous" standard as this Court articulated it in *Anderson v. City of Bessemer City*, 470 U.S. 564 (1985). While *Anderson* counsels appellate deference to plausible factual findings, it does not permit courts of appeals to abandon scrutiny of whether the evidentiary foundation for those findings—here, an expert damages model—is legally sound. A finding "is completely devoid of minimum evidentiary support displaying some hue of credibility," *Krasnov v. Dinan*, 465 F.2d 1298, 1302 (3d Cir. 1972), when it rests on a methodology inherently incapable of isolating classwide damages traceable to the certified theory.

## III. THE JUDGMENT BELOW SHOULD BE REVERSED

Because the Third Circuit permitted certification without requiring admissible, reliable evidence connecting Plaintiffs' damages model to the sole certified theory of antitrust impact, its decision cannot stand consistent with *Dukes* and *Daubert*. This Court should reverse and hold that Rule 23(b)(3) predominance requires courts to determine, through rigorous analysis, that plaintiffs possess a damages methodology reliably tied to their theory of classwide liability—not merely a facially common methodology divorced from that theory's boundaries.
