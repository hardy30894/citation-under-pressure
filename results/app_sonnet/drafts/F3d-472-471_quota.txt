ARGUMENT

I. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN GRANTING SUMMARY JUDGMENT BECAUSE BURNETT RAISED A GENUINE ISSUE OF MATERIAL FACT AS TO WHETHER HE PROVIDED HABITAT WITH ADEQUATE NOTICE UNDER THE FMLA.

Summary judgment is appropriate only where the movant demonstrates the absence of any genuine issue of material fact and entitlement to judgment as a matter of law. Celotex Corp. v. Catrett, 477 U.S. 317, 322 (1986). In assessing the record, this Court must view the evidence in the light most favorable to Burnett, and summary judgment is improper wherever "there is evidence on which the jury could reasonably find for the plaintiff." Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, Inc., 477 U.S. 242, 252 (1986). The district court disregarded this standard, resolving disputed facts concerning notice against Burnett rather than in his favor.

An employee seeking FMLA leave is not required to invoke the statute by name or to use any particular formula of words. This Court has consistently held that an employee satisfies the notice requirement so long as he supplies information reasonably sufficient to alert the employer that FMLA-qualifying leave may be needed. Price v. City of Fort Wayne, 117 F.3d 1022, 1026 (7th Cir. 1997); Stoops v. One Call Communications, Inc., 141 F.3d 309, 312 (7th Cir. 1998). The employer, once so informed, bears the responsibility of requesting whatever additional information it requires to determine whether the leave qualifies. Byrne v. Avon Products, Inc., 328 F.3d 379, 381 (7th Cir. 2003) ("[T]he employer's duties are triggered when the employee provides information that would tend to allow it to conclude that FMLA leave might be at issue."). The burden thus does not fall on the employee to diagnose himself in medical or legal terms; it falls on the employer to seek clarification once it is on notice that a serious health condition may exist. Aubuchon v. Knauf Fiberglass, GmbH, 359 F.3d 950, 953 (7th Cir. 2004).

The record here demonstrates far more than the "scintilla of evidence" insufficient to withstand summary judgment. Anderson, 477 U.S. at 252. Burnett told Polo in October 2003 that he suffered from a "weak bladder" and intended to see a doctor. He returned in December with a written doctor's order for blood testing and explained that testing had revealed elevated PSA and cholesterol levels, together with the need to see a urologist. At the December 16 meeting, in the presence of multiple managers and a union representative, Burnett stated that he was "sick" or "getting sick," and specifically analogized his condition to his brother-in-law's prostate cancer. By January 7, 2004, Burnett had given his supervisor a document describing a scheduled prostate biopsy, which itself explained that such a procedure follows "an abnormal rectal exam or an elevation of prostate cancer screening blood test (PSA)." This sequence of escalating disclosures — from a doctor's order, to a description of specific abnormal test results, to an explicit comparison with a relative's cancer, to written documentation of an impending biopsy — is precisely the kind of information that puts an employer on notice that a serious health condition may be at issue and obligates the employer to ask follow-up questions rather than issue reprimands. Price, 117 F.3d at 1026. Habitat's failure to request clarification does not transform Burnett's disclosures into insufficient notice; it establishes, at minimum, a triable question for the jury.

II. BURNETT PRESENTED SUFFICIENT EVIDENCE THAT HABITAT HAD NOTICE OF HIS DISABILITY UNDER THE ADA TO SURVIVE SUMMARY JUDGMENT.

The ADA imposes upon an employer a duty to engage in an interactive process once it is aware, or should reasonably be aware, of an employee's disability and need for accommodation. Beck v. University of Wisconsin Board of Regents, 75 F.3d 1130, 1135 (7th Cir. 1996). An employee need not utter the word "disability" or cite the statute; he need only communicate facts sufficient to alert the employer that a medical condition may be substantially limiting and that accommodation may be required. Hendricks-Robinson v. Excel Corp., 154 F.3d 685, 693 (7th Cir. 1998). Once an employer possesses such information, it "has at least some responsibility" for determining what accommodation is necessary and may not simply sit on its hands. Bultemeyer v. Fort Wayne Community Schools, 100 F.3d 1281, 1285 (7th Cir. 1996).

Burnett's disclosures — his weak bladder, his elevated PSA levels, his urology referral, his statements comparing his condition to his brother-in-law's prostate cancer, and his scheduled biopsy — collectively gave Habitat ample basis to recognize that Burnett might be suffering from a substantially limiting impairment. Rather than seeking clarification consistent with its obligations under Bultemeyer and Hendricks-Robinson, Habitat instead issued a rapid series of reprimands culminating in Burnett's effective termination. That course of conduct does not entitle Habitat to judgment as a matter of law; it presents a jury question regarding what Habitat knew and when.

CONCLUSION

Because genuine issues of material fact remain concerning the adequacy of the notice Burnett provided under both the FMLA and the ADA, the district court's grant of summary judgment should be reversed and the case remanded for trial. Celotex, 477 U.S. at 322; Anderson, 477 U.S. at 252.
