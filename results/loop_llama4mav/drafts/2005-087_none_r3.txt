The respondent respectfully submits that the Fourteenth Amendment due process clause does not guarantee a defendant the right to present expert evidence about his mental state to counter the prosecution's evidence of criminal intent, separate from his insanity plea.

The Arizona Supreme Court's decision to limit the use of Clark's expert evidence to his insanity claim is consistent with established precedent. The Court has long recognized that states have significant latitude in defining the rules of evidence and procedure in their courts. As the Court held in Twining v. New Jersey, 211 U.S. 78 (1908), the due process clause does not impose a rigid set of rules on the states, and states are not bound to provide the same procedure for their citizens as is required by the federal Constitution for federal courts. Id. at 101. This principle is reinforced by Brown v. Mississippi, 297 U.S. 278 (1936), where the Court noted that the due process clause guarantees fundamental principles of liberty and justice, not a specific form of procedure. Id. at 285.

The respondent argues that the trial court's limitation on the use of Clark's expert evidence did not deprive him of a fundamental right. The due process clause does not guarantee a defendant the right to present all evidence he desires. Fong Yue Ting v. United States, 149 U.S. 698 (1893), established that the power to exclude evidence is a part of the judicial power, and the Constitution does not guarantee a defendant the right to present all evidence he may desire. Id. at 730. Similarly, Sinclair v. United States, 279 U.S. 263 (1929), held that the due process clause does not require the admission of all relevant evidence. Id. at 269.

The Court has also recognized that the insanity defense is a distinct issue that requires separate consideration from the elements of the crime. Davis v. United States, 160 U.S. 469 (1895), held that the insanity defense is a "substantive" issue that is separate from the question of whether the defendant formed the requisite criminal intent. Id. at 488. The Arizona rule is consistent with this principle, as it requires the defendant to prove insanity separately from the prosecution's burden to prove the elements of the crime. Leland v. Oregon, 343 U.S. 790 (1952), further supports this approach, upholding an Oregon statute that placed the burden of proving insanity on the defendant.

Furthermore, the Court has upheld state laws that restrict the use of certain types of evidence. Shepard v. United States, 290 U.S. 96 (1933), held that a state law excluding certain evidence did not violate the due process clause. The Court has also recognized that states have flexibility in regulating the presentation of evidence, as seen in Snyder v. Massachusetts, 291 U.S. 97 (1934), and Jordan v. Massachusetts, 225 U.S. 167 (1912). Additionally, the Court's decision in Lawton v. Steele, 152 U.S. 133 (1894), and its holding in Re Kemmler, 136 U.S. 436 (1890), demonstrate the state's authority to regulate the admissibility of evidence.

The respondent concludes that the Arizona Supreme Court's decision to limit the use of Clark's expert evidence to his insanity claim is consistent with established precedent and does not violate the Fourteenth Amendment due process clause. The Court should affirm the judgment of the Arizona Supreme Court.
