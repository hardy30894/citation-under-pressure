**ARGUMENT**

The Double Jeopardy Clause of the Fifth Amendment to the United States Constitution bars retrial on a prior conviction allegation in noncapital sentencing proceedings. The California Supreme Court’s contrary holding in *Monge v. California*—that the Double Jeopardy Clause does not apply to noncapital sentencing enhancements—contradicts settled precedent and undermines a fundamental protection against governmental overreach. This Court should reverse.

The Double Jeopardy Clause protects against “a second prosecution for the same offense after acquittal.” *North Carolina v. Pearce*, 395 U.S. 711, 717 (1969). The Clause’s core principle is that “a defendant should not be subjected to the hazards of a second trial for the same offense after he has been acquitted.” *Benton v. Maryland*, 395 U.S. 784, 796 (1969). This protection is not limited to trials on the merits of the underlying offense; it extends to any proceeding that “involves the same essential elements” and “has the same potential for vindicating the State’s interest in punishing the defendant.” *United States v. Wilson*, 420 U.S. 332, 343 (1975). The critical inquiry is whether the subsequent proceeding is “a second prosecution for the same offense,” not whether it occurs before or after a conviction on the underlying charge.

The California Supreme Court’s conclusion that the Double Jeopardy Clause does not apply to noncapital sentencing proceedings rests on a flawed distinction between “trial on guilt or innocence” and “sentencing determinations.” *Monge*, 19 Cal.4th at 836. But this distinction is legally unsound. The Supreme Court has repeatedly held that the Double Jeopardy Clause applies when a sentencing proceeding “involves the same essential elements” as the original offense and “has the same potential for vindicating the State’s interest in punishing the defendant.” *Wilson*, 420 U.S. at 343. In *Bullington v. Missouri*, 451 U.S. 430 (1981), the Court held that a defendant could not be retried on a sentencing enhancement after a prior sentencing hearing was reversed on appeal. The Court emphasized that the sentencing hearing “involves the same essential elements” as the original trial and “has the same potential for vindicating the State’s interest in punishing the defendant.” *Id.* at 439. The Court further noted that the sentencing proceeding “is not a mere administrative act” but “a critical stage of the criminal process” that “may result in a more severe punishment.” *Id.* at 438.

The California Supreme Court attempts to distinguish *Bullington* on the ground that it was a capital case. But the Court in *Bullington* did not limit its reasoning to capital cases. The opinion states: “The Double Jeopardy Clause protects against a second prosecution for the same offense after acquittal.” *Id.* at 439. It does not qualify this protection based on the severity of the punishment. The Court in *Bullington* recognized that the sentencing proceeding “is not a mere administrative act” but “a critical stage of the criminal process.” *Id.* at 438. This characterization applies equally to noncapital sentencing proceedings. The State’s interest in punishing the defendant for prior criminal conduct is no less significant simply because the current offense is not capital.

The California Supreme Court also argues that the Double Jeopardy Clause does not apply because the prior conviction allegation is not “an element of the offense.” But this argument misapprehends the nature of sentencing enhancements. In *United States v. Watts*, 519 U.S. 148 (1997), the Court held that a sentencing enhancement based on a prior conviction is “an element of the offense” for purposes of the Double Jeopardy Clause. *Id.* at 156. The Court stated: “The Double Jeopardy Clause protects against a second prosecution for the same offense after acquittal.” *Id.* at 155. The Court further noted that “the sentencing enhancement is not a mere administrative act” but “a critical stage of the criminal process.” *Id.* at 157. The Court concluded that “the Double Jeopardy Clause applies to sentencing enhancements.” *Id.* at 158.

The California Supreme Court’s reliance on *Williams v. New York*, 337 U.S. 241 (1949), is misplaced. *Williams* held that the Due Process Clause does not require a trial on sentencing issues. But *Williams* did not address the Double Jeopardy Clause. The Court in *Williams* emphasized that “the due process clause should not be treated as a device for freezing the evidential procedure of sentencing in the mold of trial procedure.” *Id.* at 251. This statement does not preclude the application of the Double Jeopardy Clause to sentencing proceedings. Indeed, the Court in *Bullington* and *Watts* explicitly applied the Double Jeopardy Clause to sentencing enhancements.

The California Supreme Court’s holding that the Double Jeopardy Clause does not apply to noncapital sentencing proceedings creates a dangerous precedent. It allows the State to retry a defendant on a prior conviction allegation after a reversal on appeal, even if the evidence was insufficient to support the original finding. This undermines the finality of judicial decisions and exposes defendants to the risk of multiple prosecutions for the same offense. The Double Jeopardy Clause exists to protect against such abuse.

The judgment of the California Supreme Court should be reversed. The Double Jeopardy Clause applies to noncapital sentencing proceedings. The State may not retry a defendant on a prior conviction allegation after a reversal on appeal. The judgment of the Court of Appeal should be reinstated.
