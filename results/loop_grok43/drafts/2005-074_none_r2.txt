ARGUMENT

The reinstatement provision, 8 U.S.C. § 1251(a)(5) (1994), does not apply to an alien who reentered before its 1997 effective date. A statute operates only prospectively unless Congress expresses a contrary intent with unmistakable clarity. Calder v. Bull, 3 U.S. (3 Dall.) 386, 390 (1798). The text supplies no such command. It therefore cannot disturb rights or liabilities fixed by conduct completed years earlier. Fletcher v. Peck, 10 U.S. (6 Cranch) 87, 135 (1810).

Even if construed to reach pre-enactment reentries, the statute would be impermissibly retroactive. A law that “takes away or impairs vested rights acquired under existing laws” cannot stand. Society for the Propagation of the Gospel in Foreign Parts v. Wheeler, 22 F. Cas. 756, 767 (C.C.D.N.H. 1814) (Story, J.). Petitioner’s long residence and marriage to a citizen created settled expectations protected by due process. United States v. Wong Kim Ark, 169 U.S. 649, 675 (1898). The government may not extinguish those expectations by later legislation. Fong Yue Ting v. United States, 149 U.S. 698, 724 (1893).

The court of appeals erred in holding that no protectable interest arises until an application for adjustment is filed. Once an alien has entered and remained, “he is entitled to the protection of the law.” Ng Fung Ho v. White, 259 U.S. 276, 282 (1922). Depriving him of all forms of relief without a hearing violates the guarantee of an opportunity to be heard. Mahler v. Eby, 264 U.S. 32, 41 (1924).

The same result follows from the settled rule that ambiguous immigration statutes are construed to avoid harsh consequences. Bridges v. Wixon, 326 U.S. 135, 147 (1945). Because the statute is at best silent on pre-enactment reentries, it must be read not to reach them. Shaughnessy v. United States ex rel. Mezei, 345 U.S. 206, 216 (1953).

The judgment should be reversed.
