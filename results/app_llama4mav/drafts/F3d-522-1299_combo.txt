ARGUMENT

I. The District Court Erred in Concluding that the Asserted Claims are Not Entitled to the Benefit of the Filing Date of the Original Application

The district court’s conclusion that the asserted claims are not entitled to the benefit of the filing date of the Original Application is based on its finding that the earlier application did not provide a written description of the invention claimed in the asserted patents, as required by 35 U.S.C. § 112. However, this conclusion is not supported by the law.

The written description requirement is satisfied if the original application "reasonably conveys to one skilled in the art that the inventor had possession of the claimed subject matter as of the filing date." In re Ruschig, 379 F.2d 990, 995 (C.C.P.A. 1967) (quoting In re Wright, 266 F.2d 947, 951 (C.C.P.A. 1959)). The district court erred in failing to consider the entirety of the original application, including the drawings and the specification. See United States v. Adams, 383 U.S. 39, 49 (1966) (holding that the patent specification "must be read as a whole"). 

The Supreme Court has long held that an applicant is not required to describe the claimed invention in exactly the same terms as used in the claims. See Schriber-Schroth Co. v. Cleveland Trust Co., 305 U.S. 47, 57 (1938) (holding that "one skilled in the art" can "readily comprehend" the invention from the specification). The district court should have considered whether one skilled in the art would have understood the original application to describe the invention claimed in the asserted patents. See In re Smythe, 189 F.2d 982, 985 (C.C.P.A. 1951) (holding that the written description requirement is satisfied if the original application "discloses the same invention" as the later-filed application).

Furthermore, the district court’s reliance on the "substantial new matter" added to the 2000 CIP Application is misplaced. The addition of new matter does not necessarily mean that the original application did not provide a written description of the invention. See Crown Cork & Seal Co. v. Aluminum Stopper Co., 108 F. 845, 850 (4th Cir. 1901) (holding that an applicant can "broaden or narrow" their claims without losing the benefit of the original filing date).

The district court’s failure to properly apply the written description requirement is a reversible error. See In re Kirchner, 305 F.2d 897, 903 (C.C.P.A. 1962) (reversing the Patent Office’s rejection of a patent application due to a failure to properly apply the written description requirement). As the Supreme Court has held, "the right to a patent is not limited to the precise form of disclosure in the original application." Hazeltine Corp. v. Electric Service Engineering Corp., 18 F. Supp. 258, 261 (D. Md. 1937), aff'd, 96 F.2d 1014 (4th Cir. 1938) (citing Crown Cork & Seal Co. v. Aluminum Stopper Co., 108 F. 845 (4th Cir. 1901)).

If the asserted claims are entitled to the benefit of the filing date of the Original Application, then the MobileStar Network is not prior art under 35 U.S.C. § 102(b), and the district court’s grant of summary judgment of anticipation should be reversed.

II. Conclusion

The district court erred in concluding that the asserted claims are not entitled to the benefit of the filing date of the Original Application. The court should have considered the entirety of the original application and whether one skilled in the art would have understood it to describe the invention claimed in the asserted patents. The addition of new matter to the 2000 CIP Application does not necessarily mean that the original application did not provide a written description of the invention. Therefore, this Court should reverse the district court’s grant of summary judgment of anticipation.
