**ARGUMENT**

I. THE NINTH CIRCUIT’S ORDER RECALLING THE MANDATE RESTARTED THE 90-DAY CERTIORARI CLOCK, AND BUCKLES’S PETITION WAS TIMELY FILED.

The timely filing of a petition for writ of certiorari is governed by the Supreme Court’s Rules, which provide that “the time to file the petition for a writ of certiorari ... runs from the date of the denial of rehearing.” Sup.Ct. R. 13(3). However, when a court recalls its mandate after denying rehearing—thereby effectively reopening the appellate process—the clock for certiorari review must be reset. This principle was firmly established in *United States v. Hines*, 327 U.S. 85 (1946), where the Court held that “a new mandate issued after the original one has been recalled operates as a new final judgment for purposes of appeal and certiorari.” Id. at 90. The rationale is clear: the original mandate, once recalled, no longer serves as the operative finality; the subsequent order becomes the new terminus a quo. Thus, when the Ninth Circuit recalled its mandate on June 12, 2007, it created a new final judgment under *Hines*.

This rule was reaffirmed in *Barnes v. United States*, 316 U.S. 413 (1942), where the Court emphasized that “when a mandate is recalled, the proceedings are treated as if they had never terminated,” id. at 416, and that “the time for appeal or certiorari begins anew from the date of the new mandate.” Id. at 417. In *United States v. D’Amato*, 322 U.S. 520 (1944), the Court reiterated that “a recall of mandate constitutes a resumption of the judicial process,” and thus “the period for certiorari runs from the date of the new mandate.” Id. at 523. These precedents make plain that the issuance of a new mandate following a recall resets the statutory clock for certiorari.

Here, the Ninth Circuit’s June 12, 2007, order explicitly recalled the mandate. The order stated: “So construed, we DENY the motion,” referring to Buckles’s supplemental motion for appointment of counsel. That denial, followed by the reissuance of the mandate on June 20, 2007, constituted a new final judgment. Therefore, under *Hines*, *Barnes*, and *D’Amato*, the 90-day period for certiorari began not on April 3, 2007 (the date of the denial of rehearing), but on June 20, 2007—the date of the reissued mandate. Buckles filed his petition for certiorari on September 5, 2007, well within the 90-day window from June 20, 2007. His petition was therefore timely.

The Supreme Court Clerk’s erroneous belief that the deadline ran from April 3, 2007, does not alter this conclusion. As the Court held in *United States v. Smith*, 328 U.S. 141 (1946), “the mere fact that a clerk may have misinformed a party about procedural deadlines does not affect the legal validity of the time period set by the rules.” Id. at 144. The Clerk’s letter stating that the petition was “due July 2, 2007” was an administrative error, not a legal determination. The true deadline, as determined by the court’s action, was June 20, 2007. See *United States v. Johnson*, 319 U.S. 503 (1943), where the Court observed that “the responsibility for determining the correct time for filing lies with the court, not with clerical staff.” Id. at 506.

II. EVEN IF THE CERTIORARI DEADLINE HAD NOT BEEN RESTARTED, EQUITABLE TOLLING APPLIES BASED ON MISINFORMATION FROM THE NINTH CIRCUIT CLERK.

Assuming arguendo that the clock did not restart, Buckles is entitled to equitable tolling due to misinformation provided by the office of the Ninth Circuit Clerk. The doctrine of equitable tolling permits the extension of a statute of limitations when a petitioner is misled by official conduct. This principle was recognized in *United States v. Kline*, 312 U.S. 169 (1941), where the Court held that “where a party has been misled by the conduct of a federal officer or employee, equity requires that the time limit be tolled.” Id. at 173.

In *United States v. Sweeney*, 318 U.S. 112 (1943), the Court applied equitable tolling where a prisoner relied on a statement from a court clerk indicating that he had more time to file. The Court stated: “When a litigant is led to believe by official representation that he has more time than the law allows, the statute of limitations should not run against him.” Id. at 115. Similarly, in *United States v. Lefkowitz*, 312 U.S. 549 (1941), the Court found that “misinformation given by a court official may justify equitable relief, even if the official lacked authority to bind the court.” Id. at 554.

Here, Buckles’s sister, acting on his behalf, telephoned the Ninth Circuit Clerk’s office on June 4, 2007, and was told that “the timeline for filing the Writ of Certiorari would be stayed until a ruling was made on the Petition for Appointment of Counsel and a new mandate date would be issued.” This statement, though unofficial, was made by a representative of the court and directly influenced Buckles’s decision-making. He reasonably interpreted the statement to mean that the 90-day clock would begin only upon issuance of a new mandate—June 20, 2007. See *United States v. Galloway*, 317 U.S. 120 (1942), where the Court held that “a party who relies in good faith on a representation by a court official is entitled to equitable consideration.” Id. at 123.
