ARGUMENT

I. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN ADMITTING THE DEFENSE EXPERT'S OPINION ON CAUSATION BECAUSE THE OPINION WAS NOT “EXPERT” TESTIMONY.

Expert opinion is admissible only when it "assist[s] the trier of fact to understand the evidence or to determine a fact in issue," and the trial court sits as a "gatekeeper" charged with ensuring that "any and all scientific testimony or evidence admitted is not only relevant, but reliable." Daubert v. Merrell Dow Pharms., Inc., 509 U.S. 579, 589-91 (1993). Where the subject matter is within the ordinary understanding of lay jurors, and the witness's opinion amounts to nothing more than a guess dressed in credentials, the testimony is not expert testimony at all, and the requirements of reliability and helpfulness are not met. Salem v. United States Lines Co., 370 U.S. 31, 35 (1962) (recognizing that expert opinion is unnecessary and its admission is error where "there was no such necessity in this case").

Here, American Steamship's witness did not apply any specialized methodology to the physical evidence; he simply speculated, from a description of the accident, that the torch flame must have weakened the manila line. That is precisely the kind of unsubstantiated inference the Supreme Court has long condemned as insufficient to support a jury finding, whether offered by a lay witness or an "expert." See Pennsylvania R.R. Co. v. Chamberlain, 288 U.S. 333, 339 (1933) (holding that a verdict may not rest on "speculation and conjecture"). Because the witness's opinion was not the product of reliable methodology and did not require any scientific, technical, or specialized knowledge beyond what the jurors themselves possessed, it should have been excluded, and its admission was not harmless: the jury's fifty-percent reduction for comparative fault tracks directly to the defense theory that Cook's own torch work caused the line to part.

II. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN DENYING COOK JUDGMENT AS A MATTER OF LAW ON UNSEAWORTHINESS, BUT NOT ON COMPARATIVE NEGLIGENCE.

A. Unseaworthiness.

A shipowner's duty to furnish a seaworthy vessel, including seaworthy gear and equipment, is "essentially a species of liability without fault," Mitchell v. Trawler Racer, Inc., 362 U.S. 539, 549 (1960), and traces to the ancient maritime obligation recognized in The Osceola, 189 U.S. 158, 175 (1903). Unseaworthiness is established once the plaintiff shows that "a condition of the ship, its equipment, or its appurtenances" rendered the vessel not reasonably fit for its intended use. Usner v. Luckenbach Overseas Corp., 400 U.S. 494, 499 (1971). The uncontroverted evidence showed that the bos'n's chair line parted under ordinary lowering conditions, that the boom bearings were still packed with frozen summer grease, and that the crew had only fifteen to twenty minutes—rather than the customary hour—to rig the equipment safely. On this record, no reasonable jury could have found the vessel and its appurtenances reasonably fit for their intended purpose; the district court therefore erred in submitting the unseaworthiness question to the jury rather than granting judgment as a matter of law. See Boeing Co. v. Shipman, 411 F.2d 365, 374-75 (5th Cir. 1969) (en banc) (directed verdict is warranted where "the facts and inferences point so strongly and overwhelmingly in favor of one party that the Court believes that reasonable men could not arrive at a contrary verdict").

B. Comparative Negligence.

The comparative negligence question, however, was properly left to the jury. Under the Jones Act, a seaman's contributory negligence does not bar recovery but merely diminishes damages "in proportion to the amount of negligence attributable" to him. Socony-Vacuum Oil Co. v. Smith, 305 U.S. 424, 431 (1939). Because the record contained conflicting evidence about how and for how long Cook applied his torch near the rigged line, reasonable jurors could differ as to whether Cook acted with ordinary prudence under the emergency conditions the master had created. Rogers v. Missouri Pac. R.R. Co., 352 U.S. 500, 506-08 (1957) (jury question exists where the proofs justify with reason more than one conclusion as to fault). The district court thus correctly denied judgment as a matter of law on that issue, even though a new trial is required in light of the erroneously admitted causation testimony discussed above.

III. THE DISTRICT COURT DID NOT ERR IN ADMITTING EVIDENCE OF COOK'S ALCOHOL-RELATED AILMENTS.

Evidence bearing on a seaman's physical condition and fitness for duty is relevant to the jury's assessment of comparative fault under the Jones Act's "any negligence" standard. Rogers, 352 U.S. at 506-08. Courts have long permitted evidence of intoxication or alcohol-related impairment where it bears on the seaman's own conduct contributing to his injury. Chisholm v. Sabine Towing & Transp. Co., 679 F.2d 60, 62 (5th Cir. 1982). Because the alcohol-related evidence here was tied to Cook's physical capacity to perform the deicing and rigging work safely, and because the district court instructed the jury on its limited purpose, admission of that evidence was not an abuse of discretion.

CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment should be vacated and the case remanded for a new trial, with judgment as a matter of law entered for Cook on unseaworthiness.
