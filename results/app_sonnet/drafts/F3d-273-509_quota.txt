ARGUMENT

I. THE RELEASE DOES NOT BAR BANCORP’S CLAIMS BECAUSE ITS TEXT IS LIMITED TO CLAIMS ARISING FROM THE DECISION TO CLOSE THE BANK

The District Court erred in reading the June 6, 1988 release as encompassing claims that have nothing to do with the transaction that produced it. By its own terms, Bancorp released the FDIC only “from any and all claims which Bancorp . . . has or may have arising from or with respect to the decision of the [OCC] to close the Bank on June 21, 1985.” That language is not a general release of all disputes between the parties; it is confined to the closure decision itself.

New York law requires that even broadly worded releases be read in light of the controversy that produced them. In Cahill v. Regan, 5 N.Y.2d 292 (1959), the Court of Appeals held that a release, however general its phrasing, must be limited to the particular claims the parties intended to settle. The Court of Appeals reaffirmed that principle in Mangini v. McClurg, 24 N.Y.2d 556 (1969), holding that the scope of a release is determined by the parties’ intent as reflected in the dispute actually being resolved, not by the outermost reach of its words.

Here, the release was exchanged to settle the May 1986 action, in which Bancorp’s former president claimed the FDIC had wrongfully appropriated his law offices, and the September 1985 action, in which the FDIC sought recovery of loans and guarantees from Bank officers and directors. See FDIC v. Chuang, 690 F.Supp. 192, 193 (S.D.N.Y. 1988) (describing the FDIC’s claim for “assets from [those] who allegedly owe[d] funds to the Bank either as borrowers or guarantors”). Neither dispute concerned the FDIC’s subsequent administration of the receivership estate — its decision to pay itself post-insolvency interest beginning in 1991, its allocation of legal and office expenses to the estate, its sale of Bank assets, or its duty to account. Those claims arise from years of post-closure conduct as receiver, not from the OCC’s 1985 closure decision. Under Cahill and Mangini, the release’s plain limitation to claims “arising from or with respect to the decision . . . to close the Bank” cannot be stretched to cover conduct occurring long after that decision was made and executed. The District Court’s contrary reading effectively converts a narrow, transaction-specific release into a general release the parties never bargained for.

II. THE STATUTE OF LIMITATIONS DID NOT BEGIN TO RUN UNTIL THE FDIC TERMINATED THE RECEIVERSHIP

Even if the release did not resolve this appeal, the District Court’s alternative holding — that C.P.L.R. § 213(7)’s six-year period bars Bancorp’s claims — is likewise erroneous. Bancorp’s claims for unjust enrichment, breach of fiduciary duty, and corporate waste all stem from the FDIC’s conduct as receiver of the Bank, a role in which the FDIC owed fiduciary obligations to Bancorp as residual claimant of the estate.

New York law has long held that a cause of action premised on a fiduciary’s conduct in administering an estate does not accrue while the fiduciary relationship continues; it accrues only upon termination of that relationship or open repudiation of it. See Spallholz v. Sheldon, 216 N.Y. 205 (1915); In re Barabash, 31 N.Y.2d 76 (1972) (accrual of accounting-related claims tied to the close of the fiduciary relationship). The FDIC’s receivership of the Bank did not terminate until November 1, 1995. Until that date, the FDIC retained control over the estate’s assets, continued to administer expenses, and had not rendered any final accounting to Bancorp. Bancorp could not know the full extent of the FDIC’s self-dealing — including the cumulative effect of interest payments to itself and expense allocations — until the receivership wound up. Bancorp filed suit on October 31, 1995, one day before termination, well within any limitations period measured from that date.

Application of New York’s fiduciary-accrual principles to an FDIC receivership is consistent with the Supreme Court’s recognition that state law ordinarily supplies the substantive rules governing the FDIC’s liabilities as receiver absent a significant conflicting federal interest. O’Melveny & Myers v. FDIC, 512 U.S. 79, 83–85 (1994). No such conflict exists here; nothing in federal receivership law suggests that a beneficiary’s claims against the FDIC as receiver should accrue earlier than they would against any other fiduciary administering an ongoing estate.

Nor can the FDIC escape this rule by characterizing its post-insolvency interest payments as discrete, time-barred acts. Even discrete acts of retention that unjustly enrich a fiduciary are properly measured by the continuation of the benefit retained, particularly where, as here, the fiduciary relationship persisted and the ultimate scope of the retained benefit could not be known until the estate closed. See Henry Modell & Co. v. Minister, Elders & Deacons, 68 N.Y.2d 456 (1986) (accrual of unjust enrichment tied to the wrongful retention of a benefit). The FDIC’s continuing administration of the estate through November 1, 1995 — charging expenses, retaining interest, and controlling Bank assets — means Bancorp’s claims did not, and could not, accrue before that date.

CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the District Court should be reversed, and the case remanded for further proceedings on the merits of Bancorp’s claims.
