Milford violated the Good News Club’s First Amendment rights when it excluded the Club from meeting after hours in an otherwise open community forum. The school district opened its facilities to district residents for social, civic, recreational, and other community-welfare uses. Once government creates such a forum, it may not discriminate against speech because of disagreement with the speaker’s viewpoint. Hague v. CIO, 307 U.S. 496, 515-16 (1939); De Jonge v. Oregon, 299 U.S. 353, 364-65 (1937); Niemotko v. Maryland, 340 U.S. 268, 272-73 (1951). Milford did precisely that. It allowed comparable youth and community groups to use the school, but denied the Club because its instruction was overtly Christian. That is viewpoint discrimination, and the First Amendment forbids it. West Virginia State Bd. of Educ. v. Barnette, 319 U.S. 624, 642 (1943); Stromberg v. California, 283 U.S. 359, 368-69 (1931); Meyer v. Nebraska, 262 U.S. 390, 400 (1923).

The Club’s activities were also protected religious speech. The Constitution does not permit government to treat religious expression as a disfavored category outside the protection of free speech. Cantwell v. Connecticut, 310 U.S. 296, 303-04 (1940), held that the Constitution safeguards the “free exercise and enjoyment of religious profession and worship.” Murdock v. Pennsylvania, 319 U.S. 105, 110-11 (1943), protected the distribution of religious literature and solicitation in furtherance of religious expression. Follett v. McCormick, 321 U.S. 573, 577-78 (1944), likewise rejected burdens on the dissemination of religious views. And Niemotko, 340 U.S. at 272-73, held that a city could not deny a permit for religious meetings based on hostility to their religious character. Milford’s denial is constitutionally indistinguishable: it barred the Club because its speech included prayer, Bible lessons, scripture memorization, and invitation to faith.

The Establishment Clause does not justify that exclusion. The Clause forbids government sponsorship of religion, but it does not authorize the State to exclude private religious speech from an otherwise open forum. Everson v. Board of Education, 330 U.S. 1, 15-18 (1947), held that government must maintain neutrality between religion and nonreligion. Zorach v. Clauson, 343 U.S. 306, 313-15 (1952), reaffirmed that the Constitution does not require “callous indifference” to religious activity. Engel v. Vitale, 370 U.S. 421, 431-32 (1962), and School Dist. of Abington Twp. v. Schempp, 374 U.S. 203, 222-23 (1963), barred state-sponsored prayer and Bible reading in public schools, but they did not hold that a school may exclude private religious speech when it opens its facilities for community use. Here, Milford did not face compelled prayer by school officials or a religious exercise imposed on students; it faced a private after-hours club seeking equal access under a neutral-use policy. Neutral access is not establishment. Everson, 330 U.S. at 17-18; Zorach, 343 U.S. at 314.

Milford’s attempt to recast the Club’s meetings as “religious worship” does not alter the analysis. The Court has long recognized that religious expression is protected expression, even when it is devotional or evangelistic. Cantwell, 310 U.S. at 304; Murdock, 319 U.S. at 110-11; Follett, 321 U.S. at 577-78. The school’s policy did not exclude all speech on a subject it had left outside the forum; it excluded a particular perspective on morals and character because that perspective was religious. That is the very discrimination the First Amendment forbids. Barnette, 319 U.S. at 642; Niemotko, 340 U.S. at 272-73.

Nor is Milford helped by any claim that children might perceive endorsement. The proper constitutional response to any such concern is equal treatment and clarification that private speech is not school speech. The State may not avoid an asserted Establishment Clause problem by suppressing disfavored private religious expression. Everson, 330 U.S. at 15-18; Zorach, 343 U.S. at 313-15. The Constitution requires neutrality, not hostility. Because Milford excluded the Good News Club based on its religious viewpoint, and because the Establishment Clause does not require that discrimination, the judgment should be reversed.
