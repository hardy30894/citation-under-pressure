**Argument**

ANILCA does not strip the National Park Service of all regulatory authority over nonfederal lands and waters inside Alaska park boundaries. The text, structure, and history of the statute show a narrower rule: Congress preserved Alaska’s special treatment of “nonpublic lands” as land ownership, while leaving intact otherwise applicable federal regulatory authority. The Ninth Circuit’s contrary reading would convert § 103(c) into a broad jurisdictional ouster, but Congress used no language remotely that sweeping.

First, the statutory text does not support petitioner’s reading. Section 103(c) addresses “nonpublic lands” and provides that their inclusion within a conservation system unit “shall not be construed as making such lands part of the public lands of the United States.” 16 U.S.C. § 3103(c). That phrase speaks to title and land status, not to an across-the-board immunity from federal regulation. The Supreme Court has repeatedly distinguished between a state’s or private party’s proprietary interests and governmental regulatory authority. See Organized Village of Kake v. Egan, 369 U.S. 60, 67–69 (1962) (the State’s disclaimer of title was a disclaimer of “proprietary rather than governmental interest”); Alaska v. Native Village of Venetie Tribal Government, 522 U.S. 520, 529–30 (1998) (discussing ANCSA’s transfer of title and the absence of federal restrictions on transferred land). Section 103(c) uses the same ownership-focused language.

Second, Congress elsewhere in ANILCA confirmed that it did not intend to displace federal regulatory power over Alaska lands merely because those lands fall within park boundaries. In Hynes, Regional Director, Fish & Wildlife Service v. Grimes Packing Co., 337 U.S. 86, 99–101 (1949), the Court upheld federal regulations governing fishing methods in Alaska waters where Congress had authorized comprehensive federal management. Likewise, ANILCA’s structure preserves multiple federal mandates. See Amoco Production Co. v. Village of Gambell, 480 U.S. 531, 552 (1987) (ANILCA’s “primary purpose was to complete the allocation of federal lands in the State of Alaska”). Congress established extensive federal conservation units while also facilitating conveyances to the State and Native entities. Id. at 544–45. That dual structure makes little sense if § 103(c) silently disabled ordinary park regulation on any nonfederal parcel within those units.

Third, petitioner’s interpretation is inconsistent with the way the Court has treated federal authority over activities within Alaska and over lands subject to mixed jurisdiction. In Territory of Alaska v. Troy, 258 U.S. 101, 107–08 (1922), the Court recognized Congress’s broad power to legislate concerning Alaska as a federal territory. And in United States v. Pacific & Arctic Railway & Navigation Co., 228 U.S. 87, 103–05 (1913), the Court rejected the premise that location beyond one sovereign’s title disables federal control where the regulated conduct occurs within U.S. territory. The relevant question is the scope of the federal enactment, not ownership alone.

Fourth, petitioner’s reading would create serious practical anomalies. It would exempt state, Native, corporate, and private inholdings from generally applicable park rules, even where the regulation governs conduct affecting park resources, visitors, and safety. Yet Congress did not say that nonfederal lands inside a unit are regulatory islands. When Congress intends to limit federal authority so sharply, it speaks clearly. See Colorado v. Toll, 268 U.S. 228, 230–31 (1925) (finding federal park regulations that purported to displace state authority over highways and private property only by examining the specific regulations and their asserted reach). Here, the hovercraft ban is a general regulation applicable to park-administered lands and waters; it is not a regulation targeted solely at nonpublic lands.

Finally, petitioner cannot rely on isolated language suggesting that Alaska lands remain under state ownership. Title and jurisdiction are distinct. Organized Village of Kake, 369 U.S. at 67–69. ANILCA preserved the former; it did not abolish the latter. The Ninth Circuit correctly held that the hovercraft regulation is not one that applies “solely to public lands,” and that conclusion accords with the statute’s text and this Court’s precedents.

For those reasons, the judgment should be affirmed.
