Milford’s exclusion of the Good News Club from after-hours meetings at a school it had otherwise opened to district residents for social, civic, recreational, and welfare-related uses violated the Club’s free speech rights. When the government creates a limited public forum, it may restrict access only on the basis of subject matter and may not discriminate against speech because of its viewpoint. Rosenberger v. Rector and Visitors of the University of Virginia, 515 U.S. 819, 828-29 (1995); Lamb’s Chapel v. Center Moriches Union Free School District, 508 U.S. 384, 392-94 (1993); Perry Education Assn. v. Perry Local Educators’ Assn., 460 U.S. 37, 46-49 (1983). Milford’s policy did not exclude religion as a subject matter from all use; it excluded only the Club’s religious perspective on the otherwise-permitted subject of character and moral development. That is viewpoint discrimination. Rosenberger, 515 U.S. at 831-32; Lamb’s Chapel, 508 U.S. at 393-94; Widmar v. Vincent, 454 U.S. 263, 269-70 & n.6 (1981).

The Club’s meetings fit squarely within the forum Milford opened. The record shows that Milford permitted resident groups to use school facilities for “social, civic and recreational meetings and entertainment events and other uses pertaining to the welfare of the community,” while excluding only “religious purposes.” In Lamb’s Chapel, the Court held that a school district violated the First Amendment by denying church use of school facilities to show a film series on child rearing from a Christian perspective, because the district had allowed other groups to discuss family and child-rearing issues. 508 U.S. at 393-94. The same reasoning controls here. Milford allowed groups like the Boy Scouts, Girl Scouts, and 4-H to teach youth development, but denied the Good News Club the opportunity to teach morals and character from a Christian perspective. A government may not “license one side of a debate to fight freestyle, while requiring the other to follow Marquis of Queensberry rules.” R.A.V. v. City of St. Paul, 505 U.S. 377, 392 (1992). Nor may it exclude speakers because their “viewpoint” is religious. Rosenberger, 515 U.S. at 831.

The Second Circuit erred in treating the Club’s activities as “religious instruction” outside the forum rather than as protected speech from a religious viewpoint. Rosenberger rejected the notion that speech becomes a different subject simply because it is informed by religious thought; “a religious perspective can constitute a separate viewpoint on a wide variety of seemingly secular subject matter.” 515 U.S. at 831. The Club sought to discuss morals, character, and child development—topics Milford had opened to other groups. That the Club taught those subjects through Bible lessons, prayer, and songs does not convert its speech into a distinct category the school may exclude. Widmar held that “singing hymns, reading scripture, and teaching biblical principles” are speech, not unprotected worship, and that a university could not exclude religious groups from a public forum merely because their speech was religious. 454 U.S. at 269 n.6, 276-77. The same principle applies with even greater force in an elementary-school forum opened to community groups. See Board of Education of Westside Community Schools v. Mergens, 496 U.S. 226, 248-50 (1990) (plurality opinion).

Milford’s Establishment Clause defense fails for the same reason. The Free Speech Clause does not yield merely because the government fears that equal access will create an Establishment Clause problem. Widmar, 454 U.S. at 271-75; Rosenberger, 515 U.S. at 845-46. In Widmar, the Court held that allowing religious speech in a limited public forum would not violate the Establishment Clause because the access policy was neutral and any endorsement was negated by the school’s open-forum character. 454 U.S. at 274-75. In Rosenberger, the Court likewise held that “neutral treatment” of religious expression did not amount to establishment. 515 U.S. at 839-46. Here, Milford’s equal access policy would have permitted a broad range of community uses; admitting the Good News Club on the same terms would have been neutral private speech, not state-sponsored religion.

The Court’s school-speech precedents reinforce that conclusion. In Lamb’s Chapel, the Court rejected the argument that permitting religious use of school facilities would violate the Establishment Clause, explaining that the school’s “open-forum policy” and disclaimers made endorsement unlikely. 508 U.S. at 395. In Good News Club v. Milford Central School, 533 U.S. 98 (2001), this Court later confirmed that excluding a similar Good News Club from an after-hours elementary-school forum was impermissible viewpoint discrimination and that equal access did not violate the Establishment Clause. The same result follows here under settled First Amendment doctrine.

Milford’s exclusion also cannot be justified by the young age of the intended audience. The Court has repeatedly held that the proper answer to endorsement concerns is neutrality and disclaimer, not censorship. Mergens, 496 U.S. at 251-53; Rosenberger, 515 U.S. at 841-45. Because Milford excluded the Club solely for expressing a religious viewpoint on otherwise-allowed moral instruction, and because allowing the Club’s access would not have violated the Establishment Clause, the judgment below should be reversed.
