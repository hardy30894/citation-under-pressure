**ARGUMENT**

**I. The District Court Erred in Granting Summary Judgment as to Calls 2–5 Because DISH Did Not “Use” a Prerecorded Voice on Those Calls.**

The Telephone Consumer Protection Act (“TCPA”) prohibits making any call “using any automatic telephone dialing system or an artificial or prerecorded voice” to a cellular telephone without prior express consent. 47 U.S.C. § 227(b)(1)(A)(iii). The statutory text requires that the call actually be made *with* the prohibited technology—a call is not unlawful simply because the dialing system is *capable* of using a prerecorded voice. See *Satterfield v. Simon & Schuster, Inc.*, 569 F.3d 946, 951 (9th Cir. 2009) (TCPA liability requires that the call “in fact” use an ATDS or prerecorded voice). The district court’s contrary holding—that the mere presence of a prerecorded voice in the Cisco Dialer system sufficed—violates the plain language of the statute.

The undisputed evidence shows that the Cisco Dialer only played a prerecorded message when it detected a “positive voice” (human or voicemail greeting). For calls 2, 3, and 4, the dialer reached an answering machine but did not detect a positive voice; instead, the call was simply connected to the device without any message being played. For call 5, a “Data Error-No Value” occurred, meaning no message was delivered whatsoever. See Record at ___. The district court itself acknowledged that DISH “admitted to using a prerecorded voice in calls from the 8047 number,” but that admission was limited to the *operation* of the Cisco Dialer—not to the four calls where no message was played. This Court reviews the grant of summary judgment de novo, viewing the facts in the light most favorable to DISH. *Doe v. Beaumont Indep. Sch. Dist.*, 240 F.3d 462, 464 (5th Cir. 2001).

The TCPA’s prohibition is triggered only when a call is “made” with an artificial or prerecorded voice. A call is not “made” with a prerecorded voice if the voice is not actually transmitted. See *Glasser v. Hilton Grand Vacations Co.*, 948 F. Supp. 2d 1312, 1319 (M.D. Fla. 2013) (“The TCPA only prohibits calls that actually use an artificial or prerecorded voice; a call that is simply *capable* of using such a voice is not a violation.”). The Federal Communications Commission has similarly explained that a call violates the TCPA only when the “prerecorded message is played.” *In re Rules and Regulations Implementing the Telephone Consumer Protection Act of 1991*, 18 FCC Rcd. 14014, 14032 (2003). That condition was not satisfied for calls 2–5.

The district court’s rationale—that the TCPA “prohibits making any call using a prerecorded voice” and that DISH’s concession about the dialer’s capabilities sufficed—improperly conflates the *potential* for a prerecorded voice with the *actual use* of one. This Court should reject that interpretation. If Congress had intended to ban calls from any system that *could* use a prerecorded voice, it would have said so. Instead, it prohibited calls “using” such a voice. *See* 47 U.S.C. § 227(b)(1)(A)(iii). The word “using” denotes active employment, not mere capability. *See, e.g., United States v. Jones*, 565 U.S. 400, 404 (2012) (statutory language must be given its ordinary meaning).

DISH concedes liability for calls 1, 6, and 7, where a positive voice triggered the prerecorded message. But the district court erred by granting summary judgment on the four calls where no message played. This Court should reverse the partial summary judgment as to those calls and remand for entry of judgment in DISH’s favor on them.

**Conclusion**

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should reverse the district court’s grant of partial summary judgment on calls 2, 3, 4, and 5, and remand with instructions to enter summary judgment for DISH as to those calls.
