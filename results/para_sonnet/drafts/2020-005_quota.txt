# ARGUMENT

The Montana Supreme Court correctly held that Montana's courts may exercise specific personal jurisdiction over Ford because Ford's extensive contacts with Montana—including its marketing, sale, and servicing of the very model of vehicle involved in this crash—"relate to" Respondent's claims, even though the particular vehicle was not first sold in Montana.

## I. Specific Jurisdiction Requires Only That Claims "Arise Out of or Relate to" the Defendant's Forum Contacts, Not Strict Causation.

Due process requires that a defendant have "certain minimum contacts" with the forum such that maintaining suit there does not offend "traditional notions of fair play and substantial justice." *International Shoe Co. v. Washington*, 326 U.S. 310, 316 (1945). This Court has consistently phrased the specific-jurisdiction inquiry disjunctively: a court may exercise jurisdiction when the litigation "results from alleged injuries that 'arise out of or relate to'" the defendant's forum activities. *Helicopteros Nacionales de Colombia, S.A. v. Hall*, 466 U.S. 408, 414 (1984). The word "relate" is not surplusage; it captures connections between a defendant's forum conduct and a plaintiff's claim that fall short of but-for causation. Nothing in *Burger King Corp. v. Rudzewicz*, 471 U.S. 462 (1985), which asks whether the defendant "purposefully availed itself of the privilege of conducting activities within the forum State," *id.* at 475, imposes a strict causal requirement. Ford's proposed causation-only test would collapse the disjunctive "arise out of or relate to" standard into a single causation requirement, reading "relate to" out of this Court's precedent altogether.

## II. Ford Purposefully Availed Itself of the Privilege of Doing Business in Montana in a Manner That Relates Directly to Respondent's Claims.

Ford does not merely tolerate sales that happen to occur in Montana—it deliberately cultivates a market there. As in *World-Wide Volkswagen Corp. v. Woodson*, 444 U.S. 286 (1980), the touchstone is whether the defendant "delivers its products into the stream of commerce with the expectation that they will be purchased by consumers in the forum State." *Id.* at 297-98. Ford advertises, sells, and services Explorers—the very model involved here—throughout Montana. It trains and certifies Montana dealerships to repair and maintain that model, including its tires and suspension systems. Ford thus purposefully avails itself of the Montana market for Explorers generally, and Respondent's claims—arising from an alleged defect in that same model—relate directly to that purposeful forum conduct. As *Burger King* explained, a defendant who "purposefully directs" its activities toward forum residents "may not defeat jurisdiction" merely because the precise transaction giving rise to suit occurred elsewhere. 471 U.S. at 476. Ford's own conduct in Montana—not the fortuity of a prior owner's relocation—supplies the relevant connection.

## III. A Rigid Causation Requirement Would Produce Arbitrary Results Inconsistent With Fair Play and Substantial Justice.

This Court has never required that a plaintiff's injury be causally traceable to the specific unit of product sold in the forum. In *Keeton v. Hustler Magazine, Inc.*, 465 U.S. 770 (1984), the Court upheld jurisdiction over a libel claim in a forum where the defendant circulated magazines, even though the plaintiff's own contacts with the forum were minimal, because the defendant's "regular circulation of magazines in the forum State is sufficient to support" jurisdiction over claims relating to that conduct. *Id.* at 774. Justice Brennan's concurrence in *Asahi Metal Industry Co. v. Superior Court*, 480 U.S. 102 (1987), likewise recognized that a defendant who places a product into the "stream of commerce," with knowledge that it will be sold nationally, cannot escape jurisdiction simply by pointing to the vagaries of resale. *Id.* at 117 (Brennan, J., concurring in part). Ford's rule would allow it to escape accountability in every State except the two where a vehicle was designed or first sold, no matter how aggressively Ford has cultivated that State's market for the same model. Such a rule elevates arbitrary formalism over the "fair play and substantial justice" that *International Shoe* demands. 326 U.S. at 316.

## IV. Bristol-Myers Squibb Confirms That Relatedness—Not Strict Causation—Governs, and Does Not Control This Case.

Ford's reliance on *Bristol-Myers Squibb Co. v. Superior Court*, 582 U.S. 255 (2017), is misplaced. There, the Court held that California lacked jurisdiction over claims by nonresident plaintiffs who were not prescribed, and did not purchase, the drug in California. *Id.* at 264-65. The defect was the absence of any forum connection to those particular plaintiffs' claims—not a demand for strict causation. Here, by contrast, Respondent is a Montana resident injured by a Ford product in Montana, and Ford's Montana marketing and servicing of that exact model provide the required connection. *Bristol-Myers Squibb* expressly did not resolve "whether the 'arise out of or relate to' requirement" could be satisfied by conduct short of causation, leaving that question open. *Id.* at 265.

## V. General-Jurisdiction Precedent Is Inapposite.

Ford's reliance on the general-jurisdiction framework of *Daimler AG v. Bauman*, 571 U.S. 117 (2014), and *Goodyear Dunlop Tires Operations, S.A. v. Brown*, 564 U.S. 915 (2011), is misdirected. Those cases addressed when a corporation is subject to suit on *any* claim, holding jurisdiction proper only where the corporation is "at home." *Daimler*, 571 U.S. at 137. Respondent invokes specific, not general, jurisdiction, governed by the "arise out of or relate to" standard of *Helicopteros* and *Burger King*, which the Montana Supreme Court correctly applied.

The judgment below should be affirmed.
