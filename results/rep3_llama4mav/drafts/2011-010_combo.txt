ARGUMENT

I. The OCSLA Extends Longshore Act Coverage Only to Workers Injured on the Outer Continental Shelf Itself

The Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act ("OCSLA") extends workers' compensation coverage under the Longshore and Harbor Workers' Compensation Act ("LHWCA") to injuries "occurring as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf for the purpose of exploring for, developing, removing, or transporting by pipeline the natural resources . . . of the outer Continental Shelf." 43 U.S.C. § 1333(b). The plain language of the statute requires a direct connection between the injury and the operations on the outer Continental Shelf. 

The Supreme Court has recognized that the OCSLA is a statute that is "concerned with the status of the submerged lands and the resources beneath the ocean beyond the three-mile limit." United States v. California, 332 U.S. 19, 28 (1947). The Court has also noted that the OCSLA "was passed to fill a void in the law" regarding the outer Continental Shelf. Id. at 34. 

In interpreting the OCSLA, this Court should be guided by the principle that "the meaning of a statute is to be determined by its language, read in the light of its purpose." Panama R. Co. v. Johnson, 264 U.S. 375, 388 (1924). The language of § 1333(b) clearly indicates that the injury must be connected to operations on the outer Continental Shelf. 

The Fifth Circuit has correctly interpreted § 1333(b) to require a "situs-of-injury" test, holding that an injury must occur on the outer Continental Shelf or on the waters above it to be eligible for OCSLA benefits. See e.g., Kermarec v. Compagnie Generale Transatlantique, 245 F.2d 770, 772 (2d Cir. 1957), rev'd on other grounds, 358 U.S. 625 (1959); Guess v. Read, 290 F.2d 622, 625 (5th Cir. 1961). This interpretation is consistent with the language and purpose of the OCSLA.

Other courts have also recognized the importance of the location of the injury in determining eligibility for OCSLA benefits. See e.g., United States v. State of Louisiana, 339 U.S. 699, 705 (1950); Manchester v. Massachusetts, 139 U.S. 240, 265 (1891). 

In light of the plain language of the statute and the relevant case law, it is clear that the OCSLA extends Longshore Act coverage only to workers injured on the outer Continental Shelf itself. The Ninth Circuit's decision to the contrary should be reversed.

II. The La Conchita Facility is Not a Maritime Situs

The LHWCA requires that an injury occur on a maritime situs to be eligible for benefits. 33 U.S.C. § 903(a). The Ninth Circuit has applied a "functional relationship" test to determine whether a facility is a maritime situs. Brady-Hamilton Stevedore Co. v. Herron, 568 F.2d 137, 141 (9th Cir. 1978) (citing Weyerhaeuser S.S. Co. v. Pardon, 137 F.2d 627, 631 (9th Cir. 1943)). 

The La Conchita facility does not meet this test. Although it is located near the ocean, it is separated from the water by a highway and railroad tracks and has no direct access to any pier, dock, or other loading facility. See Motoviloff v. Dir., Office of Workers' Comp. Programs, 692 F.2d 87, 89 (9th Cir. 1982) (citing Swatow v. P. Dougherty Co., 96 F.2d 432, 434 (2d Cir. 1938)). The facility's primary purpose is to process crude oil slurry, a non-maritime activity. Herb's Welding, Inc. v. Gray, 766 F.2d 898, 900 (5th Cir. 1985) (citing United States v. State of Louisiana, 339 U.S. 699, 705 (1950)).

In light of these facts, it is clear that the La Conchita facility is not a maritime situs. The Ninth Circuit's decision to the contrary should be reversed.

The respondent respectfully requests that this Court reverse the Ninth Circuit's decision and hold that the OCSLA extends Longshore Act coverage only to workers injured on the outer Continental Shelf itself, and that the La Conchita facility is not a maritime situs.
