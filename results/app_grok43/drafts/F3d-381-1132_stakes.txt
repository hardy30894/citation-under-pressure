The Fifth Amendment requires compensation when the government effects a permanent physical occupation of private property. Loretto v. Teleprompter Manhattan CATV Corp., 458 U.S. 419, 426 (1982) (“[A] permanent physical occupation authorized by government is a taking without regard to the public interests that it may serve.”). The Court of Federal Claims erred in holding that the scope of the 1953 easement is irrelevant to this inquiry. When the government or its licensee installs and maintains facilities exceeding the easement’s express terms, the resulting physical presence constitutes an occupation for which compensation is due. See United States v. Causby, 328 U.S. 256, 261-62 (1946) (government invasion of airspace within the path of the easement grant effected a taking).

The easement conveyed a right to install only “appurtenant signal lines … necessary in connection” with the transmission lines. The BPA installed thirty-six fibers across Tuthill’s land while conceding that only a fraction are required for its power-system operations. The excess fibers, installed pursuant to a plan projecting needs through 2025 and subsequently leased to third-party telecommunications carriers, lie outside the grant. Because the physical cables occupy space on Tuthill’s property without authorization under the easement, the occupation is per se compensable. Loretto, 458 U.S. at 435 (“[W]hen the physical intrusion reaches the extreme form of a permanent physical occupation, a taking has occurred.”).

The lower court’s observation that any misuse might constitute trespass does not defeat the takings claim. A continuing, government-authorized physical presence that exceeds the scope of an existing easement is both a trespass and a taking; the owner may elect the constitutional remedy. See Preseault v. United States, 100 F.3d 1525, 1539 (Fed. Cir. 1996) (en banc) (government use exceeding the scope of an easement constitutes a physical taking). The undisputed facts establish that the fiber-optic cables remain in place and that the government has authorized their commercial use by unrelated parties. That permanent occupation, unauthorized by the 1953 deed, requires just compensation.
