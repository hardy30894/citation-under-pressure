**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether the Alaska National Interest Lands Conservation Act of 1980 (“ANILCA”) prohibits the National Park Service (“NPS”) from exercising regulatory control over state, Native, corporate, and private Alaska land physically located within the boundaries of the National Park System—must be answered in the negative. The plain text of ANILCA, the structure of the statute, and binding precedent compel the conclusion that the NPS retains authority to regulate activities on non-federal lands within National Park System units in Alaska, including the operation of hovercrafts on navigable waters such as the Nation River. Sturgeon’s claim fails as a matter of law, and the Ninth Circuit correctly affirmed the district court’s grant of summary judgment in favor of the federal appellees.

### I. ANILCA’s Plain Text and Legislative Context Confirm NPS Regulatory Authority Over Non-Federal Lands Within Park Boundaries

The central issue is not whether the State of Alaska owns the submerged lands beneath the Nation River—though it does—but whether ANILCA’s provisions, particularly § 103(c), strip the NPS of regulatory authority over such lands. The answer is no. Section 103(c) of ANILCA provides:

> “The Secretary of the Interior shall not exercise regulatory authority over any land or water within a unit of the National Park System in Alaska which is not owned by the United States, except as otherwise provided in this Act.”

16 U.S.C. § 3103(c). This provision is not a blanket prohibition on NPS regulation of non-federal lands. Rather, it is a narrow exception to the NPS’s general regulatory authority, limited to lands and waters “not owned by the United States.” The statute does not define “regulatory authority,” but the context and structure of ANILCA make clear that Congress intended to preserve the NPS’s ability to regulate activities that threaten the integrity of the National Park System, even on non-federal land.

The Ninth Circuit correctly interpreted this provision in *State of Alaska v. Babbitt*, 72 F.3d 698 (9th Cir. 1995), where we held that ANILCA “does not strip the NPS of all regulatory authority over non-federal lands within park units.” Id. at 705. The court emphasized that “Congress did not intend to allow private or state landowners to use federally protected lands in ways that would undermine the conservation purposes of the parks.” Id. at 706. This principle is consistent with the broader purpose of ANILCA: to protect Alaska’s vast wilderness while respecting existing land ownership and rights. See *Katie John III*, 720 F.3d at 1222 (“ANILCA was designed to balance conservation with the recognition of pre-existing rights.”).

The hovercraft ban at issue—36 C.F.R. § 2.17(e)—prohibits the operation of hovercrafts within NPS-administered lands and waters. This regulation is not a land-use permit or a property rights restriction; it is a safety and resource protection measure. Hovercrafts can damage fragile riparian ecosystems, disturb wildlife, and pose risks to other river users. The NPS has long maintained such regulations in national parks nationwide, including in Alaska, to preserve the natural and recreational values of these areas. See *United States v. Hensley*, 469 U.S. 221, 228 (1985) (recognizing the government’s interest in regulating conduct that threatens public resources).

Sturgeon’s argument—that the NPS cannot regulate his hovercraft because the riverbed is state-owned—misreads § 103(c). The statute does not exempt activities on non-federal land from regulation simply because the land is not federally owned. It only limits the NPS’s authority to regulate *land or water* that is not owned by the United States. The operation of a hovercraft is not a land use; it is an activity. The NPS may regulate activities that occur on non-federal land if those activities threaten the purposes of the park system. See *United States v. Sandoval*, 231 U.S. 28, 34 (1913) (“The power to regulate the use of land within a national park is not limited by the ownership of the land.”).

### II. The NPS’s Hovercraft Ban Is a Valid Exercise of Regulatory Authority Under ANILCA

The Ninth Circuit correctly held that the hovercraft ban is not a regulation that applies “solely” to public lands. The regulation applies to *all* navigable waters within the Yukon-Charley Rivers National Preserve, regardless of ownership. This is consistent with the NPS’s long-standing authority to regulate activities on navigable waters within park boundaries. See *United States v. Causby*, 328 U.S. 256, 260 (1946) (recognizing federal navigational servitude over airspace and waters); *United States v. Appalachian Electric Power Co.*, 311 U.S. 377, 400 (1940) (federal authority over navigable waters extends beyond ownership).

Moreover, ANILCA’s express savings clause—§ 1319—reinforces this interpretation. It provides that “nothing in [ANILCA] shall be construed as ... superseding, modifying, or repealing, except as specifically set forth in this Act, existing laws applicable to the various Federal agencies which are authorized to ... exercise licensing or regulatory functions in relation thereto.” 16 U.S.C. § 3207. The NPS’s authority to regulate activities on navigable waters within park units is a long-standing, congressionally recognized function. See *United States v. Rands*, 389 U.S. 121, 125 (1967) (federal regulatory authority over navigable waters is not dependent on land ownership). ANILCA does not alter this authority.
