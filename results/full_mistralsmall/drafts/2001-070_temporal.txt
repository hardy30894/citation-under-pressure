**ARGUMENT**

The Eighth Amendment’s prohibition of “cruel and unusual punishments” does not bar the execution of a mentally retarded offender. Penry v. Lynaugh, 492 U.S. 302, 335 (1989) (plurality opinion) (citing Gregg v. Georgia, 428 U.S. 153, 173 (1976)). The Court has repeatedly recognized that the Cruel and Unusual Punishments Clause “draws its meaning from the evolving standards of decency that mark the progress of a maturing society.” Trop v. Dulles, 356 U.S. 86, 101 (1958) (plurality opinion). Yet those evolving standards have never been held to forbid the execution of the mentally retarded. To the contrary, the Court has long permitted the execution of offenders whose culpability is diminished by mental impairment short of insanity. See, e.g., Solesbee v. Balkcom, 339 U.S. 9, 12 (1950) (rejecting Eighth Amendment challenge to execution of offender who had suffered a “mental breakdown”); Louisiana ex rel. Francis v. Resweber, 329 U.S. 459, 464 (1947) (upholding electrocution of offender who survived first attempt); cf. Powell v. Texas, 392 U.S. 514, 535–36 (1968) (plurality opinion) (recognizing that the Eighth Amendment permits consideration of mental impairment in assessing culpability but does not categorically bar punishment).

The Court’s precedents establish that the Cruel and Unusual Punishments Clause “is not fastened to the obsolete.” Trop, 356 U.S. at 100. But they also confirm that the Clause does not “draw[ ] a bright line” that automatically removes entire categories of offenders from the reach of the death penalty. Gregg, 428 U.S. at 185. Instead, the Court has required “clear and reliable evidence” of a national consensus before recognizing a categorical exemption. Id. at 173–74. No such consensus exists with respect to the mentally retarded. To the contrary, the available evidence demonstrates that a substantial majority of jurisdictions—including those that have recently revisited the issue—permit the execution of mentally retarded offenders. See Penry, 492 U.S. at 334–35 (plurality opinion) (citing statutes and case law from 24 states and the District of Columbia that permitted execution of the mentally retarded).

The Court has also emphasized that the Eighth Amendment “must draw its meaning from the evolving standards of decency that mark the progress of a maturing society.” Trop, 356 U.S. at 101. But those standards are not measured by “the subjective views of individual jurors or judges,” but rather by “objective factors to which we traditionally look.” Gregg, 428 U.S. at 181. Among those factors, the Court has consistently accorded “serious weight” to the actions of state legislatures. Id. at 175. Here, the legislative judgment is clear: the vast majority of states have not categorically exempted the mentally retarded from the death penalty. See Penry, 492 U.S. at 334–35 (plurality opinion) (citing statutes from 24 states and the District of Columbia that permitted execution of the mentally retarded). That legislative judgment reflects a societal consensus that the mentally retarded, while less culpable than other offenders, may still be held accountable for their crimes. See Solesbee, 339 U.S. at 12 (upholding execution of offender who had suffered a “mental breakdown”); cf. Powell, 392 U.S. at 535–36 (plurality opinion) (recognizing that the Eighth Amendment permits consideration of mental impairment in assessing culpability but does not categorically bar punishment).

Finally, the Court has repeatedly recognized that the Eighth Amendment does not forbid the execution of offenders whose culpability is diminished by mental impairment short of insanity. See, e.g., Solesbee, 339 U.S. at 12; Resweber, 329 U.S. at 464. The fact that a defendant may be less able to conform his conduct to the requirements of the law does not render his execution “cruel and unusual.” See Powell, 392 U.S. at 535–36 (plurality opinion). Instead, the Eighth Amendment permits the sentencer to consider such evidence in mitigation, but does not categorically bar punishment. See id. at 535–36. The same principle applies here: the fact that Atkins may be mentally retarded does not render his execution cruel and unusual punishment.
