The Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act extends Longshore and Harbor Workers’ Compensation Act coverage only to injuries occurring on the outer Continental Shelf. Section 1333(b) provides benefits for “disability or death of an employee resulting from any injury occurring as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf.” 43 U.S.C. § 1333(b). The statute’s text ties compensability to both the location of the operations and the location of the injury.

The Supreme Court has recognized that OCSLA coverage is “determined principally by locale, not by the status of the individual injured or killed.” Offshore Logistics, Inc. v. Tallentire, 477 U.S. 207, 219 (1986). In the same opinion the Court observed that “§ 1333(b) makes compensation for the death or injury of an ‘employee’ resulting from certain operations on the Outer Continental Shelf payable under the Longshoremen’s and Harbor Workers’ Compensation Act,” thereby superimposing a status requirement on “the otherwise determinative OCSLA situs requirement.” Id. at 219 n.2. That statement, though dictum, reflects the Court’s considered view that the statute imposes a geographic limit on the injury itself.

The Fifth Circuit has given effect to this limitation. In Mills v. Director, Office of Workers’ Compensation Programs, 877 F.2d 356 (5th Cir. 1989) (en banc), the court held that an employee injured onshore while constructing a platform destined for the Shelf was not covered, because “the injury must occur on an OCS platform or on the waters above the OCS.” Id. at 362. The court required both a “but for” causal connection and the statutory situs. See also Becker v. Tidewater, Inc., 586 F.3d 358, 366-67 (5th Cir. 2009); Pickett v. Petroleum Helicopters, Inc., 266 F.3d 366, 368 (5th Cir. 2001).

By contrast, the Third Circuit’s “but for” test in Curtis v. Schlumberger Offshore Service, Inc., 849 F.2d 805 (3d Cir. 1988), reads the situs language out of the statute. That approach cannot be reconciled with the text or with Tallentire’s description of OCSLA as a locale-based statute.

The Ninth Circuit’s “substantial nexus” formulation likewise departs from the statute. It substitutes a judicially created standard for the geographic boundary Congress enacted. The statute does not speak of “operations that affect” the Shelf or injuries that bear a “nexus” to Shelf activities; it requires that the injury occur “as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf.” 43 U.S.C. § 1333(b). Where Congress has drawn an explicit geographic line, courts must respect it. See Herb’s Welding, Inc. v. Gray, 470 U.S. 414, 422-24 (1985) (emphasizing the “explicit geographic limitation” in OCSLA’s incorporation of the LHWCA).

Petitioner’s claim therefore fails because the fatal injury occurred at an onshore facility in California, not on the Shelf. The judgment of the Benefits Review Board should be affirmed.
