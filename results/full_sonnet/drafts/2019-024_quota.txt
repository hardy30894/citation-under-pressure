# ARGUMENT

## I. THE PLAIN TEXT OF SECTION 1113(2) REQUIRES SUBJECTIVE AWARENESS, WHICH DISCLOSURE ALONE CAN ESTABLISH

Section 1113(2) bars suits brought "three years after the earliest date on which the plaintiff had actual knowledge of the breach or violation." 29 U.S.C. § 1113(2). This Court has consistently instructed that statutory interpretation begins with the ordinary meaning of the text. *Rotkiske v. Klemm*, 139 S. Ct. 1442, 1446 (2019). "Actual knowledge" is most naturally understood in contradistinction to "constructive knowledge"—a distinction Congress itself drew when it enacted the original version of § 1113, which contained both an actual-knowledge provision and a separate constructive-knowledge provision triggered by the filing of certain reports. *See* Pub. L. No. 93-406, § 413(a)(2)(B), 88 Stat. 829, 892 (1974). Congress's 1987 repeal of the constructive-knowledge trigger confirms that Congress intended the actual-knowledge standard to remain distinct from a standard based on availability or constructive notice. Omnibus Budget Reconciliation Act of 1987, Pub. L. No. 100-203, § 9342(b), 101 Stat. 1330, 1330-378.

Petitioner does not contend that "actual knowledge" is satisfied by mere availability of information without regard to whether a plaintiff actually possessed it. Rather, the undisputed evidentiary record here—including detailed Fund Fact Sheets, a Qualified Default Investment Alternative Notice, a Summary Plan Description, and Annual Disclosures explaining Intel's alternative-investment strategy—demonstrates that a plaintiff who received, accessed, and had every opportunity to review such disclosures has "actual knowledge" of their contents as a matter of law, regardless of subsequent claims of forgetfulness. This Court has long recognized that knowledge may be established through undisputed documentary evidence where the plaintiff cannot create a triable issue merely by asserting lack of memory. *See Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, Inc.*, 477 U.S. 242, 249-50 (1986) (a party cannot defeat summary judgment with a "mere . . . scintilla" of contrary evidence).

## II. THE NINTH CIRCUIT'S RULE—REQUIRING PROOF THAT A PLAINTIFF SUBJECTIVELY RECALLS READING DISCLOSURES—CONFLICTS WITH THIS COURT'S FIDUCIARY-DUTY FRAMEWORK AND EFFECTIVELY REWRITES THE STATUTE

ERISA imposes on fiduciaries "a duty of care with respect to the management of existing trust funds, along with liability for breach of that duty." *Lockheed Corp. v. Spink*, 517 U.S. 882, 887 (1996). Congress paired that duty with a carefully calibrated limitations scheme, including the six-year repose period and the shorter three-year actual-knowledge period, to balance participant protection against the "fiduciary's . . . interest in relying on settled expectations." *See Tibble v. Edison Int'l*, 135 S. Ct. 1823, 1828 (2015) (recognizing ERISA's limitations framework draws on established trust-law principles).

The Ninth Circuit's holding—that a plaintiff must be shown to have subjectively remembered reading and comprehending disclosed information—collapses the actual-knowledge standard into something closer to the repealed constructive-knowledge standard's opposite: a rule that disclosure, however complete, is *never* sufficient absent proof of a plaintiff's contemporaneous subjective recollection. That approach cannot be reconciled with this Court's admonition that courts must give effect to every provision of a statute and avoid interpretations that render statutory choices meaningless. *TRW Inc. v. Andrews*, 534 U.S. 19, 31 (2001) (courts should not construe a statute in a way that leaves one of its provisions with no consequence). Just as this Court held in *TRW* that Congress's deliberate choice between discovery-based and occurrence-based limitations triggers must be respected according to its terms, so too must § 1113(2)'s actual-knowledge trigger be given operative effect where fiduciaries have complied with ERISA's disclosure obligations.

Moreover, permitting a plaintiff's bare assertion of non-recollection to defeat documentary proof of disclosure invites exactly the evidentiary quagmire this Court has cautioned against in other limitations contexts. In *United States v. Kubrick*, 444 U.S. 111, 117 (1979), the Court emphasized that limitations periods are designed to require plaintiffs "to make an inquiry" once armed with the facts undergirding a claim, precisely to avoid the endless factual disputes that would result from making limitations turn on unverifiable claims of forgotten knowledge. The Ninth Circuit's rule invites litigation over what a plaintiff subjectively remembers years later—an inherently unreliable and easily manipulated inquiry that undermines the certainty § 1113(2) was designed to provide.

## III. THE NINTH CIRCUIT'S OWN PRECEDENT SUPPORTS A RULE THAT KNOWLEDGE OF THE DISCLOSED TRANSACTION SUFFICES

Even under the Ninth Circuit's own case law, actual knowledge of the "transaction that constituted the alleged violation" has been sufficient to trigger the limitations period, without requiring proof that the plaintiff appreciated the transaction's illegality. *Blanton v. Anzalone*, 760 F.2d 989, 991-92 (9th Cir. 1985). In *Blanton*, the court held that the statute "is triggered by the [defendant's] knowledge of the transaction that constituted the alleged violation, not by their knowledge of the law." *Id.* at 992. The court below did not overrule *Blanton*, *Meagher v. International Ass'n of Machinists & Aerospace Workers Pension Plan*, 856 F.2d 1418 (9th Cir. 1988), or *Ziegler v. Connecticut General Life Insurance Co.*, 916 F.2d 548 (9th Cir. 1990)—each of which permitted actual knowledge to be established through evidence that the plaintiff received or participated in disclosure of the underlying transaction. Instead, the panel manufactured a new requirement demanding proof of subjective recollection, a requirement nowhere grounded in the statutory text or in this Court's precedents governing statutes of limitations. *See Rotkiske*, 139 S. Ct. at 1447 (declining to adopt a "discovery rule" not supported by statutory text).

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Ninth Circuit should be reversed.
