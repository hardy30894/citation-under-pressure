**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE ARKANSAS TERM LIMITATION AMENDMENT DOES NOT IMPERMISSIBLY ALTER THE CONSTITUTIONALLY ENUMERATED QUALIFICATIONS FOR CONGRESS.**

The Arkansas Term Limitation Amendment does not add or alter any qualification for service in Congress. Instead, it regulates ballot access—a permissible exercise of state authority under the Elections Clause. *See U.S. Term Limits, Inc. v. Thornton*, 514 U.S. 779, 804 (1995) (plurality opinion) ("The Constitution does not preclude the States from adopting reasonable regulations governing the qualifications of candidates for national office."). The Arkansas Supreme Court erred in invalidating Amendment 73 on the ground that it impermissibly altered the qualifications for federal office.

The Constitution sets forth the exclusive qualifications for congressional service in Article I, Sections 2 and 3. *Powell v. McCormack*, 395 U.S. 486, 522 (1969) ("The Constitution leaves the House broad discretion to determine the qualifications of its members."). However, the Elections Clause grants the states authority to regulate the "Times, Places and Manner of holding Elections for Senators and Representatives." U.S. Const. art. I, § 4, cl. 1. This authority includes the power to impose ballot-access restrictions, provided they are reasonable and nondiscriminatory. *Storer v. Brown*, 415 U.S. 724, 730 (1974) ("States have evolved comprehensive, and sometimes complex, election codes regulating in most substantial ways, with respect to both federal and state elections, the time, place, and manner of holding primary and general elections.").

Arkansas’s Amendment 73 does not alter the constitutional qualifications for congressional service—age, citizenship, and residency—but merely conditions ballot access on prior service. This is a permissible regulation of the "Manner" of elections under the Elections Clause. *See Burdick v. Takushi*, 504 U.S. 428, 433 (1992) ("States have a ‘strong interest’ in regulating the election process."). The Arkansas Supreme Court’s contrary conclusion misapprehends the distinction between qualifications and ballot-access restrictions. *See Timmons v. Twin Cities Area New Party*, 520 U.S. 351, 358 (1997) ("States may impose reasonable, non-discriminatory ballot access requirements.").

**II. THE ARKANSAS SUPREME COURT ERRED IN HOLDING THAT AMENDMENT 73 VIOLATED THE U.S. CONSTITUTION.**

The Arkansas Supreme Court’s decision rests on a misreading of *Powell v. McCormack*, 395 U.S. 486 (1969), and *Thornton*, 514 U.S. 779. In *Powell*, the Court held that Congress could not exclude a duly elected member based on additional qualifications. 395 U.S. at 550. However, the Court expressly distinguished ballot-access restrictions, noting that states retain authority under the Elections Clause to regulate the manner of elections. *Id.* at 547 n.78 ("The States have the power to prescribe the qualifications of voters in federal elections, subject to the limitations of the Fifteenth, Nineteenth, Twenty-Fourth, and Twenty-Sixth Amendments.").

Similarly, in *Thornton*, the plurality opinion invalidated an Arkansas constitutional amendment imposing term limits on federal officeholders, but it did so on the ground that the amendment altered the qualifications for office—a power reserved to the federal government. 514 U.S. at 804. The Court did not hold that states lack authority to regulate ballot access. *Id.* at 804-05 ("The Framers decided that the qualifications of the members of Congress were to be fixed in the Constitution and could not be altered by the States.").

The Arkansas Supreme Court’s reliance on *Thornton* to invalidate Amendment 73 conflates qualifications with ballot access. The Amendment does not alter the constitutional qualifications for congressional service; it merely conditions ballot access on prior service. This is a permissible exercise of state authority under the Elections Clause.

**III. THE ARKANSAS SUPREME COURT ERRED IN HOLDING THAT AMENDMENT 73 WAS INVALID FOR LACK OF AN ENACTING CLAUSE.**

The Arkansas Supreme Court’s holding that Amendment 73 was invalid for lack of an Enacting Clause is unsupported by Arkansas law. Amendment 7 to the Arkansas Constitution requires that initiated acts include an Enacting Clause, but it does not apply to constitutional amendments. *See Ark. Const. amend. 7* ("The style of all the bills initiated and submitted under the provisions of this section shall be, ‘Be It Enacted by the People of the State of Arkansas.’"). The Arkansas Supreme Court’s contrary conclusion misreads the state constitution and disregards the plain text of Amendment 7.

**IV. THE ARKANSAS SUPREME COURT ERRED IN HOLDING THAT THE UNCONSTITUTIONAL PORTION OF AMENDMENT 73 COULD NOT BE SEVERED.**

The Arkansas Supreme Court’s holding that the unconstitutional portion of Amendment 73 could not be severed is unsupported by Arkansas law. The Arkansas Constitution includes a severability clause, and the Arkansas Supreme Court has held that severability clauses are enforceable unless the unconstitutional portion is "inextricably linked" to the constitutional portion. *See Combs v. Glenn Falls Ins. Co.*, 237 Ark. 745, 750 (1964) ("The test of the efficacy of a severability clause is whether the measure would have passed without the unconstitutional portion.").

The Arkansas Term Limitation Amendment includes a severability clause, and the unconstitutional portion—Section 3—is not inextricably linked to the constitutional portions—Sections 1 and 2. The Arkansas Supreme Court erred in holding otherwise.
