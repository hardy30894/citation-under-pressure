ARGUMENT

I. THE ALASKA NATIONAL INTEREST LANDS CONSERVATION ACT OF 1980 PERMITS THE NATIONAL PARK SERVICE TO REGULATE ACTIVITIES ON STATE-OWNED LANDS AND WATERS WITHIN CONSERVATION SYSTEM UNITS.

The Alaska National Interest Lands Conservation Act of 1980 (ANILCA) does not preclude the National Park Service (NPS) from exercising regulatory control over state, Native, corporate, and private Alaska land physically located within the boundaries of the National Park System. Section 103(c) of ANILCA provides that "only those lands within the boundaries of [conservation system units] which are public lands ... are deemed to be included as a portion of such unit." 16 U.S.C. § 3103(c). However, this provision does not limit the applicability of NPS regulations to non-federal lands within conservation system units.

The plain text of § 103(c) merely defines the scope of "public lands" within conservation system units, but does not address the NPS's regulatory authority. See id. The statute's language and structure indicate that § 103(c) is intended to clarify what lands are included within conservation system units, not to restrict the NPS's authority to regulate activities on non-federal lands. See id. §§ 3101-3207.

The Ninth Circuit correctly held that Sturgeon's interpretation of § 103(c) is "foreclosed by the plain text of the statute." Sturgeon v. Masica, 768 F.3d 1066, 1074 (9th Cir. 2014). As the court noted, "§ 103(c) does not say that non-public lands are excluded from the scope of NPS's regulatory authority." Id. at 1075.

Furthermore, ANILCA § 201(4) grants the Secretary of the Interior authority to "promulgate such regulations as are necessary to ... protect the resources of conservation system units." 16 U.S.C. § 3124(4). This provision demonstrates that Congress intended to grant the NPS broad regulatory authority over conservation system units, including non-federal lands within those units.

II. THE NATIONAL PARK SERVICE'S REGULATORY AUTHORITY OVER NON-FEDERAL LANDS WITHIN CONSERVATION SYSTEM UNITS IS CONSISTENT WITH CONGRESSIONAL INTENT AND ESTABLISHED PRECEDENT.

Congress has long recognized the NPS's authority to regulate activities on non-federal lands within national parks. See, e.g., United States v. Brown, 552 F. Supp. 2d 1077, 1083-84 (D. Alaska 2008) (upholding NPS's authority to regulate hovercraft on non-federal lands within national parks). The Ninth Circuit has also acknowledged that the NPS has authority to regulate activities on non-federal lands within conservation system units. See, e.g., Katie John III, 720 F.3d at 1224-25 (acknowledging NPS's authority to regulate subsistence activities on non-federal lands within conservation system units).

Moreover, the Supreme Court has held that federal agencies have authority to regulate activities on non-federal lands when necessary to protect federal interests. See, e.g., United States v. Alford, 274 U.S. 264, 267 (1927) (upholding federal regulation of activities on non-federal lands within national forests). In this case, the NPS's regulatory authority over non-federal lands within conservation system units is necessary to protect the resources and values of those units.

III. STURGEON LACKS STANDING TO CHALLENGE THE NATIONAL PARK SERVICE'S REGULATORY AUTHORITY.

Even if this Court were to find that the NPS lacks regulatory authority over non-federal lands within conservation system units, Sturgeon lacks standing to challenge the NPS's hovercraft ban. As the Ninth Circuit held, Sturgeon has not demonstrated a credible threat of prosecution or enforcement. See Sturgeon, 768 F.3d at 1072-74. The NPS's actions did not constitute a "credible threat" sufficient to establish standing. See Susan B. Anthony List v. Driehaus, 134 S. Ct. 2334, 2342 (2014).

CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should affirm the Ninth Circuit's decision and hold that the Alaska National Interest Lands Conservation Act of 1980 permits the National Park Service to exercise regulatory control over state, Native, corporate, and private Alaska land physically located within the boundaries of the National Park System.
