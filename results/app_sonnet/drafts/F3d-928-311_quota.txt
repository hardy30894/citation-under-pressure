**ARGUMENT**

**I. Standard of Review and the Categorical Approach**

This Court reviews de novo whether a prior conviction qualifies as an ACCA predicate or career-offender predicate. *United States v. King*, 673 F.3d 274, 278 (4th Cir. 2012). Both inquiries are governed by the "categorical approach," under which a court examines "only the statutory definitions—i.e., the elements—of a defendant's prior offenses, and not the particular facts underlying those convictions." *Descamps v. United States*, 570 U.S. 254, 261 (2013). The court compares "the elements of the crime of conviction" with "the elements of the generic offense—i.e., the offense as commonly understood." *Mathis v. United States*, 136 S. Ct. 2243, 2248 (2016). If the state statute "sweeps more broadly than the generic crime," a conviction under it cannot count as a predicate, "even if the defendant actually committed the offense in its generic form." *Descamps*, 570 U.S. at 261. Where the record permits resort to underlying documents at all, that resort is strictly limited to "the charging document, plea agreement, transcript of the plea colloquy, or comparable judicial record." *Shepard v. United States*, 544 U.S. 13, 26 (2005). Applying this framework, neither of Furlow's challenged convictions qualifies.

**II. Furlow's Georgia First-Degree Arson Conviction Is Not a Categorical Match for "Arson" Under the ACCA or the Career Offender Provision**

The ACCA and the Guidelines each list "arson" as an enumerated predicate, without further definition. 18 U.S.C. § 924(e)(2)(B)(ii); USSG § 4B1.2(a)(2). Under *Taylor v. United States*, 495 U.S. 575 (1990), where Congress uses an undefined common-law term to identify a predicate offense, courts must ask "what offenses were included as [that crime] in the criminal codes of most States" at the relevant time, and compare that generic offense to the elements of the state statute of conviction. *Id.* at 598–99. Generic arson, as understood at common law and in the majority of jurisdictions, requires the malicious burning of a dwelling or other specified property belonging to another, or property burned under circumstances endangering others—not the mere burning of any structure regardless of ownership or motive.

Georgia's first-degree arson statute, O.C.G.A. § 16-7-60, sweeps far beyond this generic core. It criminalizes the burning of property "of another" *and* the burning of one's own property when, for example, the property is insured and the defendant acts "with intent to defraud the insurer." O.C.G.A. § 16-7-60(a)(4). That provision does not require that the fire endanger any person, that the property belong to anyone but the defendant, or that the conduct resemble the malicious, dangerous burning the generic offense targets—an insurance-fraud burning of one's own vacant structure suffices. Because a defendant can be convicted of Georgia first-degree arson without committing anything resembling generic arson, the statute is categorically broader than the enumerated offense, and a conviction under it cannot serve as an ACCA or career-offender predicate. See *Mathis*, 136 S. Ct. at 2251 (a statute that "criminalize[s] a broader swath of conduct than the relevant generic offense" cannot support the enhancement "even if the defendant's actual conduct" would satisfy the generic definition). Because Georgia's statute is indivisible as to this broader theory of liability—the fraud-based burning is simply an alternative means of committing the single offense of first-degree arson—the modified categorical approach affords no escape. *Descamps*, 570 U.S. at 278. The district court therefore erred in treating this conviction as a qualifying predicate.

**III. Furlow's South Carolina Crack-Cocaine Distribution Conviction Does Not Satisfy the "Serious Drug Offense" and "Controlled Substance Offense" Definitions**

The ACCA and Guidelines definitions require that the predicate carry a prescribed maximum term of imprisonment of ten years or more, or be "punishable by imprisonment for a term exceeding one year." 18 U.S.C. § 924(e)(2)(A)(ii); USSG § 4B1.2(b). This Court has cautioned that the relevant "maximum term of imprisonment" for South Carolina offenses must be determined by examining South Carolina's own tiered sentencing structure as applied through the categorical lens, not simply the ceiling stated in the abstract. *United States v. Simmons*, 649 F.3d 237, 243–44 (4th Cir. 2011) (en banc). This Court has likewise recognized, in analyzing South Carolina drug offenses, that the elements—not the label—of the state crime control the categorical comparison. *United States v. Hemingway*, 734 F.3d 323, 331 (4th Cir. 2013).

South Carolina's distribution statute defines "distribute" to include a "transfer" of a controlled substance, S.C. Code Ann. § 44-53-370, without regard to remuneration. Because the federal and generic understanding of "distributing" a controlled substance is not limited by whether the transfer involves payment, Furlow acknowledges this is a closer question than the arson issue. But because the categorical inquiry must isolate the least culpable conduct criminalized by the statute, and because the record here does not permit resort beyond the statutory elements under *Shepard*, 544 U.S. at 26, this Court should resolve any doubt in Furlow's favor rather than assume the statute matches the generic, remuneration-neutral federal offense.

**CONCLUSION**

For these reasons, the sentence should be vacated and the case remanded for resentencing without the ACCA and career-offender enhancements.
