The issue presented is whether a state prisoner's procedurally defaulted claim of ineffective assistance of counsel can excuse the procedural default of another habeas corpus claim. The respondent argues that it cannot.

The Supreme Court has long held that a habeas petitioner must demonstrate cause and prejudice to overcome a procedural default. See Fay v. Noia, 372 U.S. 391, 438 (1963). In this case, Carpenter's sufficiency-of-the-evidence claim was procedurally defaulted due to his failure to raise it on direct appeal. The District Court and the Court of Appeals erred in holding that Carpenter's procedurally defaulted ineffective-assistance-of-counsel claim could serve as cause to excuse the default of the sufficiency claim.

The Supreme Court has established that a claim of ineffective assistance of counsel can constitute cause for a procedural default, but only if the ineffective-assistance claim is not itself procedurally defaulted. See Murray v. Carrier, is not available, however,  Johnson v. Zerbst, 304 U.S. 458 (1938) and  Glasser v. United States, 315 U.S. 60 (1942) provide guidance. In Johnson, the Court held that the absence of effective assistance of counsel can be a valid ground for habeas relief. 304 U.S. at 467-68. Similarly, in Glasser, the Court emphasized the importance of effective assistance of counsel in ensuring a fair trial. 315 U.S. at 76. These cases suggest that a valid claim of ineffective assistance of counsel can be a legitimate basis for cause.

However, when the ineffective-assistance-of-counsel claim is itself procedurally defaulted, it cannot serve as cause to excuse another default. Allowing a procedurally defaulted ineffective-assistance claim to excuse another default would undermine the principles of procedural default and the interests of finality and comity. See Daniels v. Allen, 344 U.S. 443, 482-87 (1953) (emphasizing the importance of procedural default rules in state post-conviction proceedings).

In this case, Carpenter's ineffective-assistance-of-counsel claim was procedurally defaulted due to his failure to raise it in a timely manner on state post-conviction review. Therefore, it cannot serve as cause to excuse the procedural default of his sufficiency-of-the-evidence claim. The District Court and the Court of Appeals erred in holding otherwise.

The respondent respectfully submits that the Court should reverse the judgment of the Court of Appeals and hold that a procedurally defaulted claim of ineffective assistance of counsel cannot excuse the procedural default of another habeas corpus claim.
