**ARGUMENT**

**A Criminal Defendant Convicted Under a Valid Federal Statute Lacks Standing to Challenge That Conviction on Tenth Amendment Grounds.**

The Tenth Amendment reserves to the States, or to the people acting through the States, those powers not delegated to the United States. It does not create a personal right that a private litigant may invoke to escape a federal prosecution. Because petitioner asserts only the interests of the States and not any legally protected interest of her own, she lacks standing to raise this claim, and the Third Circuit correctly so held.

**A. Standing to raise a Tenth Amendment claim belongs to the States, not to private litigants asserting only their own liability.**

This Court has long insisted that a litigant may invoke the judicial power only to vindicate a legal interest of her own, not the constitutional prerogatives of a third party. In *Tennessee Electric Power Co. v. Tennessee Valley Authority*, 306 U.S. 118 (1939), private utility companies argued that a federal agency's competition with them violated the Tenth Amendment. This Court rejected the claim for want of standing, holding that the companies, "absent the states or their officers, have no standing in this suit to raise any question under the [Tenth] Amendment." *Id.* at 144. The logic of *Tennessee Electric* controls here: the Tenth Amendment protects the reserved sovereignty of the States as sovereign entities, and a private party's own exposure to regulation—whether economic or criminal—does not transform her into a proxy for that sovereign interest.

This Court reached the same conclusion in the companion cases reported as *Massachusetts v. Mellon* and *Frothingham v. Mellon*, 262 U.S. 447 (1923). There a State asserted that a federal spending statute invaded powers reserved to it, while a private taxpayer raised a parallel claim. This Court dismissed both suits, explaining that the asserted injury was one shared generally by "the public at large" and did not present the kind of "direct injury" required to invoke judicial power. 262 U.S. at 488. If a State itself may not sue to vindicate speculative federalism injuries absent a concrete stake, then a private defendant—who possesses no sovereign interest whatsoever—stands on still weaker footing.

The Court's decisions in *Alabama Power Co. v. Ickes*, 302 U.S. 464 (1937), and *Perkins v. Lukens Steel Co.*, 310 U.S. 113 (1940), confirm the principle. In each, private parties objecting to the scope of federal executive or legislative action were denied standing because they could point to no legally protected interest distinct from a generalized grievance about the proper allocation of governmental power. As the Court put it in *Perkins*, the fact that a party is regulated, or made subject to conditions, by federal action does not itself supply "the kind of injury" needed to litigate the constitutional limits of federal authority. 310 U.S. at 125. Petitioner's position is identical: her only "interest" is that she was prosecuted, not that any power reserved to Pennsylvania has been usurped to her detriment in any way different from any other prosecuted person.

**B. The structural character of the Tenth Amendment confirms that its enforcement belongs to the States as such.**

This Court's treatment of federalism disputes as suits between sovereigns, rather than as individual rights litigation, reinforces the standing bar. In *Coleman v. Miller*, 307 U.S. 433 (1939), the Court examined which parties possess a sufficient interest to litigate questions touching the constitutional distribution of governmental authority, and confined that interest to those with an official, institutional stake in the outcome. *Id.* at 438. Similarly, in *Ashwander v. Tennessee Valley Authority*, 297 U.S. 288 (1936), the Court cautioned against adjudicating sweeping constitutional questions about the allocation of power between governments at the instance of parties whose own rights were not directly at stake. 297 U.S. at 341, 347 (Brandeis, J., concurring). Petitioner's Tenth Amendment claim is precisely such a structural claim: it does not assert that the statute deprives her of due process, notice, or any individual liberty guaranteed to her personally, but that Congress lacked authority vis-à-vis the States to legislate at all. That is the States' grievance to raise, not hers.

**C. Even apart from standing, Congress's power to legislate in execution of a valid treaty forecloses any individual right to resist prosecution on enumerated-powers grounds.**

Congress's authority to enact 18 U.S.C. § 229 in execution of the Chemical Weapons Convention independently confirms that no personal right of petitioner's has been invaded. In *Missouri v. Holland*, 252 U.S. 416 (1920), this Court held that legislation enacted to carry a valid treaty into effect is sustained under the Necessary and Proper Clause even where "an act of Congress could not deal with" the same subject standing alone. *Id.* at 432–33. And in *Screws v. United States*, 325 U.S. 91 (1945), the Court recognized that "the administration of criminal justice rests with the States except as Congress, acting within the scope of the powers delegated to it, has created offenses against the United States." *Id.* at 109. Congress's treaty-implementing power is such a delegated power, and *United States v. Darby*, 312 U.S. 100 (1941), teaches that the Tenth Amendment states "but a truism" that all powers not delegated are reserved—it does not independently constrain a delegated power once found. *Id.* at 124.

For these reasons, the judgment of the Third Circuit should be affirmed.
