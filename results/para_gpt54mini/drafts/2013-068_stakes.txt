**Argument**

I. The Massachusetts statute is a content-neutral, narrowly tailored time, place, and manner regulation that satisfies the First Amendment.

The First Circuit correctly upheld the challenged statute. The Supreme Court has long recognized that the government may regulate speech in traditional public fora through content-neutral time, place, and manner restrictions so long as the regulation is narrowly tailored to serve significant governmental interests and leaves open ample alternative channels of communication. *Ward v. Rock Against Racism*, 491 U.S. 781, 791 (1989); *Frisby v. Schultz*, 487 U.S. 474, 481 (1988); *Heffron v. International Society for Krishna Consciousness, Inc.*, 452 U.S. 640, 647-48 (1981).

That standard is satisfied here. Massachusetts enacted the law to protect access to reproductive health facilities, patient safety, and the orderly use of clinic entrances and driveways. Those are significant governmental interests. *Hill v. Colorado*, 530 U.S. 703, 715 (2000); *Madsen v. Women’s Health Center, Inc.*, 512 U.S. 753, 767-68 (1994). The law is also content neutral. It applies to all persons who knowingly enter or remain within the buffer zone, subject to limited exemptions tied to movement and access, not to the message conveyed. *Hill*, 530 U.S. at 719-25; *Ward*, 491 U.S. at 791-92.

Nor is the statute insufficiently tailored. Narrow tailoring does not require the government to adopt the least restrictive means. *Ward*, 491 U.S. at 798-99. It requires a regulation that does not burden substantially more speech than necessary to further the government’s legitimate interests. *McCullen v. Coakley*, 571 U.S. 464, 486-87 (2014). Even before this Court’s later decision in *McCullen*, the First Circuit had correctly reasoned that the Massachusetts law remained a permissible regulation of the place and manner of protest activity because ample means of communication remained available outside the zone. *McCullen v. Coakley*, 571 F.3d 167, 176-82 (1st Cir. 2009).

The petitioners can still speak, display signs, pray, distribute literature, and engage willing listeners outside the zone. The First Amendment does not guarantee access to the most effective position on the sidewalk. *Heffron*, 452 U.S. at 654; *Madsen*, 512 U.S. at 768. The Constitution requires ample alternative channels, not the speaker’s preferred tactical advantage. *Ward*, 491 U.S. at 802; *Frisby*, 487 U.S. at 484. The First Circuit therefore did not err in affirming the law’s constitutionality.

II. *Hill v. Colorado* controls unless and until this Court says otherwise; it should not be overruled in this case.

The petitioners ask this Court to limit or overrule *Hill v. Colorado*, 530 U.S. 703 (2000). That request should be rejected. *Hill* upheld a statute regulating communicative conduct near health care facilities, concluding that the law was content neutral and a valid time, place, and manner restriction. *Id.* at 715-30. The First Circuit properly treated *Hill* as controlling precedent. *McCullen*, 571 F.3d at 181-82.

Stare decisis is at its strongest where a decision interprets the Constitution and has been repeatedly relied upon by courts and legislatures. *Payne v. Tennessee*, 501 U.S. 808, 827-28 (1991); *Agostini v. Felton*, 521 U.S. 203, 235 (1997). Overruling requires a “special justification.” *Arizona v. Rumsey*, 467 U.S. 203, 212 (1984); *Dickerson v. United States*, 530 U.S. 428, 443 (2000). The petitioners have not shown one. To the contrary, *Hill* fits comfortably within the Court’s broader First Amendment precedents upholding content-neutral restrictions aimed at preserving access and public order. *Frisby*, 487 U.S. at 484-85; *Madsen*, 512 U.S. at 768-70; *Ward*, 491 U.S. at 791-802.

Nor does later First Amendment doctrine require repudiation of *Hill*. Decisions such as *Sorrell v. IMS Health Inc.*, 564 U.S. 552 (2011), and *Citizens United v. FEC*, 558 U.S. 310 (2010), addressed different problems—speaker discrimination and political censorship—not clinic-access buffer zones. *Sorrell*, 564 U.S. at 567-72; *Citizens United*, 558 U.S. at 340-48. This Court has repeatedly declined to unsettle settled doctrine merely because a later case states familiar First Amendment principles in a different context. *Agostini*, 521 U.S. at 237-38.

If anything, *Hill* is reinforced by the Court’s recognition that government may protect access to health care facilities from targeted obstruction and unwanted face-to-face confrontation. *Schenck v. Pro-Choice Network of Western New York*, 519 U.S. 357, 377-81 (1997); *Madsen*, 512 U.S. at 768-72. The First Circuit’s judgment should therefore be affirmed. The statute is constitutional under *Hill* and independent First Amendment doctrine, and there is no sound basis to overrule or materially limit *Hill* here.
