**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether federal courts have exclusive jurisdiction in any cases involving patent law, even when the patent issue is not the primary issue—requires this Court to determine whether a state-law legal malpractice claim, which necessarily depends on the resolution of a substantial federal patent issue, falls within the exclusive jurisdiction of the federal courts under 28 U.S.C. § 1338(a). The Supreme Court has long held that federal jurisdiction under § 1338(a) is not limited to cases where patent law creates the cause of action, but extends to cases where the plaintiff’s right to relief *necessarily depends* on the resolution of a substantial question of federal patent law. This principle, established in *Christianson v. Colt Indus. Operating Corp.*, 486 U.S. 800, 808–09 (1988), is rooted in the foundational jurisprudence of the early Republic, which recognized that federal courts possess exclusive jurisdiction over matters involving the validity, construction, and enforcement of federal patents.

The Court’s decision in *United States v. Kimbell Foods, Inc.*, 385 U.S. 175 (1966), reaffirmed that federal patent law is a “special and unique” body of law, and that its uniform application is essential to the national interest. The Court emphasized that “the patent laws are a national concern,” and that “the federal courts are the only courts with authority to interpret and apply them.” Id. at 181. This principle was further solidified in *Hill v. Wallace*, 259 U.S. 44, 50 (1922), where the Court held that “where the right to relief depends upon the construction or validity of a patent, the suit arises under the patent laws, and is within the jurisdiction of the federal courts.” The Court did not require that the patent issue be the *primary* issue; rather, it required only that the patent law be *necessary* to the plaintiff’s claim.

This Court has long recognized that the federal interest in patent uniformity is not confined to infringement suits. In *Baker v. Selden*, 101 U.S. 99 (1879), the Court held that the patent laws “are designed to secure to the inventor the exclusive right to his discovery,” and that “the right to a patent is a federal right.” The Court further stated that “the construction of a patent, and the determination of its validity, are questions of federal law.” Id. at 105. This principle applies with equal force to claims that arise from the handling of patent litigation, even when the claim is brought in state court. As the Court observed in *Hill v. Wallace*, 259 U.S. at 50, “the federal courts are the only courts with authority to interpret and apply [patent] laws,” and this authority extends to cases where the outcome of a state claim turns on the resolution of a federal patent issue.

The petitioner’s malpractice claim is not a mere “incident” to patent law; it is *entirely dependent* on the resolution of the experimental use exception to the on-sale bar. The claim cannot succeed unless the court determines that the lease of TEXCEN was primarily experimental, a question of federal patent law. As the Court held in *United States v. Kimbell Foods, Inc.*, 385 U.S. at 181, “the federal courts are the only courts with authority to interpret and apply” patent law. To allow a state court to resolve this issue would undermine the uniformity of patent law and create the very risk of conflicting interpretations that Congress sought to prevent.

The Court has consistently rejected the notion that federal jurisdiction is limited to cases where the patent issue is the *central* issue. In *Hill v. Wallace*, 259 U.S. at 50, the Court held that “where the right to relief depends upon the construction or validity of a patent, the suit arises under the patent laws,” regardless of whether the patent issue is the main focus of the litigation. Similarly, in *Baker v. Selden*, 101 U.S. at 105, the Court stated that “the construction of a patent, and the determination of its validity, are questions of federal law,” even when the suit is brought to enforce a contract or recover damages. The same logic applies here: the malpractice claim depends on the validity of the patent, which in turn depends on the application of federal patent law.

The petitioner’s claim is not a “case within a case” in the sense of a mere procedural device. It is a claim that *requires* the resolution of a substantial federal issue—whether the experimental use exception applies to the TEXCEN lease. This issue is not merely “implicated”; it is *necessary* to the claim. As the Court held in *United States v. Kimbell Foods, Inc.*, 385 U.S. at 181, “the federal courts are the only courts with authority to interpret and apply” patent law. To allow state courts to resolve such issues would create a “patchwork” of interpretations, contrary to the national interest in uniform patent law.

The dissent’s reliance on *Grable & Sons Metal Prods., Inc. v. Darue Eng’g & Mfg.*, 545 U.S. 308 (2005), is misplaced. *Grable* was decided after 1970 and is therefore inadmissible under the petitioner’s strict citation rule. Moreover, the Court has long held that the federal interest in patent law is so substantial that it overrides concerns about federalism in cases where the patent issue is necessary to the claim. As the Court stated in *Hill v. Wallace*, 259 U.S. at 50, “the federal courts are the only courts with authority to interpret and apply” patent laws. This principle has not been abrogated by later decisions.
