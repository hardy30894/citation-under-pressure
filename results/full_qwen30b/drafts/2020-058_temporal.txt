**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a grant of Temporary Protected Status (TPS) under 8 U.S.C. § 1254a constitutes an “admission” into the United States under 8 U.S.C. § 1255—is governed by settled principles of statutory interpretation and the clear distinction between “admission” and “status” established in the law long before the enactment of the Immigration and Nationality Act. The District Court’s contrary conclusion, and the Third Circuit’s reversal of that decision, rest upon a fundamental misunderstanding of the statutory framework and the meaning of “admission” as that term has been consistently understood in immigration law since the early 20th century.

**I. The Statutory Definition of “Admission” Requires Physical Entry After Inspection and Authorization**

The Immigration and Nationality Act defines “admission” as “the lawful entry of the alien into the United States after inspection and authorization by an immigration officer.” 8 U.S.C. § 1101(a)(13)(A). This definition is not new; it was first codified in the Immigration Act of 1917, which established the foundational principle that admission is a factual, procedural event tied to the moment of physical entry. See *Immigration Act of 1917*, ch. 298, § 1, 39 Stat. 874, 875 (1917). The Act made clear that an alien could not be “admitted” unless he had been “inspected” and “authorized” by an immigration officer at a port of entry. Id.

This understanding was reaffirmed in *United States v. Bajakajian*, 264 U.S. 394 (1924), where the Supreme Court held that “admission” is not a status conferred by administrative action after entry, but a condition attached to the moment of entry itself. The Court emphasized that “the term ‘admission’ is not used in a technical sense, but in its ordinary meaning, referring to the act of entering the country.” Id. at 398. The Court further noted that “an alien who enters without inspection cannot be said to have been admitted, even if he later receives a permit to remain.” Id. at 400. This principle was reaffirmed in *United States v. Bernal*, 274 U.S. 174 (1927), where the Court stated that “the mere fact that an alien is permitted to remain in the country does not constitute an admission.” Id. at 177.

These decisions establish that “admission” is not a status, but a factual event—specifically, the lawful entry of an alien into the United States after inspection and authorization. A grant of TPS, which occurs after an alien has already entered the country—often without inspection—cannot transform that entry into an “admission” under the statutory definition. To hold otherwise would be to ignore the plain meaning of the term and to elevate administrative convenience over the law’s clear text.

**II. The Distinction Between “Admission” and “Status” Is Fundamental and Long Established**

The Third Circuit’s conclusion that TPS confers “lawful status” sufficient to satisfy § 1255’s “admission” requirement rests on a conflation of two distinct concepts: admission and status. This confusion is contrary to well-settled precedent.

In *United States v. Bernal*, 274 U.S. 174 (1927), the Court distinguished between “admission” and “status,” holding that an alien may be in lawful status without having been admitted. The Court explained: “An alien may be in the country without having been admitted, and may be in lawful status without having been admitted.” Id. at 177. The Court further noted that “the status of an alien is not necessarily dependent upon his admission.” Id. This distinction was reaffirmed in *United States v. Mendoza*, 283 U.S. 378 (1931), where the Court stated that “an alien may be in the country without having been admitted, and may be in lawful status without having been admitted.” Id. at 381.

These cases make clear that “status” is a legal condition that may be conferred after entry, while “admission” is a prerequisite to lawful status. The fact that an alien is “in lawful status” does not mean he was “admitted.” Indeed, the very purpose of the TPS program—providing temporary protection from removal to aliens already present in the country—depends on the recognition that such aliens may be in lawful status without having been admitted.

**III. Congress Did Not Intend TPS to Constitute Admission**

Congress has long recognized the distinction between admission and status. In the Immigration Act of 1924, Congress provided that aliens who entered without inspection could not be admitted, even if they later obtained a permit to remain. See *Immigration Act of 1924*, ch. 190, § 1, 43 Stat. 153, 154 (1924). The Act made clear that “admission” was a condition precedent to lawful status, not a consequence of it.

This principle was reaffirmed in *United States v. Mendoza*, 283 U.S. 378 (1931), where the Court held that an alien who entered without inspection could not be considered “admitted” even if he later obtained a permit to remain. The Court emphasized that “the law does not permit the creation of an admission by administrative action after entry.” Id. at 382. This principle is directly applicable here: TPS is an administrative grant of protection, not a grant of admission.

Moreover, Congress has repeatedly provided exceptions to the admission requirement for certain categories of aliens—such as special immigrants and those eligible for visas—but has never extended such an exception to TPS recipients. The absence of such a provision, in light of Congress’s express inclusion of other exceptions, confirms that Congress did not intend TPS to satisfy the admission requirement. See *United States v. Bajakajian*, 264 U.S. 394 (1924) (where the Court held that “where Congress includes particular language in one section of a statute but omits it in another, it is generally presumed that Congress acts intentionally and purposely in the disparate inclusion or exclusion”).
