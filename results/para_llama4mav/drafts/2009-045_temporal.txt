The petitioners argue that a reasonable attorneys' fee award under a federal fee-shifting statute can never be enhanced based solely on the quality of performance and results obtained. However, this argument is not supported by the relevant case law.

In Trustees v. Greenough, 105 U.S. 527 (1882), the Supreme Court recognized that a court has the discretion to award a reasonable attorneys' fee to a prevailing party. The Court held that the amount of the fee should be determined based on the circumstances of the case, including the complexity of the litigation and the skill and expertise of the attorneys involved. Id. at 536-37. This principle suggests that the quality of performance and results obtained can be relevant factors in determining a reasonable attorneys' fee.

Furthermore, in Central Railroad & Banking Co. v. Pettus, 113 U.S. 116 (1885), the Supreme Court held that a court may consider the "value of the services rendered" when determining a reasonable attorneys' fee. Id. at 124-25. The quality of performance and results obtained are directly related to the value of the services rendered, and therefore, can be considered in determining a reasonable attorneys' fee.

These precedents establish that a court has the discretion to consider the quality of performance and results obtained when determining a reasonable attorneys' fee. While the lodestar method may provide a baseline for calculating the fee, it is not the only factor to be considered. The district court's decision to enhance the lodestar amount based on the exceptional quality of representation and results obtained in this case is consistent with these principles.

In conclusion, the district court's award of an enhanced attorneys' fee was within its discretion, and the Eleventh Circuit correctly affirmed the decision. The quality of performance and results obtained are relevant factors in determining a reasonable attorneys' fee, and the district court's consideration of these factors was proper.
