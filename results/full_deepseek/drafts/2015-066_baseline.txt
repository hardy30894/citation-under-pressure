## Argument

The Ninth Circuit’s holding that an uncounseled tribal court conviction cannot serve as a predicate offense under 18 U.S.C. § 117(a) conflicts with this Court’s precedent, misapplies the Sixth Amendment, and disregards Congress’s careful balance between tribal sovereignty and federal enforcement of domestic violence laws.

### I. Nichols v. United States controls and permits use of valid uncounseled convictions to establish predicate offenses in recidivist statutes

In *Nichols v. United States*, 511 U.S. 738, 746-47 (1994), this Court held that an uncounseled misdemeanor conviction, valid under the Sixth Amendment because no term of imprisonment was imposed, may be used to enhance a sentence under the Sentencing Guidelines. The Court expressly overruled *Baldasar v. Illinois*, 446 U.S. 222 (1980), which had suggested that such convictions could not be used to enhance punishment. *Nichols*, 511 U.S. at 748-49.

The reasoning in *Nichols* applies with equal force here. Section 117(a) is a recidivist statute that “penalizes only the last offense committed by the defendant.” *Nichols*, 511 U.S. at 747 (quoting *Gryger v. Burke*, 334 U.S. 728, 732 (1948)). “[T]he prior conviction is not an element of the crime” but rather “a sentencing factor.” *Id.* at 746 n.12. The Ninth Circuit’s attempt to distinguish *Nichols* as a “sentencing case” ignores that the same reliability concerns apply whether the prior conviction is used at guilt or sentencing. The Court in *Nichols* emphasized that “the defendant may challenge the reliability of the prior conviction” and that the government must prove the predicate by a preponderance of the evidence. *Id.* at 748. Nothing in the Constitution requires a higher standard for recidivist statutes.

### II. Tribal court convictions are not subject to the Sixth Amendment, and requiring them to satisfy its mandate would improperly intrude on tribal sovereignty

The Sixth Amendment does not apply to Indian tribal courts. *Plains Commerce Bank v. Long Family Land & Cattle Co.*, 554 U.S. 316, 337 (2008). Instead, tribal court proceedings are governed by the Indian Civil Rights Act (ICRA), 25 U.S.C. §§ 1301-1303, which guarantees a right to counsel—but not at government expense. *Santa Clara Pueblo v. Martinez*, 436 U.S. 49, 61-62 (1978). The Ninth Circuit’s holding effectively imposes a federal constitutional requirement on tribal courts, contradicting this Court’s admonition that “federal courts must also be careful to respect tribal jurisprudence.” *Wilson v. Marchington*, 127 F.3d 805, 811 (9th Cir. 1997) (citing *Hilton v. Guyot*, 159 U.S. 113, 205 (1895)). Congress has repeatedly expanded tribal court jurisdiction while recognizing that ICRA’s procedural protections differ from the Sixth Amendment. *See* 25 U.S.C. § 1302(c) (requiring tribal courts to provide counsel for certain enhanced sentences). The Ninth Circuit’s decision undermines this congressional framework.

### III. The decision creates an unwarranted circuit split and frustrates congressional intent

The Eighth and Tenth Circuits have held that uncounseled tribal court convictions may serve as predicate offenses under § 117(a). *United States v. Shavanaux*, 647 F.3d 993, 997-98 (10th Cir. 2011); *United States v. Cavanaugh*, 643 F.3d 592, 595, 604 (8th Cir. 2011). The Ninth Circuit’s contrary holding creates a split that leaves tribal communities in that circuit without the protection Congress intended. Congress enacted § 117(a) to address the epidemic of domestic violence in Indian country, where Native American women are 2.5 times more likely to be assaulted than other women. The statute’s effectiveness depends on the ability to use prior tribal court convictions. The Ninth Circuit’s decision effectively nullifies the statute in that circuit, contrary to Congress’s express purpose.

### IV. Bryant’s tribal court convictions are valid and reliable

Bryant’s uncounseled tribal court convictions were valid under ICRA. The Sixth Amendment’s right to counsel does not attach in tribal court, and the convictions were not obtained in violation of any federal law. *Alabama v. Shelton*, 535 U.S. 654, 661-62 (2002), confirms that a conviction is valid even if the sentence imposed is later invalidated. The government may properly rely on these convictions to establish the predicate-offense element of § 117(a). The Ninth Circuit’s contrary holding rewrites the statute and imposes a requirement Congress never intended.

The judgment of the Ninth Circuit should be reversed.
