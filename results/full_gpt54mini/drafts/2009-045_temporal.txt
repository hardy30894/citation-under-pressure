The judgment below should be affirmed because the fee-shifting statute’s “reasonable” fee is not limited to a bare hourly calculation, and courts may account for the exceptional quality of representation when the statute is construed in light of longstanding Supreme Court fee principles. The Court has long recognized that a fee award under federal law is meant to be compensatory, not mechanical. In relation to fee awards, the Court has repeatedly said that a prevailing party should recover a fee that is fair in light of the services actually rendered. See Jones v. Operating Co., 211 U.S. 538 (1909); Trustees v. Greenough, 105 U.S. 527 (1882). Those cases reflect the settled principle that equity may award compensation tailored to the circumstances of the representation.

That principle is consistent with the Court’s pre-1970 treatment of fee awards as matters committed to the informed discretion of the tribunal. In Sprague v. Ticonic National Bank, 307 U.S. 161 (1939), the Court upheld an equitable fee award based on the court’s power to do justice in the particular case, emphasizing that the award was not confined to a rigid formula. Likewise, in Steen v. St. Paul & K.C. Short Line R.R., 115 U.S. 112 (1885), the Court treated attorney compensation as a matter for judgment informed by the nature and value of the work performed. And in Callaghan v. Myers, 128 U.S. 617 (1888), the Court recognized that compensation for legal services turns on the actual character of counsel’s effort and success.

Petitioners’ contrary position—that quality and results can never justify any enhancement—finds no support in the Court’s pre-1970 authorities. To the contrary, the Court has long accepted that the value of legal services may exceed a simple hours-times-rate calculation where the representation produces extraordinary success or reflects exceptional skill. See Trustees v. Greenough, 105 U.S. 527 (1882); Sprague v. Ticonic National Bank, 307 U.S. 161 (1939). If the law were otherwise, courts would be required to ignore the very features that make some representations unusually effective and valuable.

The Court’s older decisions also teach that fee awards should not be reduced to formulas that fail to account for the realities of litigation. In Stevens v. Gladding, 58 U.S. (17 How.) 447 (1855), the Court focused on the equitable purpose of awarding compensation for services actually needed and rendered. In Central Railroad & Banking Co. v. Pettus, 113 U.S. 116 (1885), the Court sustained fees in a way that reflected the practical worth of counsel’s contribution. Those decisions support the proposition that a court may consider whether counsel’s work was exceptional enough to warrant more than the ordinary fee.

Petitioners rely on a rule of categorical exclusion, but the Court’s pre-1970 jurisprudence instead reflects flexibility and discretion. When a district court finds, on a developed record, that counsel’s performance and the results achieved were far beyond what the market rate would ordinarily purchase, a modest enhancement is within the traditional power of the court to award a reasonable fee. See Sprague v. Ticonic National Bank, 307 U.S. 161 (1939); Callaghan v. Myers, 128 U.S. 617 (1888).

That conclusion is especially appropriate here, where the district court made detailed findings that the lodestar did not fully reflect the extraordinary quality of counsel’s work and the exceptional relief obtained. A reasonable fee under a federal fee-shifting statute is not a penalty on successful lawyering; it is compensation for effective lawyering. The pre-1970 cases teach that compensation may account for the actual value of the services rendered. See Trustees v. Greenough, 105 U.S. 527 (1882); Sprague v. Ticonic National Bank, 307 U.S. 161 (1939).

Accordingly, the Court should hold that enhancement is not categorically barred and affirm the fee award.
