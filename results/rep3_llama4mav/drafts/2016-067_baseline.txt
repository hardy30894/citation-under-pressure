The Fair Debt Collection Practices Act (FDCPA) regulates the conduct of "debt collectors," not creditors collecting debts owed to themselves. 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(6); Heintz v. Jenkins, 514 U.S. 291, 293 (1995). The statute defines a "debt collector" as a person who regularly collects or attempts to collect debts owed or due another. 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(6). The key factor in determining whether an entity is a "debt collector" is whether it collects debts on behalf of others or for its own account. Davidson v. Capital One Bank (USA), N.A., 797 F.3d 1309, 1315-16 (11th Cir. 2015).

The petitioners argue that Santander is a "debt collector" because it acquired their defaulted debts from CitiFinancial Auto. However, this argument ignores the statutory language and the distinction between a "debt collector" and a "creditor." When Santander collected on the debts it had purchased, it was collecting debts owed to itself, not another. Therefore, it was not acting as a "debt collector" under the FDCPA. Schlegel v. Wells Fargo Bank, NA, 720 F.3d 1204, 1209 (9th Cir. 2013).

The petitioners' reliance on the exclusion from the definition of "creditor" in 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(4) is misplaced. This exclusion applies only to a person who receives defaulted debt "solely for the purpose of facilitating collection... for another." 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(4). Santander purchased the debts for its own account, not solely to facilitate collection for CitiFinancial Auto.

The exclusion in 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(6)(F)(iii) also does not support the petitioners' argument. This exclusion applies to a person collecting debts not in default at the time they were obtained. It does not imply that a person collecting defaulted debts is automatically a "debt collector." Rather, it highlights that the status of the debt at the time of acquisition is not the determining factor in whether an entity is a "debt collector."

The petitioners' argument that Santander's prior status as a debt collector when servicing their loans makes it a "debt collector" for all its collection activities is also unpersuasive. The FDCPA regulates specific conduct by debt collectors, not the general status of an entity. See 15 U.S.C. § 1692(e). Santander is subject to the FDCPA only when acting as a "debt collector" as defined in § 1692a(6).

In this case, Santander was collecting debts owed to itself, not another. Therefore, it was not acting as a "debt collector" under the FDCPA. The judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
