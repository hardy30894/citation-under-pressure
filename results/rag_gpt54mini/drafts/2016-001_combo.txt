Samsung’s damages award cannot stand because this Court has long rejected the rule that a patentee may automatically recover the infringer’s entire profits when the asserted patent covers only a component or feature of the accused product. The controlling principle is apportionment. In Westinghouse Electric & Manufacturing Co. v. Wagner Electric & Manufacturing Co., 225 U.S. 604 (1912), this Court held that where a patent “is only a part of the machine and creates only a part of the profits,” the patentee is “only entitled to recover that part of the net gains.” Id. at 614. The Court explained that the patentee must “give evidence tending to separate or apportion the defendant’s profits and the patentee’s damages between the patented feature and the unpatented features,” unless the patentee can show “that the entire value of the whole machine, as a marketable article, is properly and legally attributable to the patented feature.” Ibid.

That rule forecloses the award below. Apple’s design patents covered particular ornamental features of Samsung smartphones, not the smartphones as wholes. When a patent covers less than the entire article, the infringer’s profits are not automatically recoverable in full. Westinghouse, 225 U.S. at 614; Garretson v. Clark, 111 U.S. 120, 121 (1884). Garretson held that the patentee must provide “evidence tending to separate or apportion” profits and damages between the patented feature and the unpatented features, or prove that “the entire value of the whole machine” is attributable to the patented feature. Id. at 121. That requirement is especially important where the patented feature is only one contributor to consumer demand. Seymour v. McCormick, 57 U.S. (16 How.) 480, 490-91 (1854), rejected damages measured as if the defendant had “pirated the whole machine and every improvement on it,” when the patent covered only an improvement. Id. at 491. McCreary v. Pennsylvania Canal Co., 141 U.S. 459, 467-68 (1891), likewise recognized that damages must reflect the value of the patented improvement, not the unpatented whole.

This Court’s design-patent cases point the same way. In Dobson v. Hartford Carpet Co., 114 U.S. 439 (1885), the Court refused to allow recovery of the “entire profit” on the finished carpets merely because the infringement related to a design appearing on them. Id. at 444. The Court held that there was no basis to attribute the whole profit to the design, because the profits also reflected “carding, spinning, dyeing and weaving.” Id. at 444-45. Dobson v. Dornan, 118 U.S. 10 (1886), applied the same principle and again rejected a profits award untethered to the contribution of the patented design. Id. at 17-18. Those decisions are directly analogous here: Apple’s claimed design rights concerned partial product features, not the entire economic value of Samsung’s phones.

The government may argue that design patents are entitled to special treatment because 35 U.S.C. § 289 authorizes an infringer’s “total profit.” But this Court’s pre-1970 decisions still require a causal connection between the patented design and the profits awarded. See Dobson, 114 U.S. at 444-45; Dobson, 118 U.S. at 17-18. Nothing in those cases permits a windfall measured by profits from unrelated, unpatented features. To the contrary, the Court has repeatedly required reliable proof of the patented feature’s contribution. Westinghouse, 225 U.S. at 614; Garretson, 111 U.S. at 121.

The same remedial principle appears in the Court’s copyright cases. In Sheldon v. Metro-Goldwyn Pictures Corp., 309 U.S. 390 (1940), the Court held that profits can be apportioned so that the owner receives only the part attributable to the infringement, not what “the infringer himself has supplied.” Id. at 399. F. W. Woolworth Co. v. Contemporary Arts, Inc., 344 U.S. 228 (1952), recognized that remedies should compensate for infringement, not provide an automatic windfall detached from actual injury. Id. at 232-33. And Douglas v. Cunningham, 294 U.S. 207 (1935), explained that statutory remedial provisions were designed to give a rightsholder “some recompense for injury done,” not to dispense with proof of the proper measure of recovery. Id. at 209-10. Those cases confirm the broader remedial rule: recovery must be tied to the infringing contribution.

Apple’s award therefore cannot be sustained on a whole-product theory. The better rule, compelled by Westinghouse, Garretson, Seymour, McCreary, Dobson, and their progeny, is that when an infringed design patent covers only a component of a product, damages must be limited to the profits attributable to that component unless the patentee proves the entire product value is attributable to the design. Because Apple did not make that showing, the judgment should be reversed.
