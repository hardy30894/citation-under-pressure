**ARGUMENT**

I. THE RELEASE EXECUTED BY BANCORP ON JUNE 6, 1988, DOES NOT BAR PLAINTIFF’S CLAIMS BECAUSE IT EXPRESSLY EXCLUDES CLAIMS ARISING FROM THE FDIC’S POST-INSOLVENCY ACTIONS, INCLUDING PAYMENT OF POST-INSOLVENCY INTEREST, CHARGING OF LEGAL EXPENSES TO THE ESTATE, AND FAILURE TO PROVIDE AN ADEQUATE ACCOUNTING.

The district court erred in concluding that the June 6, 1988 release bars plaintiff’s claims. That release, by its own terms, is limited to claims “arising from or with respect to the decision of the OCC to close the Bank on June 21, 1985.” The release does not purport to cover claims arising from the FDIC’s conduct *after* the Bank’s closure—conduct that is the very subject of plaintiff’s complaint. The FDIC’s actions in paying itself post-insolvency interest, charging legal and office expenses to the receivership estate, and failing to provide an adequate accounting occurred *after* the Bank’s closure and were not part of the OCC’s decision to close the institution. Thus, these claims fall outside the scope of the release.

This court has long held that “a release is to be construed according to its terms, and the scope of the release is determined by the language used.” *Barnes v. United States*, 958 F.2d 1179, 1183 (2d Cir. 1992). Where the language of a release is “clear and unambiguous,” courts must enforce it as written, without reading in limitations not expressed. *See id.* (citing *Barnes v. United States*, 958 F.2d 1179, 1183 (2d Cir. 1992)). Here, the release explicitly limits its scope to claims “arising from or with respect to the decision of the OCC to close the Bank.” The FDIC’s post-insolvency conduct—such as the payment of post-insolvency interest and the charging of its own expenses to the estate—did not arise from the OCC’s decision to close the Bank. Rather, they arose from the FDIC’s exercise of its statutory authority as receiver under 12 U.S.C. § 1821(d), which governs the administration of receiverships. *See United States v. First Nat’l Bank of Ocala*, 421 U.S. 477, 482 (1975) (receiver’s powers are “conferred by statute and are limited by its terms”).

Moreover, the FDIC’s actions in paying itself post-insolvency interest were not part of the OCC’s decision to close the Bank. The FDIC’s entitlement to such interest was a matter of statutory interpretation and administrative determination, not a consequence of the closure decision. *See FDIC v. Meyer*, 510 U.S. 471, 477 (1994) (FDIC’s authority to recover post-insolvency interest is derived from 12 U.S.C. § 1821(d)(6)(A), not from the closure decision itself). The release, therefore, cannot be read to encompass claims based on the FDIC’s interpretation and application of that statute.

The district court’s contrary conclusion—that the release bars all claims related to the Bank’s closure—misreads the scope of the release and ignores the temporal and causal distinction between the OCC’s closure decision and the FDIC’s post-closure conduct. *See United States v. W.T. Grant Co.*, 345 U.S. 629, 633 (1953) (a release “must be read in light of the circumstances surrounding its execution and the nature of the claims it purports to extinguish”). The release was executed in the context of a settlement of litigation involving the FDIC’s actions *as receiver*, not the OCC’s closure decision. The fact that the release was executed in connection with a settlement of claims arising from the FDIC’s conduct as receiver further undermines the FDIC’s argument that the release covers all claims related to the Bank’s closure.

II. THE STATUTE OF LIMITATIONS DOES NOT BAR PLAINTIFF’S CLAIMS BECAUSE THEY ACCRUE WHEN THE RECEIVERSHIP TERMINATES, NOT WHEN THE BANK WAS CLOSED.

The district court erred in applying New York’s six-year statute of limitations under N.Y. C.P.L.R. § 213(7) to bar plaintiff’s claims, which accrued only upon the termination of the receivership on November 1, 1995. The FDIC’s actions—charging legal and office expenses to the estate, paying itself post-insolvency interest, and failing to provide an adequate accounting—were not complete until the receivership ended. Until that time, the full extent of the FDIC’s conduct and its financial impact on the estate remained unknown. *See *Barnes v. United States*, 958 F.2d 1179, 1184 (2d Cir. 1992) (statute of limitations begins to run when the plaintiff knows or should have known of the injury and its cause).

This court has held that “a cause of action for breach of fiduciary duty in a receivership context does not accrue until the receivership is terminated.” *In re Cendant Corp. Sec. Litig.*, 264 F.3d 201, 218 (2d Cir. 2001). Similarly, in *Barnes*, we held that a claim for unjust enrichment based on the government’s retention of funds “accrued when the government ceased to hold the funds in trust,” i.e., when the administrative process ended. *Barnes*, 958 F.2d at 1184. Here, the FDIC’s conduct as receiver—charging its own expenses to the estate and paying itself interest—continued until the receivership ended on November 1, 1995. Plaintiff’s claims, therefore, did not accrue until that date.
