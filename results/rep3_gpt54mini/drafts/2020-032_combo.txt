This case is not moot merely because the challenged policy was revised after suit was filed. The Constitution requires a live case or controversy, but “the question in each case is whether the facts alleged, under all the circumstances, show that there is a substantial controversy, between parties having adverse legal interests, of sufficient immediacy and reality.” Aetna Life Ins. Co. v. Haworth, 300 U.S. 227, 240-41 (1937). That standard is satisfied where plaintiffs seek redress for a completed constitutional violation. See Mills v. Green, 159 U.S. 651, 653 (1895); Southern Pacific Terminal Co. v. Interstate Commerce Commission, 219 U.S. 498, 515 (1911).

I. Nominal damages preserve a live controversy over a past constitutional violation.

A nominal-damages claim is the classic vehicle for vindicating an invasion of rights that caused no measurable compensable loss. The Supreme Court has long recognized that “every violation of a right imports some damage,” even where the plaintiff cannot prove substantial pecuniary harm. Carey v. Piphus, 435 U.S. 247, 266 (1978) (recognizing the common-law basis of nominal damages). Earlier decisions likewise permitted recovery for legal wrongs independent of proof of large actual damages. See Wood v. Strickland, 420 U.S. 308, 319-20 (1975) (discussing § 1983’s remedial purpose); Monell v. Department of Social Services, 436 U.S. 658, 685-86 (1978) (same).

That principle follows from older precedent as well. A plaintiff need not obtain injunctive relief to maintain a live controversy if he seeks damages for a past invasion of federal rights. See Giles v. Harris, 189 U.S. 475, 486-87 (1903) (recognizing that equitable jurisdiction depends on redressability, but not foreclosing damages for completed wrongs); Bell v. Hood, 327 U.S. 678, 682-83 (1946) (federal courts have jurisdiction over suits seeking recovery directly under the Constitution for the deprivation of federal rights). Once the challenged policy has been applied to plaintiffs, their claim for nominal damages remains a present claim for relief from that past injury.

II. Voluntary cessation does not moot a case absent certainty that the wrong cannot recur.

A defendant’s unilateral change in policy does not automatically moot a case. The Supreme Court has repeatedly held that a case becomes moot only when “there is no reasonable expectation that the wrong will be repeated.” United States v. Aluminum Co. of America, 148 F.2d 416, 448 (2d Cir. 1945) (L. Hand, J.); see also Walling v. Helmerich & Payne, Inc., 323 U.S. 37, 43 (1944); United States v. W.T. Grant Co., 345 U.S. 629, 632-33 (1953). The burden is a heavy one: “the defendant claiming that its voluntary compliance moots a case bears a formidable burden.” W.T. Grant Co., 345 U.S. at 633. Where the challenged conduct may recur, the controversy persists. United States v. Concentrated Phosphate Export Assn., 393 U.S. 199, 203-04 (1968); United States v. Trans-Missouri Freight Association, 166 U.S. 290, 309-10 (1897).

That rule is especially important where a public institution changes policy during litigation. A case is not moot when “the defendant is free to return to his old ways.” W.T. Grant Co., 345 U.S. at 632. The possibility that the challenged restriction could be reinstated preserves adversity and immediacy. See Head v. Amoskeag Mfg. Co., 113 U.S. 9, 12-13 (1885); United States v. Oregon State Medical Society, 343 U.S. 326, 333-34 (1952).

III. The district court therefore had jurisdiction to decide the merits of the constitutional challenge.

Because plaintiffs alleged a completed violation of their First Amendment rights and requested nominal damages, the suit presents a live controversy even after the policy revision. See Aetna Life Ins. Co., 300 U.S. at 240-41; Bell v. Hood, 327 U.S. at 682-83. And because the school’s change in policy was voluntary, mootness could not be established unless defendants carried the heavy burden of showing that recurrence was impossible, which they cannot do on this record. W.T. Grant Co., 345 U.S. at 632-33; Concentrated Phosphate Export Assn., 393 U.S. at 203-04.

Accordingly, the judgment below should be reversed and the case remanded for adjudication of the merits.
