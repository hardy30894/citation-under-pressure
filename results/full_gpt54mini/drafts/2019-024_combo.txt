**Argument**

ERISA’s text asks when the plaintiff had “actual knowledge of the breach or violation,” not when the defendant disclosed the facts or when the plaintiff could have learned them by reading available materials. 29 U.S.C. § 1113(2). The common-law meaning of “knowledge” confirms that a person does not “know” a fact merely because information was placed before him; he must actually be aware of it. See *Mackey v. Coxe*, 59 U.S. (18 How.) 100, 104 (1855) (distinguishing actual from constructive notice); *Babb v. Stromberg*, 220 U.S. 494, 496 (1911) (knowledge is awareness, not imputed awareness). That ordinary understanding controls here. Congress used the modifier “actual” to narrow the limitations bar, and courts may not rewrite the statute to convert actual knowledge into constructive knowledge. See *Patterson v. United States*, 181 U.S. 535, 551 (1901) (courts must apply the statute Congress enacted); *United States v. Dickerson*, 310 U.S. 554, 562 (1940) (same).

Intel’s contrary rule would collapse the statutory distinction between actual and constructive knowledge. That reading cannot be squared with settled interpretive principles. Where Congress uses different language, courts must give that difference effect. See *Gooch v. United States*, 297 U.S. 124, 128 (1936) (“We think that the ordinary meaning of the words should control.”); *Caminetti v. United States*, 242 U.S. 470, 485 (1917) (courts must enforce the statute’s plain meaning); *Holy Trinity Church v. United States*, 143 U.S. 457, 459-60 (1892) (a statute’s words are the starting point); *Caminetti v. United States*, 242 U.S. 470, 485 (1917). If Congress had meant knowledge obtainable from disclosures, it knew how to say so. Indeed, the Court has long recognized that actual knowledge is different from what one merely could have discovered through diligence. See *Brown v. Piper*, 91 U.S. 37, 42 (1875) (distinguishing knowledge in fact from means of knowledge); *Kirby v. Tallmadge*, 160 U.S. 379, 383 (1896) (notice principles do not supply what a party did not actually know).

The Ninth Circuit’s rule also conflicts with the basic structure of statutes of limitations. Limitations periods protect defendants from stale claims once a plaintiff has knowledge sufficient to sue, but they do not begin until the statutory trigger occurs. See *Wood v. Carpenter*, 101 U.S. 135, 139 (1879) (statutes of limitations run when the plaintiff has the requisite knowledge); *M’Cluny v. Silliman*, 28 U.S. (3 Pet.) 270, 276 (1830) (limitations statutes are enforced according to their terms). ERISA chose a heightened trigger—actual knowledge—precisely to ensure that plaintiffs are not barred by papers they never read or facts they do not recall. Courts may not replace that choice with a “reasonable opportunity to know” standard. See *McIver v. Ragan*, 15 U.S. (2 Wheat.) 25, 29 (1817) (courts may not add words to a statute); *The Harrisburg*, 119 U.S. 199, 214 (1886) (limitations rules must be applied as written).

Intel’s theory would be especially inappropriate in the ERISA fiduciary context. Fiduciary obligations are strict, and the law traditionally requires clear proof before cutting off a beneficiary’s right to sue. See *Meinhard v. Salmon*, 249 N.Y. 458, 464, 164 N.E. 545 (1928) (Cardozo, C.J.) (fiduciary standards are “the punctilio of an honor the most sensitive”); *Bowen v. Evans*, 1 N.Y. 356, 359 (1848) (beneficiary rights are not forfeited by mere constructive notice). A rule that deems a participant to have “actual knowledge” from unread disclosures would invert that protection and reward the party with superior access to information. Equity has never treated silence in the face of buried disclosures as equivalent to conscious awareness. See *Peyton v. Desmond*, 129 U.S. 505, 515 (1889) (equity looks to actual circumstances, not artificial presumptions); *In re Smith*, 98 U.S. 465, 467 (1878) (legal consequences follow actual, not imputed, knowledge).

The better reading is straightforward: a plaintiff has “actual knowledge” only when he is truly aware of the relevant breach or violation. That is the ordinary meaning of the words Congress used, the only meaning that preserves the statutory distinction between actual and constructive knowledge, and the only rule consistent with the traditional treatment of limitations statutes. Because Intel’s disclosures may show what Sulyma could have known, but not what he actually knew, the suit is not barred under 29 U.S.C. § 1113(2). See *Mackey v. Coxe*, 59 U.S. (18 How.) 100, 104 (1855); *Wood v. Carpenter*, 101 U.S. 135, 139 (1879); *Caminetti v. United States*, 242 U.S. 470, 485 (1917).
