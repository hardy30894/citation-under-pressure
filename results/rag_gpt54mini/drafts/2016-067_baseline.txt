**Argument**

The FDCPA does not treat every purchaser of defaulted debt as a “debt collector.” Congress drew a distinction between those who collect debts “owed or due another” and those who collect debts for their own account. 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(6); see Heintz v. Jenkins, 514 U.S. 291, 294 (1995) (the Act applies to those who “regularly collects or attempts to collect, directly or indirectly, debts owed or due or asserted to be owed or due another”); Jerman v. Carlisle, McNellie, Rini, Kramer & Ulrich, L.P.A., 559 U.S. 573, 577-78 (2010) (FDCPA “regulates interactions between consumer debtors and ‘debt collector[s],’ defined to include any person who ‘regularly collects ... debts owed or due or asserted to be owed or due another’”). Santander purchased the loans here and then attempted to collect them for itself. Under the statutory text, that makes Santander a creditor, not a debt collector.

The Act’s structure confirms that conclusion. Section 1692a(6) first defines “debt collector” as a person whose “principal purpose” is debt collection or who “regularly collects or attempts to collect” debts “owed or due another.” 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(6). That definition focuses on whether the entity collects for itself or for others. The statute then separately excludes from the definition persons collecting debts “owed or due or asserted to be owed or due another” when the debt “was not in default at the time it was obtained.” § 1692a(6)(F)(iii). As the Fourth Circuit explained below, the default status of the debt does not expand the threshold definition; it matters only if the person is already collecting “for another.” Henson v. Santander Consumer USA Inc., 817 F.3d 131, 135-39 (4th Cir. 2016). That reading is consistent with Davidson v. Capital One Bank (USA), N.A., 797 F.3d 1309, 1314-16 (11th Cir. 2015), which held that a person must “regularly collect or attempt to collect debts for others” to qualify as a debt collector under the Act’s ordinary definition.

Petitioners’ contrary theory would rewrite “another” out of the statute. The Supreme Court has repeatedly instructed that statutory terms must be read in context, and that the “natural construction” of qualifying language should not be ignored. Paroline v. United States, 572 U.S. 434, 447 (2014). Here, “debts owed or due or asserted to be owed or due another” refers to debts owed to a third party at the time of the collection activity, not debts that once belonged to someone else. See Henson, 817 F.3d at 138-39; Schlegel v. Wells Fargo Bank, N.A., 720 F.3d 1204, 1209 (9th Cir. 2013) (“The statute is not susceptible to the [plaintiffs’] interpretation that ‘owed or due another’ means ‘originally owed or due another’ ”). Otherwise, an entity that bought a debt outright would remain forever subject to the FDCPA merely because the debt had once been “due another,” a result nowhere stated in the Act. See Henson, 817 F.3d at 140-41.

Nor does the fact that Santander had previously serviced or collected the loans for CitiFinancial change the analysis. The FDCPA applies when a defendant is acting as a debt collector in connection with the challenged conduct. See Heintz, 514 U.S. at 294; Jerman, 559 U.S. at 577-78. Once CitiFinancial sold the receivables, Santander collected on debts it owned; it was no longer collecting “debts owed or due another.” Henson, 817 F.3d at 139-41. The relevant inquiry is the defendant’s status when the alleged violation occurred, not some earlier role it may have played. See Davidson, 797 F.3d at 1318.

Petitioners’ reliance on the default status of the loans also conflicts with the statutory purpose. Congress enacted the FDCPA “to eliminate abusive debt collection practices by debt collectors,” 15 U.S.C. § 1692(e), not to regulate creditors collecting their own accounts. Jerman, 559 U.S. at 577-78. As the Fourth Circuit recognized, adopting petitioners’ rule would sweep ordinary creditors into the statute whenever they also engage in some third-party collection activity. Henson, 817 F.3d at 141-43. Congress did not “intend this.” Id. at 142.

Because Santander purchased the defaulted receivables and then sought to collect them for its own account, it was not a “debt collector” under 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(6). The judgment below should be affirmed.
