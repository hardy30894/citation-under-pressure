# ARGUMENT

**PUNITIVE DAMAGES UNDER TITLE VII REQUIRE PROOF THAT THE EMPLOYER'S CONDUCT WAS EGREGIOUS, NOT MERELY THAT IT WAS ACCOMPANIED BY A CULPABLE STATE OF MIND**

## I. The Text and History of § 1981a(b)(1) Confirm That Congress Reserved Punitive Damages for Egregious Misconduct

Section 1981a(b)(1) permits punitive damages only where a plaintiff "demonstrates that the respondent engaged in a discriminatory practice or discriminatory practices with malice or with reckless indifference to the federally protected rights of an aggrieved individual." 42 U.S.C. § 1981a(b)(1). Congress borrowed this formulation from the common law of punitive damages, which has long confined such awards to defendants whose conduct is "outrageous, because of the defendant's evil motive or his reckless indifference to the rights of others." *See* Restatement (Second) of Torts § 908(2) (1979); *Smith v. Wade*, 461 U.S. 30, 56 (1983) (punitive damages available under § 1983 for conduct involving "reckless or callous indifference to the federally protected rights of others"). This Court in *Smith* did not divorce the mental-state inquiry from the character of the underlying conduct; rather, it treated "reckless or callous indifference" as itself a marker of aggravated, blameworthy behavior warranting the "quasi-criminal" sanction of punitive damages. 461 U.S. at 59.

The legislative history of the 1991 Act reinforces this reading. The bipartisan interpretive memorandum submitted by Senator Dole and his co-sponsors explained that punitive damages would be available only in "extraordinarily egregious cases." 137 Cong. Rec. S15473 (Oct. 30, 1991). That understanding is consistent with the House Report's observation that § 1981a demands of a plaintiff "an even higher standard" than mere proof of intentional discrimination. Congress thus signaled that ordinary intentional discrimination—the conduct necessary to establish liability at all under Title VII—cannot, without more, support the extraordinary remedy of punitive damages.

## II. This Court's Punitive Damages Jurisprudence Confirms That the Reprehensibility of the Conduct, Not Merely the Defendant's Knowledge, Must Justify the Sanction

Punitive damages exist to punish and deter conduct that society regards as intolerable, not simply to compensate for the wrong already redressed through equitable relief and back pay. *See BMW of North America, Inc. v. Gore*, 517 U.S. 559, 568 (1996) (punitive damages serve the same purposes as criminal penalties: "retribution and deterrence"). This Court in *Gore* held that the "degree of reprehensibility of the defendant's conduct" is the "most important indicium of the reasonableness of a punitive damages award." *Id.* at 575. If reprehensibility is constitutionally indispensable to the *amount* of a punitive award, it cannot be irrelevant to whether such an award may be imposed *at all*. A rule that permits punitive liability whenever an employer's agent harbors "malice" or "reckless indifference," without regard to how egregious the underlying conduct actually was, collapses the liability and punishment inquiries Congress and this Court have consistently kept distinct.

The experience of the courts of appeals applying the analogous punitive damages standard under 42 U.S.C. § 1981 illustrates the danger of conflating these inquiries. In *Williamson v. Handy Button Machine Co.*, 817 F.2d 1290 (7th Cir. 1987), the Seventh Circuit recognized that punitive damages require conduct sufficiently clear and outrageous that "reasonably competent people would have agreed" it violated the law—a standard that looks to the objective character of the conduct, not simply to the subjective state of mind of the actor. *Id.* at 1296. That approach avoids transforming every case of intentional discrimination, however garden-variety, into a punitive damages case merely because a manager subjectively understood that discrimination is unlawful.

## III. Divorcing "Egregiousness" from the Punitive Damages Inquiry Would Impose Disproportionate Liability on Employers Who Discriminate Without Aggravating Circumstances

Title VII liability itself requires only proof of intentional discrimination. *See McDonnell Douglas Corp. v. Green*, 411 U.S. 792, 802 (1973). If "malice" or "reckless indifference" is satisfied merely by proof that a decisionmaker knew that discrimination on the basis of sex was generally unlawful, then virtually every plaintiff who prevails on liability will automatically satisfy the punitive damages threshold as well, since employers are presumed to know that Title VII forbids intentional discrimination. That result would eliminate the "even higher standard" Congress intended and would convert the punitive damages provision from an exceptional remedy for egregious wrongdoing into a routine adjunct of ordinary liability.

Respondent's conduct here—an internal, contested promotion decision in which supervisors weighed competing qualifications and reached a decision a jury could find pretextual—bears none of the hallmarks of the "outrageous" or "extraordinarily egregious" conduct Congress had in mind. *See* 137 Cong. Rec. S15473. Absent evidence of egregious circumstances—such as deliberate concealment, retaliation, or repeated and flagrant violations—an employer's isolated, mistaken personnel decision, even if later found discriminatory, does not warrant the quasi-criminal sanction of punitive damages.

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should hold that punitive damages under Title VII require proof that the employer's conduct was egregious, independent of and in addition to proof of the requisite malicious or recklessly indifferent state of mind, and should affirm the judgment below.
