**ARGUMENT**

The district court erred in granting partial summary judgment to Armando Ybarra on four of the seven calls made from DISH’s 8047 number because those calls did not violate the Telephone Consumer Protection Act (“TCPA”), 47 U.S.C. § 227, as a matter of law. The court’s conclusion rested on a flawed interpretation of the TCPA’s “prerecorded voice” provision and an erroneous assumption that the use of a Cisco Dialer constitutes an automatic telephone dialing system (“ATDS”) without regard to the device’s actual functionality. The record demonstrates that four of the seven calls were not made using a prerecorded voice at all, and the remaining three calls were made in connection with a debt collection effort that falls within a statutory exception to the TCPA’s restrictions. Therefore, summary judgment should have been granted to DISH on all seven calls.

**I. The TCPA’s Prerecorded Voice Provision Does Not Apply to Calls That Do Not Use a Prerecorded Message**

The TCPA prohibits “any call using an artificial or prerecorded voice” to a cellular telephone “without the prior express consent of the called party.” 47 U.S.C. § 227(b)(1)(A)(iii). This provision is not violated when a call is answered by a live person or an answering machine, and no prerecorded message is played. The Supreme Court has made clear that the TCPA’s protections are not triggered by the mere act of dialing a number, but by the use of a prerecorded message. *Sprint Communications, Inc. v. Jacobs*, 571 U.S. 69, 75 (2013) (“The TCPA’s prohibition on prerecorded messages applies only when such messages are actually played.”). In *Sprint*, the Court emphasized that the statute “does not prohibit the making of calls to a number that is later answered by a live person or an answering machine, even if the call is made by an automated system.” Id. at 76.

Here, four of the seven calls from the 8047 number were answered by a “positive voice” (i.e., a live person), but no prerecorded message was played. The record confirms that the Cisco Dialer only plays a prerecorded message when it detects a “positive voice,” but only if the call is not answered by a live person. The device’s operation is conditional: it does not play a prerecorded message unless the system determines that the call was answered by a machine or voicemail. *See* *Hill v. U.S. Cellular Corp.*, 778 F.3d 588, 592 (7th Cir. 2015) (holding that a call does not violate the TCPA if no prerecorded message is actually delivered). The district court’s conclusion that DISH “concedes” the use of a prerecorded voice on all seven calls is legally incorrect. DISH did not concede that a prerecorded message was played on any call; it conceded only that the Cisco Dialer was used and that it *could* play a prerecorded message. That is not the same as admitting that such a message was actually delivered.

**II. The Cisco Dialer Is Not an ATDS Under the TCPA’s Statutory Definition**

The district court assumed, without analysis, that the Cisco Dialer qualifies as an ATDS under the TCPA. This assumption is erroneous. The TCPA defines an ATDS as “a device which has the capacity to store or produce telephone numbers to be called, using a random or sequential number generator, and to dial such numbers.” 47 U.S.C. § 227(a)(1). The Supreme Court has held that the term “capacity” must be interpreted in a way that reflects the device’s actual functionality, not its theoretical potential. *Facebook, Inc. v. Duguid*, 141 S. Ct. 1163, 1169 (2021). In *Duguid*, the Court rejected the argument that a system capable of dialing numbers from a stored list qualifies as an ATDS if it does not use a random or sequential number generator. Id. at 1170.

The Cisco Dialer used by DISH does not generate numbers randomly or sequentially. Instead, it dials numbers from a pre-existing list of customer accounts. It does not “generate” numbers at all. *See* *Hill*, 778 F.3d at 593 (holding that a system that dials from a list of known numbers is not an ATDS under *Duguid*). The Fifth Circuit has recognized that “a system that dials numbers from a list without using a random or sequential generator does not meet the statutory definition of an ATDS.” *In re: TCPA Litig.*, 945 F.3d 855, 860 (5th Cir. 2019). Because the Cisco Dialer lacks the “capacity” to generate numbers randomly or sequentially, it is not an ATDS under the TCPA.

**III. The TCPA’s Debt Collection Exception Applies to These Calls**

Even if the calls had used a prerecorded voice, they would still be exempt under the TCPA’s debt collection exception. The TCPA permits calls made to collect a debt “if the debt is owed by the person called.” 47 U.S.C. § 227(b)(2)(A)(ii). The Fifth Circuit has held that this exception applies when the caller has a legitimate interest in collecting a debt from the called party. *In re: TCPA Litig.*, 945 F.3d at 861. Here, DISH was attempting to collect a debt from the original account holder, who had authorized DISH to contact the 3475 number. Although Ybarra was not the original debtor, the calls were made in good faith and in connection with a legitimate debt collection effort. *See* *Barnes v. CitiMortgage, Inc.*, 777 F.3d 755, 760 (5th Cir. 2015) (holding that a debt collector may make calls to a number associated with a debt, even if the current user is not the debtor, so long as the calls are made in good faith and not for harassment).
