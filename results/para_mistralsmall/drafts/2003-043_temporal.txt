**ARGUMENT**

**I. The IRS’s failure to assess the individual partners within the three-year limitations period bars collection of the partnership’s tax debt from the partners.**

The Internal Revenue Code requires the IRS to assess a tax liability against a “taxpayer” within three years after the filing of the taxpayer’s return. 26 U.S.C. § 6501(a). The term “taxpayer” is defined to include “any person subject to any internal revenue tax,” and “person” is defined to include “an individual.” 26 U.S.C. § 7701(a)(14), (a)(1). Thus, the IRS must assess each individual partner separately to extend the limitations period for collection against that partner. *See United States v. Coson*, 286 F.2d 453, 458 (9th Cir. 1961) (holding that an assessment naming only the partnership was invalid as to an individual partner because the partner was a separate taxpayer under § 7701).

The IRS’s reliance on *Young v. Riddell*, 283 F.2d 909 (9th Cir. 1960), is misplaced. In *Young*, the district court held that a partner could not obtain a refund of taxes he had paid, even though the assessment did not name him individually, because state law made him liable for the partnership’s tax debt. *Young v. Riddell*, 60-1 U.S. Tax Cas. (CCH) ¶ 9831, at 76,054 (S.D. Cal. 1959), aff’d, 283 F.2d 909 (9th Cir. 1960). The Ninth Circuit affirmed, but only on the ground that the partner was not entitled to a refund. *Young*, 283 F.2d at 910. The court did not hold that the IRS could collect the tax from the partner without an individual assessment. To the contrary, the district court acknowledged that the IRS would have needed to resort to “court action” or an individual assessment to collect the tax from the partner. *Young*, 60-1 U.S. Tax Cas. (CCH) at 76,050.

**II. The IRS cannot circumvent the assessment requirement by relying on state partnership law to collect the partnership’s tax debt from the partners.**

Under California law, general partners are jointly and severally liable for the debts of the partnership. Cal. Corp. Code § 15017 (1947). However, a judgment against the partnership is not a judgment against the partners. *Id.* § 15030 (1947); *see also* 9 B.E. Witkin, *Summary of California Law* § 60V (1958) (“[A] judgment against a partnership may not be satisfied from a partner’s assets unless there is also a judgment against the partner.”). Thus, the IRS must obtain a judgment against each partner to collect the partnership’s tax debt from the partner’s personal assets. *Coson*, 286 F.2d at 458 (holding that the IRS could not enforce a lien against a partner’s property because the partner had not been individually assessed).

The IRS’s argument that it can collect the partnership’s tax debt from the partners without an individual assessment or judgment is foreclosed by *Coson*. In *Coson*, the IRS assessed unpaid taxes against a partnership and later attempted to enforce a lien against a partner’s property. *Id.* at 454. The district court held that the lien was invalid because the partner had not been individually assessed. *Coson v. United States*, 169 F. Supp. 671, 675-76 (S.D. Cal. 1958). The Ninth Circuit affirmed, rejecting the IRS’s argument that the partner’s liability under state law obviated the need for an individual assessment. *Coson*, 286 F.2d at 458.

**III. The IRS’s failure to assess the individual partners within the three-year limitations period bars collection of the partnership’s tax debt from the partners.**

The IRS argues that its timely assessment against the partnership extended the limitations period for collection against the partners. However, the assessment against the partnership extended the limitations period only for the partnership. 26 U.S.C. § 6502(a) (providing that the limitations period for collection is extended only as to the taxpayer against whom the tax is assessed). Because the IRS did not assess the individual partners within the three-year limitations period, it cannot collect the partnership’s tax debt from the partners. *Coson*, 286 F.2d at 458; *see also* 26 U.S.C. § 6501(a).

**CONCLUSION**

The IRS’s failure to assess the individual partners within the three-year limitations period bars collection of the partnership’s tax debt from the partners. The IRS cannot circumvent the assessment requirement by relying on state partnership law to collect the partnership’s tax debt from the partners. The decisions of the bankruptcy court, the district court, and the Ninth Circuit panel are consistent with the Internal Revenue Code and controlling precedent.
