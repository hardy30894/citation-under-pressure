**ARGUMENT**

The First Circuit correctly upheld the Massachusetts buffer zone law under the First Amendment as applied to the states through the Fourteenth Amendment. The statute is content-neutral, narrowly tailored to serve a significant governmental interest in protecting access to reproductive health care facilities, and leaves open ample alternative channels of communication. This Court has repeatedly affirmed such regulations when they are designed to preserve public order and ensure safe passage for patients and staff—interests that are both substantial and compelling. The judgment below must be affirmed.

### I. THE MASSACHUSETTS BUFFER ZONE LAW IS A VALID TIME-PLACE-MANNER REGULATION UNDER THE FIRST AMENDMENT

The First Amendment does not protect speech in all places at all times. *Hill v. Colorado*, 530 U.S. 703, 714, 120 S.Ct. 2480, 147 L.Ed.2d 597 (2000), established that “the government may regulate the time, place, or manner of protected speech so long as the regulation is content-neutral, serves a significant governmental interest, and leaves open ample alternative channels of communication.” The Massachusetts law satisfies each prong of this test.

First, the law is **content-neutral**. It prohibits no message, no viewpoint, and no speaker based on ideology. As the First Circuit observed, the statute applies equally to all individuals regardless of whether they advocate for or against abortion. *McCullen v. Coakley*, 571 F.3d 167, 178 (1st Cir.2009). The only distinction drawn is between those who enter or remain within the buffer zone and those who do not—specifically, exempting patients, employees, emergency personnel, and others using the sidewalk for purposes unrelated to the clinic. This neutrality is critical: “A regulation that is content-neutral is presumptively valid even if it incidentally burdens some speech.” *United States v. O’Brien*, 391 U.S. 367, 377, 88 S.Ct. 1673, 20 L.Ed.2d 672 (1968).

Second, the law serves a **significant governmental interest**: ensuring the free and unimpeded access of patients and staff to reproductive health care facilities. *Hill*, 530 U.S. at 715, 120 S.Ct. 2480. The Supreme Court recognized that “the right to enter and exit a medical facility without harassment or intimidation” is a fundamental aspect of personal liberty. Id. at 716, 120 S.Ct. 2480. In *Hill*, the Court upheld a Colorado law banning “any person from approaching within 100 feet of any entrance to a health care facility with the intent to speak with or persuade a patient,” noting that “the State’s interest in protecting access to health care facilities is substantial.” Id. at 715, 120 S.Ct. 2480. Massachusetts’ 35-foot buffer zone is narrower than Colorado’s and thus more protective of speech while still achieving the same core objective: preventing obstruction and intimidation at clinic entrances.

Third, the law **leaves open ample alternative channels of communication**. The plaintiffs retain full freedom to speak, distribute literature, display signs, pray, and wear symbolic garments—all outside the buffer zone. *See* *Snyder v. Phelps*, 562 U.S. 443, 453, 131 S.Ct. 1207, 179 L.Ed.2d 172 (2011) (“[T]he First Amendment protects speech even when it is offensive or unpopular.”); *Ward v. Rock Against Racism*, 491 U.S. 781, 794, 109 S.Ct. 2746, 105 L.Ed.2d 661 (1989) (“[A]n ordinance may be constitutional even if it limits the amount of speech, so long as it leaves open ample alternative channels.”). The First Circuit found that the plaintiffs could communicate effectively from the public sidewalk beyond the 35-foot zone, including by amplifying their voices, displaying large signs, and engaging in group prayer. *McCullen I*, 571 F.3d at 179–80. Even if some interactions are less immediate, the law does not eliminate meaningful communication.

### II. THE LAW OF THE CASE DOCTRINE PROPERLY BARRED RELITIGATION OF FACIAL CHALLENGES

The plaintiffs seek to relitigate the facial constitutionality of the Act—a matter already decided by this Court’s precedent and affirmed by the First Circuit in *McCullen I*. The law of the case doctrine bars such relitigation. *United States v. Matthews*, 643 F.3d 9, 13 (1st Cir.2011). The doctrine prevents “relitigation in the trial court of matters that were explicitly or implicitly decided by an earlier appellate decision in the same case.” Id. Here, the First Circuit held in *McCullen I* that the law was content-neutral, viewpoint-neutral, and a valid time-place-manner regulation. That determination remains binding unless one of three narrow exceptions applies: (1) controlling legal authority has changed dramatically; (2) new evidence has come to light; or (3) the prior decision was blatantly erroneous and would result in a miscarriage of justice.

None of these exceptions apply. The plaintiffs cite *Citizens United v. FEC*, 558 U.S. 310, 130 S.Ct. 876, 175 L.Ed.2d 753 (2010), and *Sorrell v. IMS Health Inc.*, — U.S. —, 131 S.Ct. 2653, 180 L.Ed.2d 544 (2011), but those cases concern commercial speech and campaign finance, not protest near clinics. They do not alter the analysis of buffer zones around health care facilities. *Hill v. Colorado*, 530 U.S. 703, 120 S.Ct. 2480, remains the governing precedent.
