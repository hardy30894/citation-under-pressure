**ARGUMENT**

**The Equal-Footing Test for Title Navigability Does Not Require Segment-by-Segment Proof Isolated to the Instant of Statehood; It Asks Whether the River, Considered as a Whole in Its Ordinary Condition, Was Used or Susceptible of Use as a Highway of Commerce**

PPL asks this Court to impose a rigid rule: unless the State proves that each discrete stretch of river beneath each dam was, at the precise moment of statehood, capable of sustained commercial navigation, title remains in the United States or its grantees. That rule cannot be squared with this Court's equal-footing precedents, which look to the river's *ordinary condition* as a continuous artery of commerce, not to a snapshot of an isolated segment.

**A. The Equal-Footing Doctrine Vests Title to the Beds of Navigable Waters in the States Upon Admission.**

Under *Pollard v. Hagan*, 44 U.S. 212 (1845), each new State enters the Union "on an equal footing with the original states," acquiring title to the beds of its navigable waters as an incident of sovereignty. This Court has consistently applied that principle, including in *Montana v. United States*, 450 U.S. 544 (1981), which confirms that title to riverbeds "passed to the States as an incident of sovereignty," 450 U.S. at 551, and is distinct from the federal navigational servitude, which serves Congress's commerce power rather than proprietary title.

**B. The Governing Test Asks Whether the River Was Used or Susceptible of Use, in Its Ordinary Condition, as a Highway for Commerce.**

The controlling definition comes from *The Daniel Ball*, 77 U.S. (10 Wall.) 557 (1870), which held that rivers are navigable "when they are used, or are susceptible of being used, in their ordinary condition, as highways for commerce." *Id.* at 563. That standard is functional and holistic; it does not demand mile-by-mile continuous commercial traffic, nor does it require that every foot of riverbed be independently navigable at the exact moment of admission.

This Court applied that principle in *United States v. Utah*, 283 U.S. 64 (1931), holding that a river's navigability for title purposes is properly assessed by considering the river "as a whole," even where particular reaches contain rapids, falls, or other obstructions that require portage. The Court explained that such interruptions do not defeat navigability of the river as a commercial artery, because "the fact that the river's navigability is interrupted by ... rapids ... does not affect the navigability of the river above and below." *Id.* at 87. Portage around a natural obstacle is itself part of the historic mode of using a river as a highway of trade, not evidence that the river was non-navigable.

Similarly, in *Economy Light & Power Co. v. United States*, 256 U.S. 113 (1921), the Court rejected the argument that occasional natural impediments to travel destroy a waterway's navigable character, recognizing that "the capacity of the river for use in commerce ... is the fundamental test," not the absence of any obstruction whatever. *Id.* at 121–22.

**C. Courts May Properly Consider Evidence From Before and After Statehood as Probative of the River's Natural, Ordinary Capacity.**

PPL's insistence that only evidence contemporaneous with 1889 may be considered ignores that this Court has long permitted reliance on evidence from other periods when it illuminates the river's *natural and permanent characteristics*. In *Utah v. United States*, 403 U.S. 9 (1971), the Court considered evidence of the Great Salt Lake's condition both before and after statehood in determining title navigability, explaining that "the question ... is one of fact to be resolved in light of the greatest number of factors bearing on the natural and probable use of the river." *Id.* at 11–12. Likewise, in *Oklahoma v. Texas*, 258 U.S. 574 (1922), the Court examined a lengthy historical record—including trapper, trader, and settler accounts spanning decades—to determine whether the Red River was navigable at statehood, because "the character of the river in this respect is not a matter which is naturally confined to a single point in time." *Id.* at 586.

That approach makes sense: rivers do not change their fundamental character overnight, and the natural condition of a channel, its gradient, and its capacity to float commerce are properties that persist across years. Requiring proof pegged only to the calendar date of statehood, to the exclusion of surrounding historical and physical evidence, would make the equal-footing test nearly impossible to administer and would contradict this Court's own practice.

**D. The Susceptibility Standard of *Utah Division of State Lands* Confirms That Actual, Continuous, Present-Day Commercial Use Is Not Required.**

In *Utah Division of State Lands v. United States*, 482 U.S. 193 (1987), the Court reaffirmed that title navigability turns on "susceptibility to use as a highway of commerce," not on whether the water was, in fact, regularly used for trade at a fixed historical moment. *Id.* at 196. Susceptibility is measured by the river's physical capacity—its depth, current, and continuity—not by proof that boats plied every mile on the day the State was admitted.

**Conclusion**

The Montana Supreme Court properly applied this settled framework: it examined the Missouri, Madison, and Clark Fork Rivers as continuous commercial arteries, considered portages around discrete natural obstructions consistent with *United States v. Utah*, and drew on historical evidence bearing on the rivers' natural and ordinary capacity consistent with *Utah v. United States* and *Oklahoma v. Texas*. PPL's segment-isolated, moment-frozen test finds no support in this Court's precedents and should be rejected.
