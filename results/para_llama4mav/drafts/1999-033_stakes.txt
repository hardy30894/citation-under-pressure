The Equal Protection Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment does not give rise to a cause of action on behalf of a "class of one" where the plaintiff did not allege membership in a class or group. The Seventh Circuit's holding that a plaintiff can allege an equal protection violation by asserting that state action was motivated solely by a "spiteful effort to 'get' him for reasons wholly unrelated to any legitimate state objective" is an unwarranted expansion of the Equal Protection Clause.

The Equal Protection Clause is traditionally understood to protect against discrimination based on membership in a particular class or group. See, e.g., Brown v. Board of Education, 347 U.S. 483 (1954); Loving v. Virginia, 388 U.S. 1 (1967). The Clause is not intended to provide a cause of action for individualized grievances unrelated to class-based discrimination. As the Supreme Court has noted, the Equal Protection Clause is "directed at preventing state actors from discriminating between individuals on the basis of irrelevant or invidious criteria." Engquist v. Oregon Department of Agriculture, 478 F.3d 985, 993 (9th Cir. 2007) (citing Village of Willowbrook v. Olech, 528 U.S. 562, 564 (2000) (per curiam)).

In Olech, this Court did not hold that a "class of one" can state a claim under the Equal Protection Clause. Rather, the Court assumed arguendo that such a claim was cognizable and held that the plaintiff had stated a claim by alleging that the Village's actions were "irrational and wholly arbitrary." 528 U.S. at 565. The Court's decision was based on the presence of allegations of arbitrary and irrational government action, not on the theory that a "class of one" can bring an equal protection claim.

Allowing a "class of one" to bring an equal protection claim would effectively constitutionalize many ordinary tort claims and undermine the traditional distinction between equal protection and due process claims. See, e.g., Daniels v. Williams, 474 U.S. 327 (1986). The Seventh Circuit's holding would create an unwarranted expansion of federal judicial power and invite litigation that is more properly addressed under state law.

The judgment of the Seventh Circuit should be reversed, and the case should be remanded for further proceedings consistent with a proper understanding of the Equal Protection Clause.
