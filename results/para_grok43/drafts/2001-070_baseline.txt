The Eighth Amendment prohibits only those punishments that are both cruel and unusual. Gregg v. Georgia, 428 U.S. 153, 170 (1976) (plurality opinion). A punishment is unusual if it is contrary to the "evolving standards of decency that mark the progress of a maturing society." Trop v. Dulles, 356 U.S. 86, 101 (1958) (plurality opinion). Those standards are measured primarily by "objective factors to the maximum possible extent," foremost among them "the legislation enacted by the country's legislatures." Penry v. Lynaugh, 492 U.S. 302, 331 (1989) (quoting Coker v. Georgia, 433 U.S. 584, 592 (1977) (plurality opinion)).

When this Court last addressed the question, it concluded that "there is insufficient evidence of a national consensus against executing mentally retarded people." Penry, 492 U.S. at 335. At that time, only two States had enacted statutes barring such executions. Id. Since Penry, the number of States prohibiting the practice has risen to eighteen, while thirty States that authorize capital punishment continue to permit it. This modest increase does not demonstrate the "consistency of the direction of change" required to establish a new national consensus. Atkins v. Virginia, 536 U.S. 304, 315 (2002) (Rehnquist, C.J., dissenting).

Even where a consensus might be discerned, the Court must still determine whether the punishment is excessive in light of the offender's culpability and the penological purposes served. Penry, 492 U.S. at 335-36. Mental retardation, while relevant to mitigation, does not categorically negate the retributive or deterrent value of capital punishment. Id. at 338-39. The Eighth Amendment therefore permits the States to retain discretion to impose the death penalty on mentally retarded offenders after individualized consideration of all mitigating evidence. See Eddings v. Oklahoma, 455 U.S. 104, 113-14 (1982).

The judgment of the Supreme Court of Virginia should be affirmed.
