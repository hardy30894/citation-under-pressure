**ARGUMENT**

The First Circuit correctly upheld Massachusetts’ 35-foot buffer zone law under the First Amendment as applied to the petitioners’ claims. The statute is content-neutral, narrowly tailored to serve a significant governmental interest in ensuring safe access to reproductive health care facilities, and leaves open ample alternative channels for communication. The Court should affirm.

### I. THE FIRST AMENDMENT DOES NOT PROTECT PRO-LIFE COUNSELING THAT INTERFERES WITH PATIENT ACCESS TO CLINICS

The core of the petitioners’ claim rests on a fundamental misapprehension: that the First Amendment protects speech that obstructs or intimidates individuals seeking medical care. This is not the law. In *Hill v. Colorado*, 530 U.S. 703, 714, 120 S.Ct. 2480, 147 L.Ed.2d 597 (2000), the Supreme Court upheld a Colorado law prohibiting “any person from knowingly approaching within 8 feet of any individual entering or leaving a health care facility” where such conduct would interfere with access. The Court emphasized that “[t]he right to speak does not include the right to force one’s message upon unwilling recipients.” Id. at 715, 120 S.Ct. 2480. That principle governs here.

Massachusetts’ law—like the one in *Hill*—does not target the content or viewpoint of speech. It prohibits only physical proximity to clinic entrances, exits, and driveways when such presence may impede access. As the First Circuit correctly held, the law is “content-neutral, viewpoint-neutral, and a valid time-place-manner regulation.” *McCullen v. Coakley*, 571 F.3d 167, 176 (1st Cir. 2009). The petitioners are free to speak, pray, distribute literature, and display signs so long as they do not enter the designated buffer zones. Their ability to communicate remains robust: they may speak at a distance, use amplification devices, wear symbolic clothing, and engage in group prayer outside the restricted area. These are not mere “alternative channels”—they are constitutionally protected forms of expression.

### II. THE LAW IS NARROWLY TAILORED AND LEAVES ADEQUATE ALTERNATIVE MEANS OF COMMUNICATION

The petitioners argue that the buffer zone prevents “close personal contact,” but this is not a constitutional requirement. The First Amendment does not guarantee a right to approach patients at eye level or within conversational distance. *See id.* at 715, 120 S.Ct. 2480 (“[T]he government may regulate the time, place, and manner of speech even if it has some incidental effect on the amount of speech.”). The Supreme Court has repeatedly affirmed that regulations restricting speech near sensitive locations—such as abortion clinics—are permissible so long as they are narrowly drawn to serve a substantial interest.

Here, the state’s interest in protecting patient safety and access is compelling. Abortion clinics are places where individuals seek private, often vulnerable medical services. The record shows that petitioners have engaged in conduct—including loud prayers, repeated confrontations, and blocking walkways—that can be intimidating and coercive. See *McCullen III*, 844 F.Supp.2d 206, 213–25 (D. Mass. 2012). The buffer zone ensures that patients may enter and exit without fear of harassment or obstruction. The law achieves this end without banning all speech; it merely limits the location of certain expressive activities.

Moreover, the petitioners have not demonstrated that the buffer zone eliminates meaningful communication. They admit to having persuaded women to avoid abortions. Yet their own evidence shows that they continue to speak, distribute literature, and display signs—even if at a distance. The fact that some interactions are less intimate than before does not render the law unconstitutional. *See United States v. Grace*, 461 U.S. 170, 177, 103 S.Ct. 1702, 75 L.Ed.2d 736 (1983) (upholding a law regulating picketing near federal buildings despite reduced effectiveness); *Hill*, 530 U.S. at 715, 120 S.Ct. 2480 (affirming buffer zone despite potential reduction in face-to-face interaction).

### III. THE LAW OF THE CASE DOCTRINE BARRED RELITIGATION OF FACIAL CHALLENGE

The petitioners attempt to relitigate the facial constitutionality of the law, despite this Court’s prior affirmation in *McCullen I*. The law of the case doctrine bars such relitigation. *United States v. Matthews*, 643 F.3d 9, 13 (1st Cir. 2011). The doctrine applies both to trial courts and appellate panels in successive appeals. *Id.* at 14. The petitioners offer no valid exception: there has been no dramatic change in controlling legal authority, no new evidence, and no “blatant error” that would work a miscarriage of justice.

Their reliance on *Citizens United v. FEC*, 558 U.S. 310, 130 S.Ct. 876, 175 L.Ed.2d 753 (2010), and *Snyder v. Phelps*, 562 U.S. 443, 131 S.Ct. 1207, 179 L.Ed.2d 172 (2011), is misplaced. Those cases concern political speech and religious protests at military funerals—contexts far removed from the regulation of physical access to medical facilities. They do not undermine the precedent in *Hill*, which squarely governs this case. To suggest otherwise is to treat the First Amendment as a blank check for expressive conduct that interferes with others’ rights.
