**ARGUMENT**

The Fair Debt Collection Practices Act regulates only “debt collector[s].” 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(6). Santander is not one. The Act defines a debt collector as any person “who regularly collects or attempts to collect, directly or indirectly, debts owed or due or asserted to be owed or due another.” *Id.* (emphasis added). Because Santander purchased the loans and thereafter sought to collect them for its own account, the debts were not “owed or due another” at the time of collection. *See Heintz v. Jenkins*, 514 U.S. 291, 293 (1995) (the Act reaches persons who “regularly collect[] or attempt[] to collect . . . debts owed [to] . . . another”).

The statutory text is “entirely transparent.” *Davidson v. Capital One Bank (USA), N.A.*, 797 F.3d 1309, 1315–16 (11th Cir. 2015). The phrase “owed or due another” refers to the status of the debt at the moment of collection activity, not at some earlier time. *Schlegel v. Wells Fargo Bank, NA*, 720 F.3d 1204, 1209 (9th Cir. 2013) (“The statute is not susceptible to the interpretation that ‘owed or due another’ means ‘originally owed or due another.’”). When Santander contacted petitioners, the loans belonged to Santander; no third party was owed anything.

Petitioners’ contrary reading—that default status at acquisition converts a purchaser into a debt collector—finds no support in the text. The Act distinguishes creditors from debt collectors by whether the collector acts for another or for itself, not by the timing of default. *See* 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(4) (defining “creditor” as “any person who offers or extends credit creating a debt”); S. Rep. No. 95-382, at 3 (1977) (the term “debt collector” covers “all third persons who regularly collect debts for others”). The default-status test would also produce the anomalous result that a single entity could be both creditor and debt collector with respect to the same loan depending on when it acquired the paper—an outcome the statute’s structure precludes. *Davidson*, 797 F.3d at 1318.

The complaint alleges that CitiFinancial sold the loans to Santander before the challenged communications occurred. Once the sale closed, Santander collected its own debts. That conduct places Santander outside the statutory definition of debt collector. The judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
