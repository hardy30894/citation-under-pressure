The United States respectfully submits that the Court should reverse the district court's denial of its motion to dismiss Guam's complaint. The Comprehensive Environmental Response, Compensation, and Liability Act (CERCLA) provides two avenues for potentially responsible parties (PRPs) to seek recoupment of cleanup costs: a section 107 "cost-recovery" action and a section 113(f) "contribution" action. 42 U.S.C. §§ 9607, 9613(f). The distinction between these two provisions is critical, as the statute of limitations for a contribution action is three years, while that for a remedial cost-recovery action is six years. Id. § 113(g)(2)(B), (3).

The Court of Appeals correctly held that Guam's 2004 consent decree with the Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) triggered its right to pursue a contribution claim under section 113(f)(3)(B), precluding it from now pursuing a claim under section 107. As the court noted, section 113(f)(3)(B) provides that a party that "has resolved its liability to the United States or a State for some or all of a response action or for some or all of the costs of such action in an administrative or judicially approved settlement shall be entitled to seek contribution" from other PRPs. Id. § 9613(f)(3)(B).

The terms of the consent decree demonstrate that Guam resolved its liability to the United States for the Ordot Dump cleanup. The decree required Guam to pay a civil penalty, close the dump, and design and install a cover system. Consent Decree 5-12, J.A. 94-101. This is consistent with the holding in Cooper Industries, Inc. v. Aviall Services, Inc., 543 U.S. 157, 167 (2004), that a settlement that resolves a PRP's liability for a response action triggers the right to seek contribution under section 113(f)(3)(B).

Guam's argument that the consent decree did not resolve its liability because it included a disclaimer of liability is unavailing. As the Sixth Circuit held in Florida Power Corp. v. FirstEnergy Corp., 810 F.3d 996, 1002 (6th Cir. 2015), a disclaimer of liability may weigh against the conclusion that a settlement resolves liability, but it is not dispositive. The Tenth Circuit has also held that "the mere fact that [a party] refused to admit liability is not enough to exempt [a consent] [d]ecree from the reach of section 113(f)(3)(B)." Refined Metals Corp. v. NL Industries, 937 F.3d 928, 931 (10th Cir. 2019).

Furthermore, the Supreme Court has held that CERCLA's contribution provisions are intended to encourage settlement and provide finality for settling parties. See Burlington Northern & Santa Fe Railway Co. v. United States, 556 U.S. 599, 613 (2009) (noting that CERCLA's contribution provisions "create an incentive for PRPs to settle with the EPA"). Allowing Guam to pursue a section 107 claim despite having settled with EPA would undermine this purpose.

The district court's conclusion that the consent decree did not trigger section 113(f)(3)(B) is also inconsistent with the holding in General Electric Co. v. Jackson, 610 F.3d 110, 114 (D.C. Cir. 2010), that CERCLA's provisions should be interpreted to facilitate the prompt cleanup of hazardous waste sites. By settling with EPA, Guam took on the responsibility for cleaning up the Ordot Dump, and it is now barred from seeking contribution under section 107.

Guam's argument that the consent decree falls outside CERCLA's provisions because it concerned non-CERCLA pollutant discharges is also without merit. The complaint demanded that Guam complete construction of a cover system to eliminate discharges of untreated leachate, CWA Compl. ¶ 29, and CERCLA expressly identifies the "collection of leachate and runoff" as a "remedial action." 42 U.S.C. § 9601(24). See also United States v. Bestfoods, 524 U.S. 51, 55 (1998) (noting that CERCLA is a "broad remedial statute" intended to address environmental hazards).

Finally, Guam's due process argument is forfeited because it was not raised in the district court. See Keepseagle v. Vilsack, 815 F.3d 28, 36 (D.C. Cir. 2016). Even if it were not forfeited, the argument is unpersuasive, as Guam lost the ability to bring a contribution claim not because it was deprived of due process, but because the statute of limitations ran. See Hirshfeld v. District of Columbia, 254 F.2d 774, 775 (D.C. Cir. 1958) (noting that where a statute is clear, the courts are not at liberty to construe it other than according to its terms).

The Court should reverse the district court's denial of the United States' motion to dismiss and remand with instructions to dismiss the complaint. As the Supreme Court has held, "where a statute is clear, the courts are not at liberty to construe the statute other than according to its terms." Id. The Court of Appeals correctly applied CERCLA's provisions to the facts of this case, and its decision should be affirmed.

In sum, the United States respectfully submits that the Court should reverse the district court's denial of its motion to dismiss Guam's complaint, as Guam's claim is barred by the statute of limitations for contribution actions under CERCLA section 113(f)(3)(B). The consent decree between Guam and EPA triggered Guam's right to seek contribution, and Guam's failure to bring a timely claim under section 113(f) bars its claim under section 107.
