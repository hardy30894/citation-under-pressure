Respondent’s OCSLA claim fails because § 1333(b) does not extend Longshore Act coverage to every injury with some relationship to offshore operations. The statute covers only “disability or death of an employee resulting from any injury occurring as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf.” 43 U.S.C. § 1333(b). That text limits coverage to injuries that occur as a result of covered OCS operations; it does not erase the location requirement that Congress embedded in the phrase “operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf.” See Offshore Logistics, Inc. v. Tallentire, 477 U.S. 207, 219 (1986) (“Congress determined that the general scope of OCSLA’s coverage . . . would be determined principally by locale, not by the status of the individual injured or killed.”).

The Ninth Circuit erred by reading § 1333(b) as though it said “arising out of” offshore operations, with no geographic limitation at all. But the Supreme Court has cautioned against exactly that kind of expansion. In Tallentire, the Court explained that OCSLA’s coverage is defined principally by situs, and it specifically noted that § 1333(b) is the “only” provision that superimposes a status requirement on that otherwise situs-based scheme. 477 U.S. at 219 n.2. That footnote matters because it confirms that § 1333(b) is an exception to the general locale-based structure of OCSLA, not a free-standing causation formula that detaches coverage from the Shelf. See id.

The Fifth Circuit’s en banc decision in Mills v. Director, Office of Workers’ Compensation Programs, 877 F.2d 356 (5th Cir. 1989) (en banc), correctly construed § 1333(b) to require that the injury occur on the outer Continental Shelf or over its waters. Id. at 362. Mills recognized that the statutory phrase “as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf” must be read as a unit, so that the covered injury is one tied to offshore operations in a geographic sense, not merely in a “but for” sense. Id. The court rejected the broader reading adopted below because it would sever the statute from its territorial focus. Id. at 360-62. That approach is consistent with the OCSLA’s structure, which repeatedly uses location-based terms to define the reach of the Act. See 43 U.S.C. §§ 1331(a), 1333(a)(2)(A), 1333(b).

Petitioner relies on Curtis v. Schlumberger Offshore Service, Inc., 849 F.2d 805 (3d Cir. 1988), but Curtis gives the statutory phrase too little weight. Curtis treated § 1333(b) as satisfied whenever offshore operations are a “but for” cause of injury, even when the injury occurs on land. Id. at 809-11. That reading is difficult to square with the statutory text, because “resulting from” does not eliminate the separate requirement that the injury be tied to “operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf.” 43 U.S.C. § 1333(b). Nor does Curtis account for the Supreme Court’s repeated insistence that OCSLA coverage is driven by locale. Tallentire, 477 U.S. at 219; see also Herb’s Welding, Inc. v. Gray, 470 U.S. 414, 423-24 (1985) (explaining that OCSLA’s geographic limitation produces coverage that is not coextensive with maritime employment).

The Ninth Circuit’s “substantial nexus” test is especially problematic because it has no anchor in the text of § 1333(b). The statute does not say “substantial nexus,” and courts may not rewrite a jurisdictional coverage provision to match perceived policy goals. See Tallentire, 477 U.S. at 218-19. A nexus test also invites arbitrary line-drawing: if an onshore forklift accident qualifies because the decedent once worked on a platform, what of injuries to office staff, truck drivers, or contractors whose work is indirectly related to offshore production? Mills avoided that uncertainty by retaining a location-based rule grounded in Congress’s territorial scheme. 877 F.2d at 362.

This Court should therefore reverse the Ninth Circuit’s construction of § 1333(b) and hold that OCSLA workers’ compensation extends only to injuries occurring on the outer Continental Shelf or its waters. That reading respects the statutory text, preserves the locale-based structure recognized in Tallentire, and avoids converting a narrow offshore compensation provision into a general employment-benefits statute for land-based accidents.
