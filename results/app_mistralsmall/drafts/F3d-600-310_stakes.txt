**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Bankruptcy Court Lacked Jurisdiction to Determine Post-Petition Interest and Collection Costs After the Chapter 13 Estate Was Closed and the Debtor Discharged.**

The Fourth Circuit has long held that bankruptcy courts are courts of limited jurisdiction, with authority circumscribed by statute. *In re Harris*, 590 F.3d 264, 268 (4th Cir. 2009). Pursuant to 28 U.S.C. § 1334(a), district courts have original jurisdiction over all cases under title 11, and 28 U.S.C. § 157(a) authorizes bankruptcy judges to hear such cases and core proceedings. However, as the Supreme Court explained in *Celotex Corp. v. Edwards*, 514 U.S. 300, 308 (1995), “[t]he jurisdiction of the bankruptcy courts . . . is premised upon and limited by the jurisdictional grant to the district courts.” Once a bankruptcy estate is closed and the debtor discharged, the bankruptcy court’s jurisdiction over the discharged debts terminates unless an exception applies. *In re Riverside-Linden Inv. Co.*, 945 F.2d 320, 323 (4th Cir. 1991).

Here, the bankruptcy court lacked jurisdiction to adjudicate ECMC’s entitlement to post-petition interest and collection costs because those claims arose *after* the Chapter 13 estate was closed and Kirkland’s discharge entered. The Fourth Circuit has recognized that “post-discharge disputes concerning the amount or validity of a debt are not core proceedings and may not be adjudicated by the bankruptcy court.” *In re Fostvedt*, 823 F.2d 305, 307 (4th Cir. 1987). In *Fostvedt*, the court held that a creditor’s claim for post-discharge attorney’s fees was not a core proceeding under 28 U.S.C. § 157(b)(2), as it did not “stem from the bankruptcy itself.” *Id.* at 308. Similarly, ECMC’s claim for post-petition interest and collection costs arose from Kirkland’s post-discharge default, not from the bankruptcy proceedings. The bankruptcy court therefore lacked jurisdiction to adjudicate these claims.

The district court erred in affirming the bankruptcy court’s exercise of jurisdiction. As the Fourth Circuit has emphasized, “bankruptcy courts are not courts of general jurisdiction and cannot adjudicate disputes that are not inherently part of the bankruptcy process.” *In re Cnty. Mgmt., Inc.*, 927 F.2d 302, 305 (4th Cir. 1991). Post-discharge collection efforts are quintessentially outside the bankruptcy process, and the bankruptcy court’s attempt to adjudicate them exceeded its statutory authority.

**II. The Bankruptcy Court Erred in Awarding Post-Petition Interest Without a Proper Determination of the Underlying Debt’s Dischargeability.**

Even assuming arguendo that the bankruptcy court had jurisdiction, its award of post-petition interest was erroneous because it failed to address whether the underlying student loan debt was dischargeable. The Fourth Circuit has held that “a creditor may not recover post-petition interest on a debt that has been discharged in bankruptcy.” *In re O’Neal*, 718 F.2d 1312, 1315 (4th Cir. 1983). In *O’Neal*, the court explained that “[p]ost-petition interest is part of the debt itself and is dischargeable along with the principal unless the creditor can show that the debt is nondischargeable under 11 U.S.C. § 523.” *Id.*

Here, the bankruptcy court never determined whether Kirkland’s student loan debt was nondischargeable under § 523(a)(8). Instead, it summarily concluded that no payments had been made on the loan and awarded ECMC the full principal amount. However, as the court acknowledged, Kirkland had listed the loan in her Chapter 13 plan, and the plan was designed to pay the loan in full except for post-petition interest. The bankruptcy court’s failure to address whether the loan was dischargeable—let alone whether it was nondischargeable—rendered its award of post-petition interest unsupported by law.

Moreover, the bankruptcy court’s reliance on *In re Saxman*, 325 F.3d 1168 (9th Cir. 2003), is misplaced. *Saxman* does not stand for the proposition that a creditor may recover post-petition interest on a discharged debt; rather, it holds that post-petition interest may be recovered *only* if the underlying debt is nondischargeable. *Id.* at 1174. Because the bankruptcy court never made the requisite finding that Kirkland’s student loan was nondischargeable, its award of post-petition interest was legally erroneous.

**III. The Bankruptcy Court Improperly Awarded Collection Costs Without a Statutory or Contractual Basis.**

The bankruptcy court also erred in denying ECMC’s request for collection costs. Under 11 U.S.C. § 523(a)(8), a student loan is nondischargeable unless the debtor proves undue hardship, but the statute does not expressly authorize the recovery of collection costs. The Fourth Circuit has held that “collection costs are recoverable only if they are expressly provided for in the loan agreement or by statute.” *In re Smith*, 848 F.2d 119, 121 (4th Cir. 1988). Here, ECMC did not provide any evidence that the loan agreement or applicable law authorized the recovery of collection costs. The bankruptcy court therefore correctly denied ECMC’s request for such costs.

**Conclusion**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the district court should be reversed. The bankruptcy court lacked jurisdiction to adjudicate ECMC’s claim for post-petition interest, and its award of such interest was erroneous because it failed to determine whether the underlying debt was nondischargeable. The bankruptcy court’s denial of collection costs, however, was correct.
