The Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment does not impose a heightened "reasoned choice" standard for determining a defendant's competency to plead guilty or waive counsel. Instead, the governing inquiry remains whether the defendant possesses "sufficient present ability to consult with his lawyer with a reasonable degree of rational understanding" and "has a rational as well as factual understanding of the proceedings against him." Dusky v. United States, 362 U.S. 402, 402 (1960) (per curiam).

This Court has long held that a valid waiver of constitutional rights must be made "voluntarily" and with "full understanding of what the plea connotes and of its consequence." Boykin v. Alabama, 395 U.S. 238, 243-44 (1969). Yet the inquiry into competency to effect such a waiver is not distinct from the competency required to stand trial. See Pate v. Robinson, 383 U.S. 375, 385 (1966) (due process forbids trial of an incompetent defendant). The same baseline protects the integrity of a guilty plea or self-representation. See Adams v. United States ex rel. McCann, 317 U.S. 269, 275 (1942) (waiver of counsel must be "intelligent and competent").

Historical practice confirms that no separate, elevated standard applies. In Johnson v. Zerbst, 304 U.S. 458 (1938), the Court required only that the accused "competently and intelligently" forgo counsel, without demanding a capacity for "reasoned choice" among strategic options. Id. at 468. Likewise, Von Moltke v. Gillies, 332 U.S. 708 (1948), examined whether the defendant understood the charges and possible penalties, not whether she could weigh every tactical alternative. Id. at 724.

When a trial court possesses information raising a bona fide doubt as to competency, due process requires a hearing. Pate, 383 U.S. at 385. But the hearing need only resolve the Dusky inquiry; it need not apply a more demanding test. See Westbrook v. Arizona, 384 U.S. 150, 150 (1966) (per curiam) (remanding for determination whether defendant was competent to waive counsel under the ordinary standard). Post-conviction review similarly tests whether the record supports a finding of competency under that standard. See Machibroda v. United States, 368 U.S. 487, 493 (1962); Waley v. Johnston, 316 U.S. 101, 104-05 (1942).

The Ninth Circuit's imposition of a distinct "reasoned choice" requirement therefore lacks foundation in the Due Process Clause. The Clause guarantees only that a defendant not be tried or permitted to plead while lacking the capacity described in Dusky. Because the state courts applied that standard and found Moran competent, the judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
