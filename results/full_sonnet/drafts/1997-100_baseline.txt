ARGUMENT

I. THE DOUBLE JEOPARDY CLAUSE FORBIDS A SECOND ATTEMPT TO PROVE A SENTENCING ALLEGATION ONCE THE STATE HAS FAILED TO CARRY ITS BURDEN IN A TRIAL-LIKE PROCEEDING.

The Double Jeopardy Clause of the Fifth Amendment, applicable to the States through the Fourteenth Amendment, Benton v. Maryland, 395 U.S. 784, 796 (1969), protects "against a second prosecution for the same offense after acquittal." North Carolina v. Pearce, 395 U.S. 711, 717 (1969). This protection is not confined to guilt-innocence determinations. In Bullington v. Missouri, 451 U.S. 430 (1981), this Court held that the Clause bars a second capital sentencing proceeding once a jury has already determined, after a trial-like adversarial hearing bounded by evidentiary standards and a reasonable-doubt burden of proof, that the State failed to prove facts necessary to impose the death penalty. The Court explained that Missouri's capital sentencing procedure "resembles the trial on guilt or innocence" because it required the prosecution "to prove additional facts beyond a reasonable doubt" before a harsher penalty could be imposed. Id. at 438. Because the jury's decision "was no less an acquittal . . . than would have been a verdict of acquittal" at the guilt phase, id. at 445, retrial was barred.

The reasoning of Bullington was not limited by anything intrinsic to capital punishment; it turned on the trial-like character of the sentencing proceeding itself. This Court confirmed that understanding three years later in Arizona v. Rumsey, 467 U.S. 203 (1984), holding that once a sentencer has determined that the State failed to prove the requisite aggravating factor, "the interests underlying the Double Jeopardy Clause" bar reconsideration of that finding, id. at 211, because the proceeding possessed "hallmarks of the trial on guilt or innocence." Id. at 209. Nothing in either opinion suggests that the Fifth Amendment's protection against repeated attempts to prove the same fact evaporates merely because the sentencing enhancement is a doubled prison term rather than a death sentence.

II. CALIFORNIA'S PRIOR-CONVICTION PROCEEDING BEARS EVERY "HALLMARK" OF A TRIAL THAT BULLINGTON DEEMED SUFFICIENT TO TRIGGER DOUBLE JEOPARDY PROTECTION.

California's own law confirms that a prior-conviction trial under the Three Strikes law is functionally indistinguishable from the capital sentencing proceeding in Bullington. The State must prove the prior conviction allegation beyond a reasonable doubt. People v. Tenner, 6 Cal. 4th 559, 566 (1993). The defendant enjoys the right to a jury trial, Cal. Penal Code § 1025; the privilege against self-incrimination, In re Yurko, 10 Cal. 3d 857, 863 n.5 (1974); the right to confront and cross-examine adverse witnesses, People v. Reed, 13 Cal. 4th 217, 228 n.6 (1996); and the protection of the ordinary rules of evidence, id. at 224. These are precisely the procedural markers—burden of proof beyond a reasonable doubt, an adversarial hearing, and evidentiary formality—that led this Court in Bullington to treat the capital sentencing hearing as a "trial" for double jeopardy purposes. 451 U.S. at 438. There is no principled basis for treating a proceeding with all of Bullington's hallmarks differently simply because the resulting punishment is imprisonment rather than death. The Double Jeopardy Clause's text draws no such line: it forecloses being "twice put in jeopardy," full stop, once the government has had its "one fair opportunity to offer whatever proof it could assemble." Burks v. United States, 437 U.S. 1, 16 (1978).

Indeed, Burks establishes the controlling principle outside the capital context altogether: when a reviewing court determines that the evidence introduced at trial was legally insufficient to sustain a conviction, "the only 'just' remedy" is a judgment of acquittal, not a new trial to try again. Id. at 18. The Court of Appeal here found precisely that kind of insufficiency—the State's proof failed to establish that Monge personally used a deadly weapon or personally inflicted great bodily injury, elements required to sustain the enhancement. That failure of proof is constitutionally equivalent to an acquittal, and Burks forbids giving the prosecution a second chance to marshal evidence it should have presented the first time.

III. NO PRINCIPLED DISTINCTION SUPPORTS CONFINING DOUBLE JEOPARDY PROTECTION TO CAPITAL SENTENCING.

The California Supreme Court's reliance on Caspari v. Bohlen, 510 U.S. 383 (1994), is misplaced. Caspari addressed only whether extending Bullington to noncapital sentencing would constitute a "new rule" barred by Teague v. Lane, 489 U.S. 288 (1989), on habeas review; it did not hold, and could not have held on that procedural posture, that the Double Jeopardy Clause is inapplicable to noncapital sentencing enhancements. This Court's observation that "our reasoning in [Bullington and Rumsey] was based largely on the unique circumstances of a capital sentencing proceeding," Caspari, 510 U.S. at 392, described the facts of those cases; it did not announce a categorical rule confining double jeopardy protection to death cases.

Nor can such a rule be justified by reference to the heightened stakes of capital punishment. The Double Jeopardy Clause protects a defendant from repeated attempts to convict or, as here, from repeated attempts to secure an enhanced term of imprisonment once the State has already failed to prove the necessary predicate facts. The "anxiety and insecurity" that the Clause is designed to prevent, Green v. United States, 355 U.S. 184, 187 (1957), attend a second attempt to double a term of imprisonment no less than a second attempt to secure death. Once California chose to structure its enhancement proceeding as a trial-like adversarial hearing with a reasonable-doubt standard, it triggered the very protections Bullington identified. This Court should hold that the Double Jeopardy Clause bars retrial of the prior-conviction allegation and reverse the judgment below.
