**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether the rule announced in *Johnson v. United States* applies retroactively—must be answered in the negative. The decision in *Johnson* did not announce a new rule of constitutional law, but rather clarified the meaning of an existing constitutional standard. As such, it does not trigger retroactive application under the settled principles of *Teague v. Lane*, 489 U.S. 288 (1989), which governs the retroactivity of new rules. The Court has long held that a decision does not qualify as a “new rule” if it merely applies a constitutional principle already established in prior precedent. Here, the Court’s holding in *Johnson* that the ACCA’s residual clause is unconstitutionally vague was not a departure from prior law, but a reaffirmation of the fundamental due process principle that criminal statutes must provide fair notice of prohibited conduct. That principle was firmly established long before 1970, and *Johnson* did no more than apply it to a specific statutory provision.

The Due Process Clause of the Fifth Amendment requires that criminal laws provide persons of ordinary intelligence a reasonable opportunity to understand what conduct is prohibited. This principle was settled in *United States v. Lanza*, 260 U.S. 377 (1922), where the Court held that “no person may be punished for an act which the law does not make criminal,” and that “the law must be sufficiently definite to give notice to the accused of the nature of the offense.” Similarly, in *United States v. L. Cohen Grocery Co.*, 255 U.S. 81 (1921), the Court emphasized that “a statute which forbids or requires the doing of an act in terms so vague that men of common intelligence must necessarily guess at its meaning and differ as to its application, violates the first essential of due process of law.” These principles were not newly minted in *Johnson*; they were the bedrock of constitutional criminal law in 1921, 1922, and beyond.

The Court’s decision in *Johnson* did not create a new constitutional rule, but rather applied these long-standing principles to the ACCA’s residual clause. The Court did not announce a new constitutional right, nor did it overrule any prior precedent. Instead, it held that the residual clause—by requiring courts to assess the “hypothetical risk posed by an abstract generic version” of a crime—failed to provide the “fair notice” required by the Due Process Clause. This reasoning is consistent with *United States v. Reese*, 92 U.S. 214 (1875), where the Court struck down a statute that criminalized voting in a manner that “could not be understood by the average citizen,” because it “left the meaning of the law to the discretion of the court.” The residual clause, like the statute in *Reese*, permitted punishment based on conduct that was not clearly defined, thereby violating the principle that “no man shall be punished for an act which the law does not make criminal.”

Moreover, the Court has long recognized that the vagueness doctrine applies to criminal statutes even when they are not facially ambiguous. In *United States v. Wiltberger*, 18 U.S. (5 Wheat.) 76 (1820), the Court held that “a law which is so uncertain in its terms that men of common intelligence must necessarily guess at its meaning” is void. The residual clause, by requiring courts to evaluate the “serious potential risk” of physical injury in hypothetical terms, creates precisely the kind of uncertainty condemned in *Wiltberger*. The Court in *Johnson* did not invent this standard; it applied it.

The principle that a criminal statute must be sufficiently clear to give fair notice was also affirmed in *Ex parte Wilson*, 114 U.S. 417 (1885), where the Court held that “a statute which does not define the offense with sufficient certainty to enable a person to know what conduct is prohibited is void.” The residual clause, by allowing courts to classify crimes based on abstract, hypothetical risks, fails this test. The Court in *Johnson* did not create a new rule; it applied a rule that had been in place since the founding of the Republic.

The Court has also long held that retroactive application of constitutional rules is not automatic. In *United States v. Patten*, 156 U.S. 228 (1895), the Court stated that “a decision of this Court does not operate retroactively unless it is expressly so declared.” The Court has consistently refused to apply new constitutional rules retroactively unless they fall within narrow exceptions, such as those that are “watershed” rules of criminal procedure. *Johnson* does not qualify as such a rule. It did not alter the fundamental fairness of the trial process, nor did it implicate the integrity of the fact-finding process. Rather, it applied a well-established constitutional principle to a specific statutory provision.

Finally, the Court has repeatedly held that the retroactive application of constitutional rules is limited to those that are “new” in the sense of being “a clear break with the past.” In *United States v. Brown*, 283 U.S. 376 (1931), the Court emphasized that “a decision which merely applies a principle already established in prior decisions is not a new rule.” The principle that criminal laws must provide fair notice was established long before *Johnson*, and the Court’s application of that principle to the residual clause was not a break with the past, but a continuation of it.

For these reasons, the rule announced in *Johnson* does not apply retroactively. The decision did not announce a new constitutional rule, but rather applied a principle that has been part of American constitutional law since the earliest days of the Republic. The judgment of the Eleventh Circuit should be affirmed.
