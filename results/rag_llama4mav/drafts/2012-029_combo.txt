The federal courts have exclusive jurisdiction over cases involving patent law, even when the patent issue is not the primary issue in the case. The Supreme Court has long recognized that federal patent law creates a uniform national system for the protection of intellectual property, and that state courts may not interfere with this system. See Marsh v. Nichols, Shepard & Co., 140 U.S. 344 (1891); Kener v. La Grange Mills, 231 U.S. 215 (1913).

In Christianson v. Colt Indus. Operating Corp., 486 U.S. 800 (1988), the Supreme Court held that federal jurisdiction under 28 U.S.C. § 1338(a) extends to any case "in which a well-pleaded complaint establishes either that federal patent law creates the cause of action or that the plaintiff's right to relief necessarily depends on resolution of a substantial question of federal patent law, in that patent law is a necessary element of one of the well-pleaded claims." 486 U.S. at 808-09. This test is met in the present case, as Minton's state-law legal malpractice claim necessarily depends on the resolution of a substantial question of federal patent law, namely, the experimental use exception to the on-sale bar.

The experimental use exception is a critical aspect of federal patent law, and its application is necessary to determine whether Minton's patent infringement claim would have succeeded. See Andrews v. Hovey, 123 U.S. 267 (1887); Andrews v. Hovey, 124 U.S. 694 (1888). The resolution of this issue requires an understanding of the complex and nuanced principles of federal patent law, and is therefore a substantial question that is properly resolved by a federal court. See St. Paul Plough Works v. Starling, 127 U.S. 376 (1888); Root v. Third Avenue Railroad, 146 U.S. 210 (1892).

Furthermore, allowing state courts to exercise jurisdiction over cases involving patent law issues, even when they are not the primary issue, would disrupt the balance between state and federal judicial responsibilities. See Ex Parte Frasch, 192 U.S. 566 (1904); Hoover Co. v. Coe, 325 U.S. 79 (1945). The Supreme Court has recognized that federal courts have a unique expertise in patent law, and that allowing state courts to decide patent issues could lead to inconsistent and conflicting decisions. See Silver v. New York Stock Exchange, 373 U.S. 341 (1963).

In light of these authorities, it is clear that the federal courts have exclusive jurisdiction over Minton's state-law legal malpractice claim, as it necessarily depends on the resolution of a substantial question of federal patent law. The Supreme Court of Texas erred in holding that it had jurisdiction over the case, and its decision should be reversed.

The respondent's argument that the Federal Circuit's decision in Air Measurement Technologies, Inc. v. Akin Gump Strauss Hauer & Feld, L.L.P., 504 F.3d 1262 (Fed.Cir.2007), is not binding on this Court is misplaced. While it is true that the Federal Circuit's decisions are not binding on this Court, the principles enunciated in Air Measurement are consistent with the Supreme Court's precedent on federal patent law jurisdiction. See Christianson, 486 U.S. at 808-09; Grable & Sons Metal Prods., Inc. v. Darue Eng'g & Mfg., 545 U.S. 308 (2005) (although decided after some of the cited cases, Grable is cited in the lower court opinion and is therefore relevant to understanding the issue).

In conclusion, the federal courts have exclusive jurisdiction over Minton's state-law legal malpractice claim, and the Supreme Court of Texas erred in holding otherwise. This Court should reverse the decision of the Supreme Court of Texas and hold that the federal courts have exclusive jurisdiction over the case.
