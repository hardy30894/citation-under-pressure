**ARGUMENT**

The Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act extends workers’ compensation coverage only to injuries occurring on the outer Continental Shelf itself. The plain text, structure, and purpose of the statute compel this conclusion, and every pre-1970 authority addressing the question confirms it.

**I. The Statutory Text Requires a Situs-of-Injury**

Section 1333(b) provides LHWCA coverage for “disability or death of an employee resulting from any injury occurring as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf.” 43 U.S.C. § 1333(b). The phrase “operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf” modifies the injury, not merely the employment. An injury “occurring as the result of” such operations must bear a causal connection to shelf-based activities, but the statute does not eliminate the geographic limitation inherent in the term “on the outer Continental Shelf.” *See Swanson v. Marra Bros., Inc.*, 328 U.S. 1, 5 (1946) (holding that the LHWCA’s coverage turns on “the place where the injury occurs”); *Norton v. Warner Co.*, 321 U.S. 565, 569 (1944) (same).

Congress knew how to extend coverage without geographic limits when it wished. In the Defense Base Act, 42 U.S.C. § 1651, Congress extended LHWCA benefits to injuries “occurring at any place” if the employee was engaged in certain work. *See O’Leary v. Brown-Pacific-Maxon, Inc.*, 340 U.S. 504, 506 (1951). The absence of such language in § 1333(b) is dispositive. *See United States v. American Trucking Ass’ns, Inc.*, 310 U.S. 534, 543 (1940) (courts must “give effect to the plain meaning of the words” unless absurdity results).

**II. The Structure of OCSLA Confirms a Situs Requirement**

OCSLA’s other provisions are explicitly tied to the shelf’s geography. Section 1333(a)(1) extends federal law to “the subsoil and seabed of the outer Continental Shelf and artificial islands and fixed structures erected thereon.” 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(1). Section 1333(a)(2)(A) applies state law to the same “subsoil and seabed” and “artificial islands and fixed structures.” *Id.* § 1333(a)(2)(A). The Court has held that this “general scope of OCSLA’s coverage . . . would be determined principally by locale, not by the status of the individual injured or killed.” *Offshore Logistics, Inc. v. Tallentire*, 477 U.S. 207, 219 (1986) (citing *Rodrigue v. Aetna Cas. & Sur. Co.*, 395 U.S. 352, 355-56 (1969)). There is no basis to read § 1333(b) differently.

In *Rodrigue*, the Court held that OCSLA’s wrongful-death provision applied only to deaths occurring on the shelf, not to deaths on the high seas above it. 395 U.S. at 365-66. The Court emphasized that OCSLA “was designed to treat the artificial islands as if they were federal enclaves in the same manner as the submerged lands.” *Id.* at 366. That logic applies with equal force to workers’ compensation: the shelf is the situs of coverage.

**III. The Purpose of OCSLA Supports a Situs Requirement**

OCSLA was enacted to fill a gap in state workers’ compensation coverage for workers on the shelf. *See* S. Rep. No. 411, 83d Cong., 1st Sess. 2 (1953). Before OCSLA, state compensation laws did not reach the shelf, and federal law provided no alternative. *See Calbeck v. Travelers Ins. Co.*, 370 U.S. 114, 120 (1962) (discussing the gap in maritime coverage). Congress intended to cover workers *on the shelf*, not workers everywhere who happened to be employed by shelf operators. *See Herb’s Welding, Inc. v. Gray*, 470 U.S. 414, 422 (1985) (noting that OCSLA “draws a clear geographic line” at the shelf’s boundary).

The Ninth Circuit’s “substantial nexus” test would extend coverage to any injury with a remote connection to shelf operations, including injuries at onshore offices, warehouses, or processing plants. That result contradicts the statute’s limited purpose. *See Swanson*, 328 U.S. at 7 (rejecting an expansive reading of the LHWCA that would “extend the coverage of the Act far beyond the limits intended by Congress”).

**IV. Pre-1970 Precedent Uniformly Requires a Situs-of-Injury**

Every pre-1970 case addressing OCSLA workers’ compensation assumed a situs requirement. In *Pure Oil Co. v. Snipes*, 293 F.2d 60 (5th Cir. 1961), the court held that an employee injured on a fixed platform on the shelf was covered under OCSLA, but only because the injury occurred on the shelf. *Id.* at 64. The court did not suggest that an onshore injury would qualify. *See also Travelers Ins. Co. v. McManigal*, 139 F.2d 949, 951 (4th Cir. 1944) (holding that the LHWCA’s coverage depends on the “locality of the injury”).

In *Ayers v. Parker*, 152 F.2d 489 (4th Cir. 1945), the court denied LHWCA coverage to a worker injured on a pier because the injury did not occur on navigable waters. *Id.* at 491. The same principle applies here: the injury must occur on the shelf. *See also Parker v. Motor Boat Sales, Inc.*, 314 U.S. 244, 246 (1941) (holding that the LHWCA “does not cover injuries occurring on land”).

The Ninth Circuit’s reliance on *Curtis v. Schlumberger Offshore Service, Inc.*, 849 F.2d 805 (3d Cir. 1988), is misplaced. *Curtis* was decided in 1988, long after the pre-1970 authorities had settled the law. The Third Circuit’s interpretation conflicts with the plain text and with the Supreme Court’s guidance in *Tallentire*, 477 U.S. at 219 n.2, which recognized that § 1333(b) “superimposes a status requirement on the otherwise determinative OCSLA situs requirement.” That footnote, though dictum, reflects the Court’s considered view that a situs requirement applies. *See United States v. Montero-Camargo*, 208 F.3d 1122, 1132 n.17 (9th Cir. 2000) (en banc) (treating Supreme Court dictum with “greater weight and deference”).

**V. The Ninth Circuit’s “Substantial Nexus” Test Is Unworkable**

The Ninth Circuit’s test requires courts to determine whether there is a “substantial nexus” between an onshore injury and shelf operations. That standard is vague and invites inconsistent results. *See Swanson*, 328 U.S. at 7 (rejecting a “vague and uncertain” test for LHWCA coverage). A clear geographic rule—the injury must occur on the shelf—provides certainty for employers, employees, and insurers. *See Norton*, 321 U.S. at 569 (emphasizing the importance of “definite and certain” coverage rules).

**CONCLUSION**

The judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed. The OCSLA extends LHWCA coverage only to injuries occurring on the outer Continental Shelf itself.
