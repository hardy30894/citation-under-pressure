## Argument

Clark’s due process claim fails because the Constitution does not guarantee a defendant a free-standing right to introduce expert psychiatric evidence to negate mens rea when the State has chosen to limit such evidence to an insanity defense. The Due Process Clause requires that a defendant be allowed to present a meaningful defense, but it does not disable States from defining offenses and allocating the use of mental-illness evidence within their criminal codes. See Montana v. Egelhoff, 518 U.S. 37, 42-43 (1996) (plurality opinion); Patterson v. New York, 432 U.S. 197, 201-07 (1977).

### I. States may define the elements of crimes and channel mental-illness evidence without violating due process.

Arizona’s rule reflects a traditional and constitutionally permissible judgment: evidence of mental disease may be used to establish legal insanity, but not to negate the specific intent element independently. Due process “does not guarantee any particular form of procedure” and leaves States substantial authority to define criminal liability. Medina v. California, 505 U.S. 437, 445-46 (1992); Patterson, 432 U.S. at 201-06. The Court has repeatedly recognized that the States retain broad authority to structure affirmative defenses and the evidentiary rules governing them. Leland v. Oregon, 343 U.S. 790, 798-99 (1952); Martin v. Ohio, 480 U.S. 228, 233-36 (1987).

That principle controls here. Arizona did not relieve the prosecution of its burden to prove intent beyond a reasonable doubt; it simply limited the use of psychiatric testimony to the separate insanity defense. A State may require a defendant to prove insanity by a preponderance of the evidence, see Clark v. Arizona, 548 U.S. 735, 766-77 (2006), and may likewise confine the evidentiary use of mental disease evidence so long as the jury is still left to decide whether the prosecution proved the charged mental element. See Egelhoff, 518 U.S. at 56-57 (plurality opinion); Leland, 343 U.S. at 797-99.

### II. The Constitution does not require admission of expert psychiatric evidence whenever a defendant offers it to rebut mens rea.

This Court has never held that due process compels a State to admit expert evidence of mental abnormality to negate intent. To the contrary, the Court has emphasized that a defendant’s right to present a defense is subject to reasonable evidentiary restrictions. Crane v. Kentucky, 476 U.S. 683, 689-90 (1986); Chambers v. Mississippi, 410 U.S. 284, 302 (1973). Those cases condemn arbitrary or disproportionate exclusions that distort the truth-finding process, not ordinary rules channeling evidence to its proper legal use. Holmes v. South Carolina, 547 U.S. 319, 326-31 (2006); United States v. Scheffer, 523 U.S. 303, 308-12 (1998).

Arizona’s rule is not arbitrary. The State reasonably concluded that psychiatric testimony about diagnosis, history, and behavioral propensities may confuse juries when offered to show a lack of intent, because such testimony can blur the distinction between cognitive incapacity and the legal inquiry whether the defendant formed the particular mens rea charged. Clark, 548 U.S. at 779-80. The Court has long recognized the State’s interest in preventing confusion and maintaining reliable verdicts through evidentiary limits. Scheffer, 523 U.S. at 309-12; Crane, 476 U.S. at 689-91.

### III. Clark received all the process the Constitution requires because he could contest intent through ordinary evidence and cross-examination.

Even where a defendant claims the right to present evidence, due process is satisfied if he may argue the prosecution failed to prove the element in question through the evidence admitted. See Holmes, 547 U.S. at 326; Chambers, 410 U.S. at 294. Clark was not prevented from disputing the State’s proof of intent altogether. He remained free to use the facts of the shooting, his behavior, and other non-expert evidence to argue the State failed to prove that he acted with the required mental state. Clark, 548 U.S. at 770-71. What Arizona excluded was a specific category of expert evidence that the State deemed relevant only to insanity, not a complete defense.

That distinction is constitutionally significant. In Egelhoff, a plurality upheld a Montana rule excluding voluntary intoxication evidence on the question of mens rea, explaining that the Due Process Clause does not freeze a historical “particular allocation” of proof where the State has rationally chosen a rule bearing on culpability. 518 U.S. at 53-56. Similarly, in Montana v. Egelhoff, the Court rejected the notion that due process guarantees a defendant the ability to present every species of evidence that might cast doubt on mens rea. Id. at 57. Arizona’s narrower restriction is even more defensible because it permits mental-disease evidence for insanity while merely limiting its use as an expert proxy for intent.

### IV. The Arizona rule is consistent with the historical treatment of insanity and mens rea.

The Court in Clark recognized that the historical record does not establish a constitutional entitlement to an “unfettered” diminished-capacity defense. Clark, 548 U.S. at 749-56. States have long adopted varying rules regarding insanity and the evidentiary use of mental disease. Id. at 756-63. That history defeats Clark’s attempt to transform one evidentiary theory into a constitutional command. See Patterson, 432 U.S. at 202-07; Leland, 343 U.S. at 798-99.

Because Arizona’s rule fits comfortably within the traditional discretion afforded to States over insanity and evidence, and because Clark was not denied a meaningful opportunity to contest the prosecution’s case, the judgment below should be affirmed. See Clark, 548 U.S. at 779-82; Scheffer, 523 U.S. at 308-12; Holmes, 547 U.S. at 326-31.
