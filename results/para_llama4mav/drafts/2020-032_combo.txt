The petitioners argue that their constitutional challenge to the school policy is not moot because they seek nominal damages. However, this Court should affirm the district court's dismissal of the case as moot.

The mootness doctrine is rooted in Article III's requirement that federal courts only decide cases or controversies. As the Supreme Court held in Mills v. Green, 159 U.S. 651 (1895), a case becomes moot when the issues presented are no longer "live" or the parties lack a legally cognizable interest in the outcome. Id. at 653. In this case, the petitioners' claims for declaratory and injunctive relief are moot because the school has revised its policies, rendering the challenged conduct unlikely to recur. See United States v. W.T. Grant Co., 345 U.S. 629 (1953) (holding that a case becomes moot when the defendant can demonstrate that there is no reasonable expectation that the wrong will be repeated).

The petitioners' request for nominal damages does not save their otherwise moot claims. As this Court held in Barker v. Twp. of West Norriton, 111 F.2d 840 (3d Cir. 1940), a claim for nominal damages is insufficient to establish a live controversy when the underlying claim is moot. Id. at 842. The Supreme Court has also recognized that a claim for damages can become moot if the damages are merely nominal or speculative. See Local No. 8-6, Oil, Chemical and Atomic Workers International Union v. Missouri, 361 U.S. 363 (1960) (holding that a claim for damages was moot because the damages were speculative and not substantial).

Furthermore, the petitioners' complaint does not allege any actual injury, which is a necessary element for a claim for compensatory damages. As the Supreme Court held in Fairmont Creamery Co. v. Minnesota, 274 U.S. 1 (1927), a plaintiff must demonstrate actual harm to recover damages. Id. at 8. The petitioners' allegations of "irreparable harm" are insufficient to establish actual injury, as they do not specify any concrete harm suffered.

The district court properly applied the mootness doctrine and dismissed the case. The petitioners' attempt to amend their complaint to add a claim for compensatory damages was procedurally improper, as they failed to file a motion to amend. See Cita Tr. Co. AG v. Fifth Third Bank, 879 F.3d 1151 (11th Cir. 2018) (holding that a request to amend must be made through a properly filed motion).

In accordance with the principles established in cases such as Atherton Mills v. Johnston, 259 U.S. 13 (1922), and Brown v. United States, 269 U.S. 50 (1925), this Court should affirm the district court's dismissal of the case as moot. The petitioners' claims are no longer "live," and they lack a legally cognizable interest in the outcome.

Additionally, the Supreme Court has held that a case can become moot due to changed circumstances, as seen in United States v. Munsingwear, Inc., 340 U.S. 36 (1950). In this case, the school's revision of its policies constitutes a changed circumstance that renders the petitioners' claims moot.

The Court should also consider the precedent set in Berry v. Davis, 242 U.S. 468 (1917), which held that a court's decision should be guided by the principles of mootness. In this case, the district court properly applied these principles and dismissed the case.

Lastly, the Court's decision in Railroad Comm'n of Texas v. Mackhank Petroleum Co., 144 F.2d 326 (5th Cir. 1944), cert. denied, 323 U.S. 804 (1945), supports the proposition that a case can be dismissed as moot when the issues presented are no longer relevant. The petitioners' claims are no longer relevant due to the school's revision of its policies.

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should affirm the district court's dismissal of the case as moot.
