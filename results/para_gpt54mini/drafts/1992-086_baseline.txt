### ARGUMENT

The District’s refusal to let Lamb’s Chapel use school premises for an after-hours film series violated the First Amendment. When the government opens its property for expressive activity, it may not exclude speech because of its religious viewpoint absent a constitutionally sufficient justification. *Perry Educ. Ass’n v. Perry Local Educators’ Ass’n*, 460 U.S. 37, 44–46 (1983); *Cornelius v. NAACP Legal Def. & Educ. Fund, Inc.*, 473 U.S. 788, 802 (1985).

The school facilities here were not a traditional public forum, but the District deliberately opened them for community expressive use after hours. Once it did so, it created at least a limited public forum, and restrictions within that forum must be reasonable and viewpoint neutral. *Perry*, 460 U.S. at 45–46; *Widmar v. Vincent*, 454 U.S. 263, 267–70 (1981). The District’s rule barring use “for religious purposes” is not viewpoint neutral. It does not simply regulate a subject matter; it singles out a religious perspective for exclusion from a forum otherwise available for public discussion. The First Amendment forbids that discrimination. *Rosenberger v. Rector & Visitors of the Univ. of Va.*, 515 U.S. 819, 828–31 (1995); *Lamb’s Chapel v. Ctr. Moriches Union Free Sch. Dist.*, 508 U.S. 384, 392–93 (1993).

That is exactly what happened here. Lamb’s Chapel sought to show a film series about family values and child rearing from a Christian perspective. The District did not deny access because the topic was outside the forum’s permissible uses; it denied access because the presentation was “church related.” That is viewpoint discrimination, because the same subject matter—family life—was otherwise permitted, but one religious perspective on that subject was excluded. *Lamb’s Chapel*, 508 U.S. at 393–94; *Rosenberger*, 515 U.S. at 831–32. As this Court has explained, the government may not “regulat[e] speech when the specific motivating ideology or the opinion or perspective of the speaker is the rationale for the restriction.” *Rosenberger*, 515 U.S. at 829.

The Second Circuit erred by treating the District’s exclusion of all religious uses as constitutionally permissible simply because the District had not previously admitted religious groups. But a forum is not closed to a viewpoint just because the government has historically suppressed it; what matters is how the forum is opened and the terms under which it is made available. *Cornelius*, 473 U.S. at 802–06; *Widmar*, 454 U.S. at 267–70. Once the District invited community organizations to use school property for expressive activities, it could not reserve the forum for secular viewpoints alone. *Lamb’s Chapel*, 508 U.S. at 392–93.

Nor does the Establishment Clause justify the exclusion. The Supreme Court has repeatedly rejected the notion that allowing religious speakers equal access to a generally available forum amounts to establishment of religion. *Widmar*, 454 U.S. at 271–75; *Board of Educ. of the Westside Cmty. Schs. v. Mergens*, 496 U.S. 226, 248–53 (1990); *Good News Club v. Milford Cent. Sch.*, 533 U.S. 98, 113–19 (2001). Neutral access does not constitute sponsorship. To the contrary, excluding religious expression from an otherwise open forum itself threatens First Amendment neutrality. *Rosenberger*, 515 U.S. at 839–46.

The District may argue that the school is a limited public forum and may restrict access by subject matter. But even in a limited forum, exclusions must be viewpoint neutral. *Perry*, 460 U.S. at 46; *Cornelius*, 473 U.S. at 806; *Good News Club*, 533 U.S. at 106–07. Here, the District permitted outside community use while forbidding only religious use of the same subject matter. That is not a permissible subject-matter line; it is a forbidden religious viewpoint exclusion. *Lamb’s Chapel*, 508 U.S. at 393–94; *Rosenberger*, 515 U.S. at 830–31.

This Court should therefore reverse. The First Amendment does not permit a school district to open its facilities to community speech and then deny access because the speaker presents a religious perspective. *Lamb’s Chapel*, 508 U.S. at 393–95.
