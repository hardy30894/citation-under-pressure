**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE SCHOOL-ZONE STATUTE IS UNCONSTITUTIONALLY VAGUE BECAUSE IT FAILS TO SPECIFY THE METHOD FOR MEASURING THE 1,000-FOOT DISTANCE FROM A SCHOOL.**

The Due Process Clause of the Fifth Amendment requires that criminal statutes “define the criminal offense with sufficient definiteness that ordinary people can understand what conduct is prohibited.” *Kolender v. Lawson*, 461 U.S. 352, 357 (1983). A statute is void for vagueness if it “either forbids or requires the doing of an act in terms so vague that men of common intelligence must necessarily guess at its meaning and differ as to its application.” *United States v. Petrillo*, 332 U.S. 1, 7 (1947). The school-zone statute, 18 U.S.C. § 922(q)(2)(A), fails this standard because it does not specify whether the 1,000-foot distance should be measured “in a straight line, along the ground, or following the path of travel.” *United States v. Edgerton*, 75 F.3d 47, 50 (2d Cir. 1996). This ambiguity invites arbitrary enforcement and leaves individuals unable to determine whether their conduct falls within the prohibited zone. *See id.* (holding that a similar ambiguity in a school-zone statute rendered it unconstitutionally vague).

This Court should follow the reasoning of the Second Circuit in *Edgerton*, which concluded that a statute lacking a clear measurement method is unconstitutionally vague. *Id.* at 51. The Supreme Court has repeatedly emphasized that “[t]he void-for-vagueness doctrine requires that a penal statute define the criminal offense with sufficient definiteness that ordinary people can understand what conduct is prohibited.” *Kolender*, 461 U.S. at 357. Section 922(q)(2)(A) fails this test because it does not specify the measurement method, leaving individuals to guess whether their conduct is lawful. *See Edgerton*, 75 F.3d at 50.

**II. THE EVIDENCE WAS INSUFFICIENT TO ESTABLISH THE REQUIRED MENS REA FOR THE MACHINE-GUN POSSESSION CHARGE.**

To sustain a conviction under 18 U.S.C. § 922(o), the government must prove that the defendant knowingly possessed a machine gun. *Staples v. United States*, 511 U.S. 600, 602 (1994). The government’s evidence here was insufficient because it failed to establish that Nieves-Castaño knew the firearm was a machine gun.

In *Staples*, the Supreme Court held that “the Government must prove that the defendant knew the characteristics of the weapon that brought it within the statutory definition.” *Id.* at 619. Here, the government’s evidence showed only that Nieves-Castaño knew the firearm was an AK-47, not that she knew it was a machine gun. *See id.* (requiring proof of knowledge of the weapon’s characteristics). The government did not present evidence that AK-47s are inherently machine guns or that Nieves-Castaño knew the firearm’s functional capabilities. *See id.* at 615 (noting that the government must prove knowledge of the weapon’s characteristics).

The government’s reliance on *United States v. X-Citement Video*, 513 U.S. 64 (1994), is misplaced. In *X-Citement Video*, the Supreme Court held that knowledge of the proscribed conduct is required, but the statute at issue there explicitly required proof of knowledge. *Id.* at 70. Section 922(o), by contrast, does not explicitly require proof of knowledge, and the Supreme Court has held that such knowledge is nonetheless required. *Staples*, 511 U.S. at 619. The government’s evidence here was insufficient to meet this standard.

**III. THE EVIDENCE WAS INSUFFICIENT TO ESTABLISH THE REQUIRED MENS REA FOR THE SCHOOL-ZONE POSSESSION CHARGE.**

To sustain a conviction under 18 U.S.C. § 922(q)(2)(A), the government must prove that the defendant knowingly possessed a firearm in a school zone. *United States v. Lopez*, 514 U.S. 549, 562 (1995). The government’s evidence here was insufficient because it failed to establish that Nieves-Castaño knew the firearm was in a school zone.

In *Lopez*, the Supreme Court held that the government must prove that the defendant knew the firearm was in a school zone. *Id.* at 562. Here, the government’s evidence showed only that Nieves-Castaño possessed the firearm in her apartment, not that she knew it was in a school zone. *See id.* (requiring proof of knowledge of the firearm’s location). The government did not present evidence that Nieves-Castaño knew the location of her apartment relative to a school. *See id.* at 562 (noting that the government must prove knowledge of the firearm’s location).

The government’s reliance on *United States v. Genao*, 79 F.3d 1333 (2d Cir. 1996), is misplaced. In *Genao*, the Second Circuit held that knowledge of the firearm’s location is not required, but the statute at issue there explicitly required proof of knowledge. *Id.* at 1336. Section 922(q)(2)(A), by contrast, does not explicitly require proof of knowledge, and the Supreme Court has held that such knowledge is nonetheless required. *Lopez*, 514 U.S. at 562. The government’s evidence here was insufficient to meet this standard.
