ARGUMENT

I. STELWAGON FAILED TO PROVE ACTUAL ANTITRUST DAMAGES, AND THE TREBLE-DAMAGES AWARD MUST BE VACATED.

This Court reviews the denial of a renewed motion for judgment as a matter of law under the same standard applied by the district court, asking whether the evidence, viewed in the light most favorable to the verdict winner, was sufficient to sustain the jury's finding. Fineman v. Armstrong World Indus., Inc., 980 F.2d 171, 190 (3d Cir. 1992). Sufficiency of proof of damages is a question of law for this Court. Merican, Inc. v. Caterpillar Tractor Co., 713 F.2d 958, 966 (3d Cir. 1983).

Section 4 of the Clayton Act does not award treble damages for the bare fact of a Robinson-Patman violation. A private plaintiff must separately establish (1) a violation of the antitrust laws, (2) injury to the plaintiff proximately caused by that violation, and (3) a measurable amount of damages. J. Truett Payne Co. v. Chrysler Motors Corp., 451 U.S. 557, 562 (1981). As the Supreme Court has explained, a plaintiff seeking treble damages under the Robinson-Patman Act "must prove more than a violation of § 2(a), since such proof establishes only that injury may result." Id. at 562. Proof that a competitor received a lower price does not, by itself, establish that the plaintiff suffered antitrust injury or that the injury is measurable in dollars; the plaintiff must connect the discriminatory pricing to an actual, ascertainable loss. Brooke Group Ltd. v. Brown & Williamson Tobacco Corp., 509 U.S. 209, 225 (1993) (antitrust plaintiff must demonstrate "antitrust injury" flowing from the challenged conduct).

While a jury may make "a just and reasonable estimate of the damage based on relevant data," Zenith Radio Corp. v. Hazeltine Research, Inc., 395 U.S. 100, 114 n.9 (1969) (quoting Bigelow v. RKO Radio Pictures, Inc., 327 U.S. 251, 264 (1946)), that latitude applies only after the plaintiff has laid a proper foundation isolating the effect of the discriminatory pricing from other causes of lost profits. Estimation cannot substitute for proof of causation. Here, Stelwagon's damages case did neither. Stelwagon's expert calculated damages by comparing Stelwagon's gross margins on Tarmac products to the margins Standard and Celotex allegedly enjoyed at the discounted prices, then extrapolated a lost-profits figure across Stelwagon's entire volume of MAP sales for the relevant period. That methodology never isolated the price differential from the myriad other variables affecting Stelwagon's profitability — including its own operating costs, its voluntary decision to forgo other product lines under the exclusive-distributorship arrangement, and independent market conditions in the Philadelphia roofing supply market. The record is devoid of any analysis tying a specific dollar loss to the specific discriminatory prices charged to Standard and Celotex, as opposed to ordinary competitive pressures Stelwagon would have faced regardless of Tarmac's pricing to others.

The district court itself recognized the infirmity of this proof. In granting remittitur, the court found that "the damages awarded by the jury in this case are unsupported by the evidence and are grossly excessive." Stelwagon Mfg. Co. v. Tarmac Roofing Sys., Inc., 862 F. Supp. 1361, 1369 (E.D. Pa. 1994). That finding is not merely a quarrel over degree; it is an acknowledgment that the evidentiary predicate for any damages figure — excessive or otherwise — was lacking. A court may not cure a failure of proof by simply reducing an unsupported number to a smaller unsupported number. Where, as here, the plaintiff's own methodology cannot distinguish antitrust injury from ordinary business risk, no amount of remittitur can convert speculation into the "just and reasonable estimate" that Bigelow and Zenith permit. See Story Parchment Co. v. Paterson Parchment Paper Co., 282 U.S. 555, 562 (1931) (damages must rest on a "certain" fact of injury even where the amount admits of some imprecision).

Because Stelwagon failed to present evidence from which a jury could reasonably calculate damages attributable to the price discrimination itself, as opposed to Stelwagon's overall business performance, judgment as a matter of law should have been entered for Tarmac on the Robinson-Patman claim, or, at minimum, the treble-damages award must be vacated in its entirety.

II. IN THE ALTERNATIVE, THE DISTRICT COURT'S REMITTED DAMAGES FIGURE REMAINS SPECULATIVE AND UNSUPPORTED.

Even if this Court declines to hold that Stelwagon's proof was insufficient as a matter of law, the remitted award of $450,383.60 in antitrust damages cannot stand because it was derived from the very expert methodology the district court itself criticized as unsupported by the evidence. Stelwagon, 862 F. Supp. at 1369. A remittitur must be grounded in record evidence establishing a rational basis for the reduced figure; it cannot simply select an arbitrary fraction of an already-unsupported verdict. See Story Parchment, 282 U.S. at 563 (the plaintiff bears the burden of furnishing a basis for a reasoned estimate of loss). Because no properly isolated evidence of injury caused by the discriminatory pricing exists in this record, any damages figure — whether $2,272,000 or $450,383.60 — rests on the same speculative foundation. This Court should therefore vacate the treble-damages judgment and remand with instructions to enter judgment for Tarmac on the Robinson-Patman claim, or, at minimum, for a new trial limited to damages, at which Stelwagon must present evidence properly attributing lost profits to the challenged price discrimination.

CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment awarding treble damages under Section 4 of the Clayton Act should be vacated.
