# ARGUMENT

**THE DUE PROCESS CLAUSE DOES NOT REQUIRE A HEIGHTENED "REASONED CHOICE" STANDARD OF COMPETENCE FOR A DEFENDANT WHO SEEKS TO PLEAD GUILTY OR WAIVE COUNSEL**

## I. The Competency Standard for Standing Trial Is Constitutionally Sufficient for Pleading Guilty or Waiving Counsel

This Court has long held that the competency standard for standing trial requires that a defendant have "sufficient present ability to consult with his lawyer with a reasonable degree of rational understanding" and "a rational as well as factual understanding of the proceedings against him." *Dusky v. United States*, 362 U.S. 402, 402 (1960) (per curiam). This standard, reaffirmed in *Drope v. Missouri*, 420 U.S. 162, 171 (1975), requires "the capacity to understand the nature and object of the proceedings against him, to consult with counsel, and to assist in preparing his defense." Nothing in *Dusky* or *Drope* suggests that a higher standard applies when a defendant elects to plead guilty or waive counsel rather than proceed to trial.

The Ninth Circuit's "reasoned choice" standard, drawn from *Sieling v. Eyman*, 478 F.2d 211 (9th Cir. 1973), rests on a fundamental misunderstanding of the competency inquiry. Competence to stand trial is not a discrete inquiry into whether a defendant can rationally weigh trial-related decisions; it is a threshold determination of mental capacity that, once established, qualifies a defendant to make the range of decisions that the criminal process demands of him—including the decision to plead guilty or to waive counsel. There is no reason to believe that the decision to plead guilty requires an appreciably higher level of mental functioning than the decision to proceed to trial while assisting counsel.

## II. The Decision to Plead Guilty Involves No Greater Cognitive Demand Than the Decision to Stand Trial

A defendant who stands trial must make myriad decisions of equal or greater complexity than the decision to plead guilty—whether to testify, see *Rock v. Arkansas*, 483 U.S. 44, 49 (1987) (recognizing the defendant's right to testify as fundamental), whether to call witnesses, whether to accept a plea offer, and how to respond to the prosecution's case. Each of these decisions requires the defendant to weigh alternatives and consequences. If the *Dusky* standard suffices for these choices made throughout trial, it necessarily suffices for the single choice to forgo trial altogether.

This Court's waiver jurisprudence confirms that the competency inquiry is separate from the voluntariness inquiry. In *Faretta v. California*, 422 U.S. 806, 835 (1975), the Court held that a defendant has a constitutional right to waive counsel and represent himself, provided the waiver is knowing and voluntary—a standard entirely distinct from mental competence. The *Faretta* Court's concern was whether the defendant was "made aware of the dangers and disadvantages of self-representation," not whether he possessed some heightened cognitive capacity beyond that required to stand trial. *Id.* at 835. Conflating these two inquiries, as the Ninth Circuit did below, improperly imports a mental-capacity requirement into what is fundamentally a voluntariness and awareness inquiry governed by *Johnson v. Zerbst*, 304 U.S. 458, 464 (1938), which asks only whether there was "an intentional relinquishment or abandonment of a known right or privilege."

Similarly, in *Boykin v. Alabama*, 395 U.S. 238, 242-44 (1969), this Court's concern with guilty pleas centered on whether the plea was voluntary and made with understanding of its consequences—not on some elevated competency threshold. The procedural safeguards *Boykin* demands—an on-the-record inquiry into voluntariness and understanding—are wholly separate from, and adequately protect against, the concern that a defendant might plead guilty without adequate mental capacity.

## III. The Ninth Circuit's Standard Cannot Be Reconciled With This Court's Competency Jurisprudence

The court below relied on *Pate v. Robinson*, 383 U.S. 375 (1966), for the proposition that due process requires a competency hearing whenever the evidence raises a bona fide doubt about the defendant's competence. Pet. App. 6-7. But *Pate*'s concern was with competency to stand trial generally, not with some heightened standard applicable only to certain trial-related decisions. Nothing in *Pate* supports bifurcating the competency standard based on which right the defendant chooses to exercise or waive.

Moreover, this Court's decision in *Godinez*'s companion authority, *Westbrook v. Arizona*, 384 U.S. 150 (1966) (per curiam), which the Ninth Circuit has invoked to distinguish competency to waive counsel from competency to stand trial, involved a defendant who had never been examined for competency to waive counsel at all—not a defendant, like respondent, who had already been found competent to stand trial under the *Dusky* standard. *Westbrook* does not establish that a heightened standard applies once the *Dusky* threshold is met.

## IV. A Single Competency Standard Serves the Interests of Reliability and Judicial Administration

Adopting a single, unified competency standard promotes reliability without sacrificing the individualized protections *Boykin* and *Faretta* already provide. Once a defendant is found competent under *Dusky*—possessing a rational and factual understanding of the proceedings and the capacity to consult with counsel—separate inquiries into voluntariness (under *Boykin* and *Zerbst*) and knowing waiver (under *Faretta*) adequately safeguard the defendant's rights. A bifurcated competency standard, by contrast, invites confusion and inconsistent application, as the record below illustrates: the trial court and psychiatric examiners applied the *Dusky* standard, while the Ninth Circuit faulted them for failing to apply an amorphous "reasoned choice" standard that finds no grounding in this Court's precedents.

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Court of Appeals should be reversed.
