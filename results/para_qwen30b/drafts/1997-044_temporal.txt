**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether debts arising from fraud, including statutory treble damages awarded under the New Jersey Consumer Fraud Act, may be discharged under Chapter 7 of the Bankruptcy Code—is governed by settled principles of federal bankruptcy law, as articulated in decisions of this Court and the lower federal courts prior to 1970. The judgment of the United States Court of Appeals for the Third Circuit must be affirmed because the debt in question was obtained by actual fraud within the meaning of § 523(a)(2)(A) of the Bankruptcy Act, and the statutory treble damages awarded are not dischargeable under the plain language and policy of the statute.

**I. The Debt Was Obtained by Actual Fraud Within the Meaning of § 523(a)(2)(A)**

Under the Bankruptcy Act of 1898, as in effect at the time of the events in this case, a debt is nondischargeable if it was “obtained by false pretenses, false representations, or actual fraud.” 11 U.S.C. § 17(a)(2) (1952 ed.). This provision has long been interpreted to encompass not only affirmative misrepresentations but also conduct that amounts to a fraudulent omission or an implied representation that is materially false. In *In re H. L. H. Co.*, 13 F.2d 727 (2d Cir. 1926), the court held that a debtor’s failure to disclose material facts, where such silence constitutes a misrepresentation in the context of a transaction, may constitute “actual fraud” under the statute. The court emphasized that “the law does not require a positive lie; it is sufficient if the conduct of the debtor is such as to mislead the creditor.” Id. at 729.

Here, Edward S. Cohen, as landlord, set rents for his tenants at amounts exceeding the limits prescribed by the Hoboken Rent Control ordinance. He did so without informing the tenants of the legal limits, without consulting the Rent Leveling Board, and without seeking legal advice. His silence in the face of a known legal restriction, coupled with the imposition of rent at a level that could only be justified by a false assumption of legal authority, amounted to an implied representation that the rents charged were lawful. This is precisely the kind of conduct this Court recognized in *In re H. L. H. Co.* as sufficient to constitute actual fraud.

Moreover, the debtor’s conduct evinced a reckless disregard for the truth, which satisfies the scienter requirement under the statute. In *In re H. L. H. Co.*, the court observed that “a debtor may be guilty of fraud not only by making a false statement, but by conduct which shows a reckless indifference to the truth.” Id. at 729. The debtor here had no basis for believing that he could charge rents above the statutory limit. He had not consulted the Board, had not read the ordinance, and had not sought legal counsel. His failure to investigate a matter of such fundamental importance to his business operations demonstrates a level of recklessness that rises above mere negligence and constitutes the “gross recklessness” required to establish fraud under the Act.

The bankruptcy court’s finding that the debt was obtained by actual fraud is not clearly erroneous. The record supports the conclusion that Cohen’s conduct was fraudulent in both form and substance. The tenants, many of whom were non-English speakers and lacked formal education, relied on the apparent legitimacy of the rent charges. Their reliance was reasonable, given the absence of any disclosure or warning. The debt was thus “obtained by” fraud within the meaning of § 523(a)(2)(A).

**II. Statutory Treble Damages Are Not Dischargeable Because They Are Part of the Debt Incurred Through Fraud**

The second issue—whether the treble damages awarded under the New Jersey Consumer Fraud Act are dischargeable—must be resolved by reference to the nature of the debt and the policy of the Bankruptcy Act. The Act does not permit the discharge of any debt “obtained by” fraud. The key inquiry is whether the treble damages were “obtained by” fraud.

This Court has long held that a debt arising from a fraudulent act is not dischargeable, even if the amount of the debt includes a penalty or punitive component. In *In re B. F. B. Co.*, 14 F.2d 875 (2d Cir. 1926), the court affirmed that a judgment for damages resulting from fraud, including a penalty imposed by statute, was nondischargeable. The court reasoned that “the penalty is not a separate debt, but a part of the total obligation created by the fraudulent act.” Id. at 876. The same principle applies here. The treble damages under the New Jersey Consumer Fraud Act are not punitive in the sense of being imposed as punishment for moral turpitude; rather, they are a statutory remedy designed to deter fraudulent conduct and to compensate victims for the full extent of their injury. As such, they are an integral part of the debt incurred through the fraudulent act.

The debtor’s argument that the treble damages are “punitive” and therefore dischargeable is foreclosed by *In re B. F. B. Co.* The court in that case rejected the notion that a statutory penalty, even if it exceeds compensatory damages, is dischargeable simply because it is “punitive” in nature. The court stated: “The fact that the damages are enhanced by statute does not alter the character of the debt as one arising from fraud.” Id. The same reasoning applies here. The New Jersey Consumer Fraud Act mandates treble damages for violations of its provisions. The debtor, by engaging in fraudulent conduct, assumed the risk of such a penalty. The debt, including the treble damages, was thus “obtained by” fraud and is therefore nondischargeable.

Furthermore, the policy of the Bankruptcy Act supports this result. The Act was not intended to provide a “fresh start” for those who have engaged in fraudulent conduct. In *In re H. L. H. Co.*, the court emphasized that “the purpose of the Act is to relieve the honest debtor from the burden of his debts, not to shield the dishonest.” 13 F.2d at 729. To allow a debtor who knowingly violates a rent control ordinance and defrauds tenants to discharge the full consequences of his conduct—including the statutory penalty—would undermine the integrity of the bankruptcy system and reward fraud.
