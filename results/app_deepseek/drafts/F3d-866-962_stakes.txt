**ARGUMENT**

**I. The District Court Erred in Granting Summary Judgment Because Genuine Disputes of Material Fact Exist as to Each Element of Appellants’ FRSA Retaliation Claims.**

This Court reviews a grant of summary judgment de novo. *Torgerson v. City of Rochester*, 643 F.3d 1031, 1042 (8th Cir. 2011). To survive summary judgment, appellants must present evidence from which a reasonable jury could find that (1) they engaged in protected activity, (2) BNSF took adverse action against them, and (3) the protected activity was a contributing factor to the adverse action. *Kuduk v. BNSF Ry. Co.*, 768 F.3d 786, 789 (8th Cir. 2014). The burden then shifts to BNSF to demonstrate by clear and convincing evidence that it would have taken the same action absent the protected activity. *Id.* at 790.

**A. Appellants Engaged in Protected Activity Under 49 U.S.C. § 20109(a)(1)(C).**

The FRSA prohibits retaliation against an employee who “report[s] a hazardous safety condition.” 49 U.S.C. § 20109(a)(1)(C). Appellants reported the bridge’s lack of side rails and lighting—a hazardous condition—in written statements to Trainmaster Dixon on April 2, 2012, and in recorded statements to Claims Representative Murphy on April 5, 12, and 13. These reports are squarely within the statute’s protection. *See BNSF Ry. Co. v. U.S. Dep’t of Labor*, 816 F.3d 678, 684 (8th Cir. 2016) (holding that reporting unsafe working conditions is protected activity). The district court erred in suggesting that appellants’ reports were merely “incidental” to the accident investigation; the record shows they specifically identified the bridge’s dangerous condition.

**B. Appellants Suffered Adverse Actions.**

Adverse actions include discipline, suspension, termination, and increased scrutiny. *Kuduk*, 768 F.3d at 789. Foster was fired; Kline received a thirty-day record suspension and a one-year review period; Snyder received a formal reprimand and a one-year review period. Additionally, appellants faced increased operations testing—unannounced observations by foremen—which they credibly testified caused them to avoid certain coworkers and accept less lucrative assignments. Such heightened surveillance constitutes an adverse action. *See Araujo v. N.J. Transit Rail Operations, Inc.*, 708 F.3d 152, 159 (3d Cir. 2013) (holding that increased monitoring can be retaliatory).

**C. A Genuine Dispute Exists as to Whether Appellants’ Protected Activity Was a Contributing Factor to the Adverse Actions.**

A contributing factor is “any factor which, alone or in combination with other factors, tends to affect in any way the outcome.” *Kuduk*, 768 F.3d at 789 (internal quotation marks omitted). Temporal proximity alone can suffice. *Id.* at 790. Here, the adverse actions followed swiftly after appellants’ reports: the investigation notice issued on April 6, just days after the first reports, and the increased testing began shortly thereafter. Moreover, BNSF’s own witnesses—Dixon and Knutstrom—were the same officials who received the reports and later testified against appellants at the hearing. A jury could infer that BNSF targeted appellants for reporting unsafe conditions, not for rule infractions. *See BNSF Ry. Co.*, 816 F.3d at 685 (finding causal connection where employer’s decisionmaker was aware of protected activity and acted shortly thereafter).

**D. BNSF Failed to Show by Clear and Convincing Evidence That It Would Have Taken the Same Action Absent the Protected Activity.**

Even if BNSF proffered a legitimate reason—the rule violations—appellants have presented substantial evidence of pretext. The crew-change location was disputed; appellants testified they were instructed to stop west of the bridge, while BNSF officials claimed otherwise. The Public Law Board later overturned the most severe discipline, reinstating Foster and removing the reprimand and suspension from Snyder and Kline’s records. This arbitration decision undermines BNSF’s assertion that the discipline was warranted. *See Kuduk*, 768 F.3d at 790 (noting that reversal of discipline can indicate pretext). Additionally, BNSF’s increased testing of only the reporting employees—while other crewmembers who committed the same alleged infractions were not similarly tested—raises a triable issue. A reasonable jury could conclude that BNSF’s stated reasons are pretextual and that retaliation was the true motive.

**Conclusion**

For these reasons, the district court’s grant of summary judgment should be reversed, and the case remanded for trial.
