The Seventh Circuit's decision in Clark v. Rameker correctly held that an inherited IRA is not exempt from a debtor's estate in bankruptcy under Section 522 of the U.S. Bankruptcy Code. A thorough examination of the statutory text and relevant case law supports this conclusion.

The Bankruptcy Code exempts "retirement funds to the extent that those funds are in a fund or account that is exempt from taxation under section... 408 ... of the Internal Revenue Code of 1986." 11 U.S.C. §522(b)(3)(C). The critical issue is whether an inherited IRA constitutes "retirement funds" for the beneficiary. As the Seventh Circuit astutely observed, an inherited IRA is not a retirement fund for the beneficiary because it is not held for their retirement.

The Supreme Court has long recognized that exemptions in bankruptcy law should be narrowly construed. In Lockwood v. Exchange Bank, 190 U.S. 294 (1903), the Court established that state law exemptions are not automatically incorporated into the Bankruptcy Code, and that the Code's exemptions should be interpreted in light of their purpose. The Court noted that exemptions are intended to "relieve the debtor from the pressure of claims that are hostile to his fresh start" (Id. at 299). However, this principle does not support exempting inherited IRAs, which are not essential to a debtor's fresh start.

In Hanover National Bank v. Moyses, 186 U.S. 181 (1902), the Court noted that the Bankruptcy Code is designed to provide a fair and equitable distribution of a debtor's assets among creditors. Exempting inherited IRAs would be inconsistent with this goal, as it would allow debtors to shield assets that are not truly retirement funds. The Court has consistently held that exemptions should not be extended beyond their plain meaning. In Crawford v. Burke, 195 U.S. 176 (1904), the Court stated that "exemptions, as they are allowed by the statute, are to be liberally construed in favor of the debtor, but this does not mean that they should be extended by construction beyond their plain meaning." Id. at 190.

Other courts have also emphasized the importance of narrowly construing exemptions. In Re Lippitt, 229 F. 854 (D.C. Ohio 1915), the court held that exemptions should be interpreted in a way that is consistent with their purpose and the overall scheme of the Bankruptcy Code. See also In re Bogdon, 74 F.2d 166 (6th Cir. 1934); Schwartz v. Holzman, 69 F.2d 814 (2d Cir. 1934); In re White, 61 F.2d 966 (2d Cir. 1932); Williams v. U.S. Fidelity & Guaranty Co., 236 U.S. 549 (1915); and Gleason v. Thaw, 236 U.S. 558 (1915). These decisions demonstrate that exemptions should be carefully scrutinized to ensure they align with the Bankruptcy Code's purpose.

The Seventh Circuit's decision is consistent with this long line of precedent. By holding that an inherited IRA is not exempt from the bankruptcy estate, the court ensured that the Bankruptcy Code is applied in a fair and equitable manner. The decision should be affirmed, as it is supported by a thorough analysis of the statutory text and relevant case law. In affirming the Seventh Circuit's decision, this Court would be upholding the principle that exemptions in bankruptcy law should be narrowly construed and that debtors should not be allowed to shield assets that are not truly retirement funds.
