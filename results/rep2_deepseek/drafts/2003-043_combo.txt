**ARGUMENT**

The decision of the court of appeals should be affirmed. The Internal Revenue Service cannot collect a partnership’s tax deficiency from individual partners without first making individualized assessments against those partners. The IRS’s timely assessment against the partnership extended the statute of limitations only as to the partnership, not as to the individual partners.

**I. The IRS Must Assess Each Taxpayer Individually to Extend the Statute of Limitations**

The Internal Revenue Code requires that an assessment be made by “recording the liability of the taxpayer.” 26 U.S.C. § 6203. A “taxpayer” is defined as “any person subject to any internal revenue tax,” and a “person” includes both “an individual” and a “partnership.” 26 U.S.C. § 7701(a)(14), (a)(1). Because the statute defines these as distinct categories, an assessment against a partnership is not an assessment against its individual partners. *See United States v. Coson*, 286 F.2d 453, 458 (9th Cir. 1961) (holding that a tax lien cannot attach to an individual partner’s property unless the tax obligation has been assessed against that partner under § 6203).

The IRS’s failure to assess respondents individually within the three-year period provided by 26 U.S.C. § 6501(a) bars collection from them. *See United States v. Updike*, 281 U.S. 489, 494 (1930) (holding that the statute of limitations for collection runs from the date of assessment against the particular taxpayer). The assessment against the partnership extended the collection period to ten years under 26 U.S.C. § 6502(a), but only for the partnership. *See United States v. Garbutt Oil Co.*, 302 U.S. 528, 531 (1938) (statute of limitations must be strictly construed in favor of the taxpayer). Because respondents were never assessed, the ten-year extension does not apply to them.

**II. State Law Does Not Substitute for a Federal Assessment**

The IRS argues that California partnership law, which makes partners jointly and severally liable for partnership debts, allows it to collect from respondents without individual assessments. This argument conflates substantive liability with procedural collection mechanisms. *See Young v. Riddell*, 283 F.2d 909, 910 (9th Cir. 1960) (holding only that a partner who voluntarily paid a partnership tax could not obtain a refund; the court did not authorize collection without assessment). Indeed, the district court in *Young* expressly acknowledged that an assessment against the partnership alone would require the IRS to “resort to court action” to collect from individual partners. *Young v. Riddell*, 60-1 U.S. Tax Cas. (CCH) ¶ 9831, at 76,050 (S.D. Cal. 1959).

Under California law, a judgment against a partnership is not a judgment against a partner. *See Cal. Corp. Code § 16307(c)*. A creditor must obtain a separate judgment against the partner to reach the partner’s personal assets. *See id.* The IRS obtained no such judgment against respondents, and the time to do so has expired. *See 26 U.S.C. § 6501(a)*.

**III. The IRS’s Reliance on Inapposite Authority Is Misplaced**

The IRS cites *United States v. Wright*, 57 F.3d 561 (7th Cir. 1995), but that case is distinguishable. In *Wright*, the IRS assessed both the partnership and the individual partners. *United States v. Wright*, 868 F. Supp. 1070, 1071 & n.1 (S.D. Ind. 1994). Here, no assessment was made against respondents. *See Coson*, 286 F.2d at 458 (distinguishing cases where individual assessments were made). The IRS also relies on *United States v. W. Prods., Ltd.*, 168 F. Supp. 2d 84 (S.D.N.Y. 2001), but that case was decided after the events at issue and is not binding on this Court. Moreover, it conflicts with the longstanding principle that a tax assessment must identify the taxpayer. *See 26 C.F.R. § 301.6203-1* (requiring “identification of the taxpayer”).

**IV. The Statute of Limitations Has Run**

The IRS had three years from the filing of respondents’ returns to assess them individually. 26 U.S.C. § 6501(a). It failed to do so. The assessment against the partnership did not toll or extend the limitations period for respondents. *See Updike*, 281 U.S. at 494. The IRS’s claims are therefore time-barred. *See Coson*, 286 F.2d at 458 (holding that a lien cannot exist without an assessment against the individual).

**CONCLUSION**

The judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
