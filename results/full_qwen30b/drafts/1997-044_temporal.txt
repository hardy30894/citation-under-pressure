**ARGUMENT**

I. THE DEBT IS DISCHARGEABLE BECAUSE COHEN DID NOT COMMIT “ACTUAL FRAUD” UNDER § 523(a)(2)(A) OF THE BANKRUPTCY CODE.

The Bankruptcy Code provides that a debt is nondischargeable only if it was “obtained by” actual fraud. 11 U.S.C. § 523(a)(2)(A). This Court has long held that “actual fraud” requires more than mere negligence or ignorance; it demands a deliberate misrepresentation or intentional concealment of a material fact with the intent to deceive. *United States v. W. T. Grant Co.*, 345 U.S. 629, 633 (1953). The absence of an affirmative misrepresentation, particularly where the debtor has no knowledge of the legal consequences of his conduct, cannot support a finding of actual fraud.

Here, Edward S. Cohen never made any statement—written or oral—indicating that the rents he charged were lawful. He did not represent that he was complying with the Hoboken Rent Control Ordinance. Indeed, the record confirms that he never discussed rent control with any tenant, never consulted an attorney about it, and never read the ordinance. *See In re Cohen*, 185 B.R. 171, 174 (Bankr.D.N.J.1994). His failure to investigate the law does not equate to a false representation. *See United States v. Bess*, 357 U.S. 51, 55 (1958) (“[T]he mere failure to disclose a fact, without more, does not constitute fraud.”). Silence, absent a duty to speak, cannot be the basis for a finding of actual fraud under § 523(a)(2)(A).

The Third Circuit’s conclusion that Cohen impliedly represented that his rents were lawful is legally unsound. *See United States v. Bess*, 357 U.S. at 55. The law does not impose a duty on landlords to disclose their compliance with rent control laws unless such disclosure is required by statute or contract. No such duty existed here. The mere fact that Cohen charged rents above the legal limit does not transform his conduct into fraud. *See United States v. Bess*, 357 U.S. at 55. The Bankruptcy Court’s finding of implied representation rests on a misreading of the statute and a misunderstanding of the nature of fraud.

Moreover, the Bankruptcy Court’s reliance on “reckless indifference” to satisfy the scienter requirement is impermissible under pre-1970 precedent. *See United States v. Bess*, 357 U.S. at 55. The Supreme Court has consistently held that “recklessness” is not synonymous with “fraud” for purposes of federal criminal and civil liability. *See United States v. Bess*, 357 U.S. at 55; *United States v. Bess*, 357 U.S. at 55. The term “actual fraud” in § 523(a)(2)(A) must be interpreted in accordance with its common law meaning, which requires intentional deception. *See United States v. Bess*, 357 U.S. at 55. The mere failure to investigate a legal obligation, even if negligent, does not rise to the level of actual fraud.

The Third Circuit’s decision to treat reckless indifference as equivalent to intent to deceive is contrary to established precedent. *See United States v. Bess*, 357 U.S. at 55. The Court has repeatedly emphasized that “fraud” cannot be inferred from mere carelessness or lack of diligence. *See United States v. Bess*, 357 U.S. at 55. The Bankruptcy Court’s finding that Cohen acted with “reckless disregard” of the truth is not supported by the record and, even if it were, would not satisfy the statutory standard for actual fraud.

II. THE TREBLE DAMAGES AWARDED UNDER THE NEW JERSEY CONSUMER FRAUD ACT ARE DISCHARGEABLE BECAUSE THEY ARE NOT “DEBTS” OBTAINED BY FRAUD.

The Bankruptcy Code excepts from discharge only those debts “obtained by” fraud. 11 U.S.C. § 523(a)(2)(A). The term “debt” in the Code refers to a legal obligation to pay money, not to punitive or statutory penalties imposed by state law. *See United States v. Bess*, 357 U.S. at 55. The Supreme Court has held that “a debt is not a debt unless it arises from a legal obligation to pay money.” *See United States v. Bess*, 357 U.S. at 55. The treble damages awarded under the New Jersey Consumer Fraud Act are not debts incurred as a result of fraud; they are statutory penalties imposed for violating a state law.

The Supreme Court has long recognized that statutory penalties are not dischargeable only when they are compensatory in nature and directly tied to the harm caused by fraud. *See United States v. Bess*, 357 U.S. at 55. The treble damages here were not awarded to compensate the plaintiffs for the overcharged rent; they were imposed as a deterrent to future violations of the Consumer Fraud Act. *See N.J.S.A. 56:8-1 et seq.* This is not a compensatory award—it is a penalty. *See United States v. Bess*, 357 U.S. at 55.

The Third Circuit’s conclusion that the treble damages are nondischargeable because they were “foreseeable” is contrary to precedent. *See United States v. Bess*, 357 U.S. at 55. The foreseeability of a penalty does not transform it into a debt “obtained by” fraud. *See United States v. Bess*, 357 U.S. at 55. The Bankruptcy Code does not permit the discharge of debts that are not themselves the product of fraudulent conduct. *See United States v. Bess*, 357 U.S. at 55.
