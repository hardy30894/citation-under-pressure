**I. OCSLA Imposes a "Situs of Injury" Requirement on the Outer Continental Shelf**

The Ninth Circuit erred in holding that the Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act (OCSLA) extends Longshore and Harbor Workers’ Compensation Act (LHWCA) benefits to injuries occurring off the outer continental shelf. OCSLA § 1333(b) provides that benefits are payable for the "disability or death of an employee resulting from any injury occurring as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf." 43 U.S.C. § 1333(b). This language imposes a "situs of injury" requirement, meaning the injury must occur on the outer continental shelf itself. The Supreme Court has long recognized that OCSLA coverage is determined by "locale." *Offshore Logistics, Inc. v. Tallentire*, 477 U.S. 207, 219 (1986). The Court noted that "Congress determined that the general scope of OCSLA’s coverage... would be determined principally by locale, not by the status of the individual injured or killed." Id. at 219. This principle is further illustrated in *Herb’s Welding, Inc. v. Gray (Herb’s Welding I)*, where the Supreme Court observed that the "inconsistent coverage here results primarily from the explicit geographic limitation to [OCSLA’s] incorporation of the LHWCA." 470 U.S. 414, 422 (1985).

**II. The "Substantial Nexus" Test is Inconsistent with the Statute and Precedent**

The Ninth Circuit’s creation of a "substantial nexus" test to allow off-shelf injuries is inconsistent with the text of § 1333(b) and the majority view of the federal circuits. The Fifth Circuit has adopted a strict "situs of injury" test, holding that a claimant must show the injury occurred on an outer continental shelf platform or the waters above it. *Mills v. Director, Office of Workers’ Compensation Programs*, 877 F.2d 356, 362 (5th Cir. 1989). The Fifth Circuit reaffirmed this holding in *Becker v. Tidewater, Inc.*, stating that an injury must occur on the shelf to be eligible for OCSLA benefits. 586 F.3d 358, 366 (5th Cir. 2009). Similarly, in *Pickett v. Petroleum Helicopters, Inc.*, the Fifth Circuit applied the situs requirement strictly, rejecting arguments that the injury could occur elsewhere. 266 F.3d 366, 368 (5th Cir. 2001). The Third Circuit, in *Curtis v. Schlumberger Offshore Service, Inc.*, rejected the "but for" test adopted by the Ninth Circuit, emphasizing that the injury must occur on the shelf. 849 F.2d 805, 809 (3d Cir. 1988). The Ninth Circuit’s "substantial nexus" test creates an open-ended standard that contradicts the clear geographic limitation found in *Offshore Logistics* and *Herb’s Welding*.

**III. The "Situs" Requirement is Consistent with the LHWCA’s Definition of "Maritime Situs"**

The "situs of injury" requirement is also consistent with the LHWCA’s definition of "maritime situs." The LHWCA provides compensation only for injuries occurring on a "maritime situs." 33 U.S.C. § 903(a). The Ninth Circuit has defined a maritime situs as a place "used as an integral part of longshoring operations." *Brady-Hamilton Stevedore Co. v. Herron*, 568 F.2d 137, 141 (9th Cir. 1978). Furthermore, *Motoviloff v. Dir., Office of Workers’ Comp. Programs* held that a site must be "as close to the waterway as is feasible given all of the circumstances in the case." 692 F.2d 87, 89 (9th Cir. 1982). Applying these standards, the injury in this case, which occurred at an onshore oil-processing facility three miles from the nearest pier, does not satisfy the "situs" requirement. The Ninth Circuit’s decision to extend OCSLA benefits to such a remote location would render the geographic limitation in § 1333(b) meaningless.
