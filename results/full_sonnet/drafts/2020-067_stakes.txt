**ARGUMENT**

**I. Rule 52(b)’s plain-error standard has always required courts to consult the whole record, not merely the evidence placed before the jury.**

Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 52(b) permits correction of a “plain error that affects substantial rights” even absent a contemporaneous objection. This Court’s precedents make clear that the inquiry into whether an unpreserved error affected “substantial rights” is not confined to the four corners of the trial transcript. In *United States v. Olano*, 507 U.S. 725 (1993), the Court held that the defendant bears the burden of showing the error “affected the outcome of the district court proceedings,” 507 U.S. at 734, and that this determination is necessarily a practical, record-wide inquiry rather than a technical one limited to what the jury heard.

This Court confirmed as much in *United States v. Vonn*, 535 U.S. 55 (2002), holding that “a silent defendant has the burden to satisfy the plain-error rule and … a reviewing court may consult the whole record when considering the effect of any error on substantial rights.” *Id.* at 59. Vonn rejected the very argument Petitioner advances here—that appellate review of an unobjected-to error must be confined to a particular slice of the proceedings (there, the Rule 11 colloquy). The Court explained that limiting review to a single transcript “would generally serve no practical purpose” given that Rule 52(b) itself calls for assessment of the error’s real-world effect. *Id.* at 74–75.

*United States v. Dominguez Benitez*, 542 U.S. 74 (2004), reinforces this approach. There, the Court articulated the substantial-rights inquiry as requiring “a reasonable probability that, but for the error,” the result of the proceeding would have differed, *id.* at 83, and it examined the full circumstances of the case—not simply the words spoken during the plea colloquy—to reach that conclusion.

Most directly on point is *Johnson v. United States*, 520 U.S. 461 (1997). There, the district court failed to submit the element of materiality to the jury in a perjury prosecution, an error the Government conceded was plain. In assessing whether the error affected substantial rights and the fairness of the proceedings, the Court did not restrict itself to what the jury had been told; it looked to the record as a whole and concluded that “the evidence supporting materiality was so overwhelming” that reversal was unwarranted, and that no “miscarriage of justice” would result from leaving the conviction undisturbed. *Id.* at 470. Johnson thus establishes that appellate courts, in gauging prejudice and the impact on fairness, integrity, and public reputation, may rely on undisputed facts drawn from the broader record rather than confining themselves to the evidence formally placed before the jury.

**II. Confining plain-error review to the trial record would contravene Rule 52(b)’s narrow, discretionary purpose.**

This Court has repeatedly cautioned that the plain-error doctrine exists “to be used sparingly, solely in those circumstances in which a miscarriage of justice would otherwise result,” a standard drawn from *United States v. Frady*, 456 U.S. 152, 163 n.14 (1982), and reaffirmed in *United States v. Young*, 470 U.S. 1, 15 (1985). The fourth prong of the Olano test—whether the error “seriously affect[s] the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of judicial proceedings”—derives from *United States v. Atkinson*, 297 U.S. 157, 160 (1936), and, as *United States v. Marcus*, 560 U.S. 258 (2010), explains, that discretion is to be exercised only in exceptional circumstances after a court has fairly assessed the likely effect of the error, 560 U.S. at 262. A rule barring courts from considering readily available, undisputed facts—such as a defendant’s extensive history of felony convictions and terms of imprisonment exceeding one year—would force appellate courts to indulge factual fictions that no one, including the defendant, actually disputes. That result cannot be squared with Rule 52(b)’s function of preventing “miscarriage[s] of justice,” Young, 470 U.S. at 15, nor with Olano’s command that the prejudice inquiry be a practical assessment of “the outcome of the district court proceedings,” 507 U.S. at 734.

Nothing in *Rehaif v. United States*, 139 S. Ct. 2191 (2019), suggests otherwise. Rehaif clarified the elements the Government must prove under 18 U.S.C. § 922(g) and § 924(a)(2); it said nothing about altering the settled plain-error framework of Olano, Johnson, Vonn, and Dominguez Benitez. Where, as here, the record—including matters such as a defendant’s undisputed history of multiple felony convictions and lengthy terms of imprisonment—forecloses any reasonable probability that a properly instructed jury would have found the defendant unaware of his status, the court of appeals is not merely permitted but obligated under this Court’s precedents to take that record into account.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should hold that a reviewing court, in assessing plain error, may examine the whole record—not merely the evidence submitted to the jury—in determining whether an unpreserved error affected a defendant’s substantial rights or the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of the proceedings, and should affirm the judgment below.
