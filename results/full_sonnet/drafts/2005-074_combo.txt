# ARGUMENT

## I. The Reinstatement Statute Does Not Apply to Reentries, Like Petitioner's, That Occurred Before Its 1997 Effective Date

Section 241(a)(5) says nothing about aliens who reentered the country long before its enactment. Where Congress has not spoken clearly to a statute's temporal reach, courts must apply the settled presumption that legislation operates only prospectively. This canon is not a modern invention; it traces to the earliest years of the Republic. In *United States v. Heth*, 7 U.S. (3 Cranch) 399 (1806), this Court refused to give a customs statute retroactive effect absent clear language compelling that result, reasoning that statutes are ordinarily to be understood as looking forward, not backward, in time. The Court has repeated that instruction ever since. In *Union Pacific Railroad Co. v. Laramie Stock Yards Co.*, 231 U.S. 190 (1913), the Court held that a statute will not be read to reach conduct predating its enactment unless the legislature's intent to do so is unmistakable. And in *Winfree v. Northern Pacific Railway Co.*, 227 U.S. 296 (1913), the Court likewise declined to apply a newly enacted survival provision to a cause of action that arose before the statute took effect, holding that Congress must speak plainly before a court will treat a statute as disturbing rights and expectations formed under prior law.

This canon has particular force in the immigration context. In *Chew Heong v. United States*, 112 U.S. 536 (1884), the Court construed an amendment to the Chinese exclusion laws not to extinguish the reentry rights of a laborer who had departed the United States before the amendment's certificate requirement existed, explaining that Congress will not be presumed to have intended to cut off rights that aliens had already exercised or relied upon under the law as it previously stood. The same logic governs here. Petitioner reentered the United States in 1982, fifteen years before Section 241(a)(5) existed. Nothing in the statute's text suggests that Congress intended reinstatement to reach back across such a gulf of time to reentries completed under an entirely different legal regime. Absent the "clear, strong, and imperative" congressional statement that *Heth*, *Union Pacific*, and *Winfree* require, the statute should be construed to apply only to reentries occurring after its 1997 effective date.

## II. Applying the Reinstatement Statute to Petitioner's Pre-Enactment Reentry Is Impermissibly Retroactive

Even if the statutory text could be stretched to cover pre-1997 reentries, applying it to Petitioner would violate the settled principle that new statutory burdens may not be imposed on conduct and relationships completed under the law as it previously stood. This Court has long recognized that retroactive application of a law offends fundamental fairness when it attaches new legal consequences to events already concluded, thereby defeating the reasonable expectations parties formed in reliance on existing law. In *Ochoa v. Hernandez y Morales*, 230 U.S. 139 (1913), the Court held that due process forbids a legislature from retroactively divesting rights that had already vested under prior law. The same principle recurs throughout this Court's retroactive-taxation decisions, which are directly instructive here because they address the precise evil at issue: a law reaching back to impose new burdens on a transaction the individual could not have anticipated when he acted. In *Nichols v. Coolidge*, 274 U.S. 531 (1927), and *Untermyer v. Anderson*, 276 U.S. 440 (1928), the Court struck down retroactive application of tax statutes to transactions completed before the statutes were enacted, holding that individuals are entitled to order their affairs according to the law as it exists at the time they act, and that a subsequent legislative change may not reach back to punish that reliance. *Shwab v. Doyle*, 258 U.S. 529 (1922), likewise instructs that a statute imposing new burdens will not be read to reach transactions completed before its passage absent unmistakable congressional intent to that effect.

Petitioner reentered the United States, built a life here, and married a United States citizen—all in reliance on an immigration regime that permitted him to seek adjustment of status notwithstanding his earlier deportation. Section 241(a)(5), applied to him, retroactively strips away that possibility entirely, imposing an absolute bar that did not exist when he acted. This is precisely the kind of legislative reach-back that *Ochoa*, *Nichols*, and *Untermyer* condemn: it attaches a new and severe disability to a completed act—his 1982 reentry—based on legislation enacted fifteen years later. Nor should courts lightly presume Congress meant to eliminate previously available relief through statutory silence. In *Greene v. United States*, 376 U.S. 149 (1964), this Court cautioned against construing statutes to work such a change in settled rights unless Congress has made its intention to do so unambiguous. No such unambiguous intention appears in Section 241(a)(5).

For these reasons, the reinstatement statute should not be construed to reach Petitioner's 1982 reentry, and its application to him is impermissibly retroactive.
