The Bankruptcy Act of 1898 excepts from discharge only those debts that are “obtained by” actual fraud. 30 Stat. 550. This language limits nondischargeability to the sum actually procured through the fraudulent conduct. Neal v. Clark, 95 U.S. 704, 709 (1877) (“The fraud referred to in that section means positive fraud, or fraud in fact, involving moral turpitude or intentional wrong.”). Additional statutory multiples or penalties are not sums “obtained by” the debtor and therefore remain dischargeable.

The statutory text and its predecessors have consistently been read to reach only the property or money the debtor himself received. Crawford v. Burke, 195 U.S. 176, 190 (1904) (the exception “was intended to prevent the bankrupt from retaining the fruits of his fraud”). Treble damages awarded under state consumer statutes are not fruits received by the debtor; they are a legislative sanction imposed after the fact. McIntyre v. Kavanaugh, 242 U.S. 138, 141 (1916) (distinguishing the original debt from “additional liability imposed as a penalty”).

This construction accords with the Act’s central purpose. Local Loan Co. v. Hunt, 292 U.S. 234, 244 (1934) (“One of the primary purposes of the bankruptcy act is to relieve the honest debtor from the weight of oppressive indebtedness and permit him to start afresh free from the obligations and responsibilities consequent upon business misfortunes.”). Allowing discharge of the compensatory sum obtained by fraud while excepting only that sum preserves the fresh-start policy without enlarging the statutory exception beyond its terms.

The rent overcharges here total $31,382.50. Any further award of treble damages, fees, or costs was not money or property obtained by the debtor and is therefore dischargeable under the 1898 Act.
