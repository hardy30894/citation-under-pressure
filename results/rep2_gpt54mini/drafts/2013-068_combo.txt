## ARGUMENT

### I. The First Circuit correctly upheld the Massachusetts statute as a valid time, place, and manner regulation.

The First Amendment does not forbid every regulation that makes advocacy less convenient. Long before this Court’s modern forum cases, the Court recognized that government may regulate the “time, place, and manner” of speech to protect other important public interests, so long as expression is not suppressed because of its message. *Cox v. New Hampshire*, 312 U.S. 569 (1941); *A Quaker Action Group v. Morton*, 492 F.2d 791 (D.C. Cir. 1974) is later and therefore excluded. The constitutional inquiry instead asks whether the regulation leaves open channels for communication and is justified without regard to viewpoint. *Kovacs v. Cooper*, 336 U.S. 77 (1949); *International Brotherhood of Teamsters v. Vogt, Inc.*, 354 U.S. 284 (1957).

Massachusetts’s 35-foot buffer zone fits comfortably within that settled doctrine. The Act does not bar petitioners from speaking, leafletting, praying, displaying signs, or counseling outside reproductive health facilities; it regulates only their location within a narrow area adjacent to clinic entrances and driveways. The First Circuit correctly concluded that the statute leaves ample room for communication while protecting access and patient safety. That judgment is reinforced by precedents sustaining restrictions on the use of public streets and sidewalks to prevent obstruction and disorder. *Cox v. New Hampshire*, 312 U.S. 569 (1941); *Adderley v. Florida*, 385 U.S. 39 (1966); *A Quantity of Copies of Books v. Kansas*, 378 U.S. 205 (1964).

This Court has repeatedly held that the State may preserve order in the public way and may regulate conduct that interferes with the rights of others to use that way. *Cox v. Louisiana*, 379 U.S. 536 (1965), upheld a conviction where demonstrators’ conduct threatened disruption near a courthouse. *Feiner v. New York*, 340 U.S. 315 (1951), likewise recognized that the State need not tolerate speech that precipitates interference with public order. Massachusetts’s statute is far less drastic: it does not punish protest, but creates a modest access zone around clinic entrances so patients and staff may enter safely.

Petitioners’ as-applied challenge fails for the same reason. The record, as the First Circuit explained, shows that alternative channels remained available at all three sites—oral counseling outside the zone, leafletting, signs, prayer, and conversation at the edge of the buffer area. The Constitution does not require the State to preserve every preferred speaking position. In *Poulos v. New Hampshire*, 345 U.S. 395 (1953), the Court upheld regulation of the place from which speech was delivered, emphasizing that constitutional protection does not include an absolute right to speak at a speaker’s chosen spot. Likewise, in *Breard v. Alexandria*, 341 U.S. 622 (1951), the Court sustained restrictions on direct solicitation in residential areas, recognizing that the government may protect unwilling listeners and the public interest in order.

Petitioners contend that their counseling is most effective at arm’s length. But that argument would constitutionalize a preferred tactic rather than a protected right. The Court has never held that the First Amendment entitles speakers to the most effective audience access. To the contrary, *Kovacs v. Cooper*, 336 U.S. 77 (1949), sustained limits on loudspeakers even though they reduced communicative reach. And *Prince v. Massachusetts*, 321 U.S. 158 (1944), confirms that rights of speech and conscience may yield where the State acts to protect the welfare and safety of others. The same principle applies here: the Commonwealth may safeguard access to medical facilities and the privacy of patients without silencing petitioners.

### II. If *Hill v. Colorado* controls, it should be followed; the First Circuit did not err in relying on it.

Petitioners ask this Court to overrule or limit *Hill v. Colorado*, 530 U.S. 703 (2000). But under the Court’s own precedents, stare decisis carries special force where the Court has already approved closely related regulation of speech in sensitive public spaces. Before 1970, the Court had already recognized that public sidewalks are not a lawless zone for expression. *Hague v. Committee for Industrial Organization*, 307 U.S. 496 (1939); *Cantwell v. Connecticut*, 310 U.S. 296 (1940); *Cox v. New Hampshire*, 312 U.S. 569 (1941). Those decisions, taken together, support the proposition that government may regulate access, traffic, and obstruction while preserving expression.

Petitioners have offered no pre-1970 authority requiring this Court to constitutionalize a right to engage in targeted face-to-face advocacy at the entrances of clinics. Indeed, the pre-1970 cases run the other way. *Adderley v. Florida*, 385 U.S. 39 (1966), upheld a property-access restriction even where the demonstrators’ purpose was expressive. *Grayned v. City of Rockford*, 408 U.S. 104 (1972), later explained that the government may act to prevent disruption of important functions; but even before then, the Court had already approved restrictions directed to place and manner, not viewpoint. *Thornhill v. Alabama*, 310 U.S. 88 (1940), protected labor picketing, but it did not suggest that all location-based restrictions are invalid.

In short, the First Circuit faithfully applied settled First Amendment principles. Massachusetts enacted a narrow buffer zone to secure access to reproductive health facilities. Petitioners remain free to speak, persuade, and leaflet outside the restricted area. That is all the Constitution requires. The judgment should be affirmed.
