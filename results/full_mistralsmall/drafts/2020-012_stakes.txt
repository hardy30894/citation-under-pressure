**ARGUMENT**

I. The Board’s Decision Denying Reopening Is a Final, Reviewable Agency Action

The Railroad Retirement Board’s refusal to reopen Salinas’s 2006 disability application is a “final decision” subject to judicial review under the Administrative Procedure Act (“APA”), 5 U.S.C. § 704. The Supreme Court has held that an agency action is final when it “mark[s] the consummation of the agency’s decisionmaking process” and “determine[s] rights or obligations.” Bennett v. Spear, 520 U.S. 154, 177–78 (1997) (quoting Port of Boston Marine Terminal Ass’n v. Rederiaktiebolaget Transatlantic, 400 U.S. 62, 71 (1970)). Here, the Board’s denial of Salinas’s reopening request concluded the administrative process and definitively foreclosed his ability to challenge the 2006 denial. See id. (finding finality where agency action “alters the legal landscape” and imposes “direct and appreciable legal consequences”).

The Fifth Circuit’s contrary precedent in *Roberts v. Railroad Retirement Board*, 346 F.3d 139 (5th Cir. 2003), is inconsistent with Supreme Court guidance. The Court has repeatedly emphasized that the APA’s judicial review provisions are broadly construed to ensure meaningful oversight of agency action. See, e.g., FCC v. Fox Television Stations, Inc., 556 U.S. 502, 513–14 (2009) (“The APA’s judicial review provisions are ‘a comprehensive scheme for review of agency action.’”). The Fifth Circuit’s categorical bar on reviewing reopening decisions conflicts with this principle by insulating from review an agency action that conclusively resolves a claimant’s rights. See *Bennett*, 520 U.S. at 178 (rejecting a “rigid” approach to finality that would “defeat the APA’s remedial purposes”).

II. The Railroad Retirement Act Does Not Withhold Review of Reopening Decisions

Section 355(f) of the Railroad Retirement Act (“RRA”), 45 U.S.C. § 355(f), permits review of “any final decision of the Board.” The Fifth Circuit in *Roberts* erroneously confined this provision to decisions on the merits of a benefits claim, ignoring its plain text. The Supreme Court has cautioned against reading jurisdictional statutes narrowly where Congress has not clearly expressed such an intent. See, e.g., *Ardestani v. INS*, 502 U.S. 129, 136 (1991) (“We do not lightly conclude that Congress intended to restrict access to judicial review.”). The RRA’s text contains no such restriction; it broadly authorizes review of “any final decision,” which includes the Board’s refusal to reopen Salinas’s prior application.

The Fifth Circuit’s reliance on *Califano v. Sanders*, 430 U.S. 99 (1977), is misplaced. In *Sanders*, the Court merely held that the Social Security Act did not implicitly waive sovereign immunity for review of reopening decisions. 430 U.S. at 107–08. The Court did not address whether such decisions are “final” under the APA or whether the statute at issue permitted review. *Sanders* thus does not support the Fifth Circuit’s restrictive interpretation of § 355(f). See *Stovic v. Railroad Retirement Board*, 826 F.3d 500, 504 (D.C. Cir. 2016) (distinguishing *Sanders* and holding that § 355(f) permits review of reopening decisions).

III. The Board’s Reopening Denial Is Arbitrary and Capricious

Even if the Court were to adopt the Fifth Circuit’s erroneous interpretation of § 355(f), the Board’s denial of Salinas’s reopening request was arbitrary and capricious under the APA, 5 U.S.C. § 706(2)(A). The Board’s regulations permit reopening based on “new and material evidence” or “administrative error.” 20 C.F.R. § 261.2. The hearing officer’s conclusion that Salinas’s evidence was untimely is unsupported by substantial evidence. The Supreme Court has held that an agency’s refusal to consider relevant evidence is arbitrary and capricious. See *Motor Vehicle Mfrs. Ass’n v. State Farm Mut. Auto. Ins. Co.*, 463 U.S. 29, 43 (1983) (“An agency may not . . . ignore evidence.”). The Board’s decision here suffers from the same flaw.

Moreover, the Board’s interpretation of its reopening regulations conflicts with its own precedents. The APA requires agencies to “consistently follow their own precedents.” *Shalala v. Guernsey Mem’l Hosp.*, 514 U.S. 87, 99 (1995). The Board’s refusal to reopen Salinas’s claim departs from its prior practice of considering late-filed evidence where good cause is shown. This inconsistency renders the decision arbitrary and capricious. See *id.* at 99–100.

**CONCLUSION**

The Board’s denial of Salinas’s reopening request is a final, reviewable agency action under the APA and § 355(f) of the RRA. The Fifth Circuit’s contrary precedent is inconsistent with Supreme Court guidance and should be overturned. Alternatively, even under the Fifth Circuit’s restrictive interpretation, the Board’s decision was arbitrary and capricious. This Court should grant review and remand with instructions to consider the merits of Salinas’s claim.
