# ARGUMENT

## I. MILFORD'S EXCLUSION OF THE CLUB WAS PERMISSIBLE SUBJECT-MATTER LINE-DRAWING WITHIN A LIMITED PUBLIC FORUM, NOT VIEWPOINT DISCRIMINATION

When a government entity opens its property for expressive activity by the public, the nature of the forum determines what restrictions are permissible. *Perry Educ. Ass'n v. Perry Local Educators' Ass'n*, 460 U.S. 37 (1983). Milford's Community Use Policy created, at most, a limited public forum, opened only for enumerated categories of "social, civic and recreational" activities "pertaining to the welfare of the community." Within such a forum, the State "may be justified in reserving [its] forum for certain groups or for the discussion of certain topics," so long as the restriction is reasonable and viewpoint neutral. *Cornelius v. NAACP Legal Def. & Educ. Fund, Inc.*, 473 U.S. 788, 806 (1985). A limited forum need not be held open to expression "on every subject." *Id.* at 811.

Milford did not exclude the Good News Club because of the viewpoint it wished to express on an otherwise permitted subject; it excluded the Club because the Club sought to use school facilities for religious worship and instruction, a category of use never opened by the Policy to any group. This case is therefore unlike *Lamb's Chapel v. Center Moriches Union Free School District*, 508 U.S. 384 (1993), where the school district had opened its forum to the discussion of "family values," yet barred the church from discussing that same subject only because its perspective was religious. There, the Court found viewpoint discrimination because the district drew a line "not [on] the fact that films were to be shown, but [on] the desire to prevent the presentation of point[s] of view." *Id.* at 393-94. Here, by contrast, the Second Circuit correctly found that the Club's meetings—which include prayer, proselytizing "challenge and invitation" segments inviting children to accept Christ as Savior, and the ceremonial trappings of worship—are not a discussion of a permitted secular topic "from a religious perspective," but are "quintessentially religious" in a way that "fall[s] outside the bounds of pure 'moral and character development.'" 202 F.3d 502, 510 (2d Cir. 2000).

This distinction accords with *Rosenberger v. Rector & Visitors of University of Virginia*, 515 U.S. 819 (1995), which reaffirmed that a State may exclude an entire subject matter—there, "religion"—from a forum without engaging in viewpoint discrimination, so long as it does not permit secular speakers to address that subject while excluding religious ones. 515 U.S. at 831. Milford's Policy excludes religious worship and instruction categorically; it does not permit secular clubs to teach "morals and character" while forbidding only the religious take on that topic. The Boy Scouts, Girl Scouts, and 4-H Club were permitted to use the building because their programs, whatever their content, do not consist of proselytizing worship services. Denying the Good News Club the same access it would deny to any group proposing devotional exercises is reasonable, viewpoint-neutral forum management of the kind long sanctioned in *Cornelius*, 473 U.S. at 811-13, and *Perry*, 460 U.S. at 49.

## II. IN THE ALTERNATIVE, THE ESTABLISHMENT CLAUSE JUSTIFIED MILFORD'S EXCLUSION OF WORSHIP-LIKE ACTIVITY DIRECTED AT ELEMENTARY SCHOOLCHILDREN ON SCHOOL PROPERTY

Even if this Court concludes that Milford's Policy operated as viewpoint discrimination, the Establishment Clause independently justified the exclusion. Avoiding Establishment Clause violations is a compelling interest that may justify content-based, and even viewpoint-based, restrictions on access to a limited forum. *Widmar v. Vincent*, 454 U.S. 263, 271 (1981) (recognizing that "the State's interest in avoiding an Establishment Clause violation may be [a] compelling" justification for restricting access). Milford's concern here is not speculative. The Club's activities are directed at children between six and twelve, held immediately after the school day in the same classrooms used for instruction, under the auspices of a program that the school permits to operate under its name and roof.

This Court has repeatedly recognized the heightened Establishment Clause concerns that attend religious activity involving impressionable schoolchildren in the school setting. In *Lee v. Weisman*, 505 U.S. 577, 592 (1992), the Court emphasized that public schools must be especially careful to avoid even the appearance of religious sponsorship because of the "concern that individuals not be coerced" by the "State's machinery" into religious observance, a concern amplified when the affected individuals are children. Similarly, in *Santa Fe Independent School District v. Doe*, 530 U.S. 290 (2000), the Court reiterated that school sponsorship of religious activity carries a unique risk of endorsement precisely because of the students' impressionable ages and their perception that such activity, occurring on school property under school auspices, bears the school's approval. *Id.* at 309-10 (citing *Lee*, 505 U.S. at 592).

Under *Lemon v. Kurtzman*, 403 U.S. 602, 612-13 (1971), a government practice must have a secular purpose, must not have the primary effect of advancing religion, and must not foster excessive entanglement between government and religion. Allowing an evangelical religious club to conduct prayer, scripture memorization, and salvation invitations to young children in public school classrooms immediately after the instructional day risks precisely the appearance of endorsement this Court's Establishment Clause jurisprudence forbids. Milford's judgment that permitting such use would create an unacceptable risk of perceived endorsement was a reasonable, good-faith application of these principles, and it independently supports the judgment below.
