# ARGUMENT

The equal-footing doctrine vests title to the beds of navigable waters in the States upon their admission to the Union. *See* *Pollard v. Hagan*, 44 U.S. 212 (1845). This Court has consistently held that navigability for title purposes must be assessed as of the date of statehood, and the Montana Supreme Court correctly applied that settled rule below.

## I. This Court's Precedents Require Navigability to Be Assessed at Statehood

The rule is not new. In *Pollard v. Hagan*, this Court held that the shores of navigable waters and the soils under them passed to the new States "as an incident of sovereignty," and that this transfer occurred at the moment of statehood. 44 U.S. at 230. Since *Pollard*, this Court has reaffirmed time and again that "the title to the beds of navigable waters passes to the state at the time it is admitted to the Union." *United States v. Utah*, 283 U.S. 64, 75 (1931). In *Utah*, this Court explained that "the question of navigability" for equal-footing purposes "is determined according to the natural and ordinary condition of the river" at statehood. *Id.* at 76.

This Court's decision in *United States v. Holt State Bank*, 270 U.S. 49 (1926), likewise instructs that title-navigability turns on the condition of the waterway "at the time Minnesota was admitted to the Union." *Id.* at 55. And in *Utah v. United States*, 403 U.S. 9 (1971), this Court reiterated that the navigability inquiry for title purposes looks to whether the water body "was navigable in fact" at the time of statehood, and that "the crucial date for determining navigability" is statehood itself. *See id.* at 10-11.

Respondent's position tracks this unbroken line of authority. The Montana Supreme Court below did not deviate from this framework; it applied precisely the constitutional test this Court has articulated, examining historical evidence of the Missouri, Madison, and Clark Fork Rivers as those rivers existed in 1889, the year of Montana's statehood.

## II. The Historical-Navigability Inquiry Necessarily Requires Trial Courts to Examine Evidence Contemporaneous with Statehood

Because title vests at statehood, a court cannot determine title-navigability by reference to a river's present condition, uses that developed after human alteration, or modern recreational or commercial activity untethered to the historical record. This Court's decision in *Utah v. United States*, 403 U.S. 9 (1971), confirms that the inquiry must reconstruct historical conditions: the Court there examined historical evidence of use of the Great Salt Lake to determine whether it was navigable "at the time of Utah's admission to the Union." *Id.* at 10.

Similarly, in *Oklahoma v. Texas*, 258 U.S. 574 (1922), this Court examined historical navigability of a river as it existed at the relevant sovereignty-defining moment, holding that susceptibility to use "in their ordinary condition" as highways for commerce is the touchstone. *Id.* at 586. The word "ordinary" necessarily calls for historical, not contemporary, assessment.

This is why lower courts, including the trial court and Montana Supreme Court in this case, properly received and weighed historical evidence—including affidavits, historical publications, and archival records documenting river conditions in and around 1889—to determine navigability at statehood. The Montana Supreme Court held that such evidence was properly considered under state evidentiary rules governing ancient documents and public records, *see* M. R. Evid. 901(8), 902(4)-(6), (8), precisely because the constitutional test demands proof of historical conditions that, by definition, predates any living witness.

## III. Petitioner's Proposed Test Would Contradict This Court's Precedents and Destabilize Settled Title

Petitioner's suggestion that navigability may be assessed by reference to conditions other than those existing at statehood cannot be reconciled with this Court's precedents. In *Utah v. United States*, 403 U.S. 9 (1971), this Court rejected attempts to rely on evidence of a waterway's capacity for use in eras or contexts disconnected from the statehood inquiry, holding that the test looks to the "natural and ordinary" condition of the water body "at the time" of statehood. *Id.* at 11 (quoting *Utah*, 283 U.S. at 76).

Nor can the Federal Power Act or federal licensing of PPL's dams alter the constitutional title analysis. This Court held in *Federal Power Commission v. Niagara Mohawk Power Corp.*, 347 U.S. 239 (1954), that a federal license under the FPA does not extinguish or diminish state-law property rights, and Congress must speak with clarity before divesting States of their sovereign title to riverbeds. *Id.* at 249-51. Nothing in the FPA purports to alter the settled constitutional test for navigability at statehood, nor could it, since that title vested in 1889—decades before the FPA's enactment.

## CONCLUSION

The constitutional test for navigability under the equal-footing doctrine has always required courts to determine navigability as of the date of statehood. That is precisely the inquiry the Montana courts undertook below, consistent with *Pollard v. Hagan*, *United States v. Utah*, *United States v. Holt State Bank*, and *Utah v. United States*. This Court should affirm.
