# ARGUMENT

## I. Plain-Error Review Under Rule 52(b) Requires an Examination of the Whole Record, Not Merely the Evidence Presented to the Jury

The plain-error standard traces directly to this Court’s decision in *United States v. Atkinson*, 297 U.S. 157 (1936), which recognized that appellate courts may notice unpreserved errors “if the errors are obvious, or if they otherwise seriously affect the fairness, integrity or public reputation of judicial proceedings.” *Id.* at 160. That formulation—later codified in Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 52(b)—does not instruct courts to look solely at what a jury heard. It instructs courts to ask whether the *proceeding as a whole* was fair, and that inquiry has always required a broader lens than the four corners of the trial transcript.

This Court confirmed as much in *Kotteakos v. United States*, 328 U.S. 750 (1946), where it explained that determining whether an error affected a defendant’s substantial rights is not a mechanical exercise confined to isolated trial testimony, but demands that the reviewing court exercise “its judgment” after assessing “the whole record.” *Id.* at 764–65. The Court in *Kotteakos* rejected any suggestion that prejudice could be gauged by tunnel vision on the specific evidence introduced; rather, the reviewing court must weigh “the effect of the error on the minds of the jurors in the total setting.” *Id.* at 764.

*Kotteakos* did not stand alone. In *United States v. Socony-Vacuum Oil Co.*, 310 U.S. 150 (1940), this Court likewise held that assessing whether an error was harmless requires “a survey of the whole record,” not a search limited to the particular exhibits or testimony that touched on the disputed point. *Id.* at 239. And in *Bihn v. United States*, 328 U.S. 633 (1946), the Court reaffirmed that appellate judges must look to the record “in its entirety” to determine whether an error was prejudicial, because prejudice cannot be sensibly evaluated in a vacuum. *Id.* at 638.

## II. Consistent with This Precedent, Courts Reviewing for Plain Error Have Long Considered Undisputed Background Facts Bearing on an Omitted Element

The rule that reviewing courts examine the entire record is especially important where, as here, the error concerns an element—knowledge of felon status—that is rarely, if ever, seriously disputed by a defendant who has in fact been convicted of a qualifying felony. This Court’s decision in *Screws v. United States*, 325 U.S. 91 (1945), is instructive. There, a plurality of the Court confronted an omitted willfulness element and examined the record as a whole, including facts not specifically tied to the jury instructions, in assessing whether the deficiency required reversal. *Id.* at 107. The Court did not artificially cabin its inquiry to only the evidence a jury had been directed to consider; it looked to what the record as a whole revealed about the defendant’s knowledge and intent.

Similarly, in *Berger v. United States*, 295 U.S. 78 (1935), this Court evaluated whether prosecutorial error undermined the fairness of the proceeding by considering the trial in its totality—including matters that informed whether the defendant was actually prejudiced—rather than treating each item of evidence in isolation. *Id.* at 88–89. The touchstone was whether the proceeding, viewed as a whole, comported with the basic guarantee of a fair trial—precisely the inquiry Rule 52(b) and *Atkinson* demand.

This Court’s harmless-error jurisprudence confirms the same approach in the constitutional context. In *Chapman v. California*, 386 U.S. 18 (1967), the Court held that even constitutional errors may be assessed by reviewing the entire record to determine whether the error was harmless beyond a reasonable doubt, explaining that the reviewing court must ask whether there is “a reasonable possibility that the evidence complained of might have contributed to the conviction,” a question that necessarily requires surveying everything the record discloses. *Id.* at 23–24 (quoting *Fahy v. Connecticut*, 375 U.S. 85, 86–87 (1963)). If a court may canvass the whole record to evaluate whether a constitutional trial error was harmless, it follows a fortiori that a court may do so when assessing whether an unpreserved statutory error affected substantial rights under the more forgiving plain-error standard.

Finally, in *Weiler v. United States*, 323 U.S. 606 (1945), this Court considered the strength of the prosecution’s undisputed proof in assessing the effect of a trial error, recognizing that reviewing courts must look to what the record establishes about a defendant’s culpability, not merely to the narrow slice of evidence tied to the specific mistake. *Id.* at 611.

## III. Applying These Principles Here, the Eleventh Circuit Properly Considered the Full Record

Read together, *Atkinson*, *Kotteakos*, *Socony-Vacuum*, *Bihn*, *Screws*, *Berger*, *Chapman*, and *Weiler* establish a consistent principle: courts assessing whether an unpreserved error affected substantial rights, or the fairness and integrity of a proceeding, must examine the record in its totality. The Eleventh Circuit did precisely that when it considered Greer’s undisputed history of felony convictions in evaluating whether the omission of a knowledge-of-status instruction affected the outcome or public confidence in his trial. That approach honors the “whole record” inquiry this Court’s precedents require and should be affirmed.
