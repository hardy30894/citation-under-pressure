# ARGUMENT

The Double Jeopardy Clause of the Fifth Amendment guarantees that no person shall "be subject for the same offence to be twice put in jeopardy of life or limb." U.S. Const. amend. V. This guaranty binds the States with full force, for it is "fundamental to the American scheme of justice." Benton v. Maryland, 395 U.S. 784, 796 (1969). Among its core protections is the rule that the Clause "protects against a second prosecution for the same offense after acquittal." North Carolina v. Pearce, 395 U.S. 711, 717 (1969). The California Supreme Court's holding that this guaranty is categorically inapplicable to noncapital prior-conviction enhancement proceedings cannot survive scrutiny under this Court's settled double jeopardy precedents.

## I. An Adjudication That the Evidence Is Insufficient to Prove an Enhancement Allegation Is the Equivalent of an Acquittal, and Retrial Is Barred Regardless of Whether That Adjudication Was Correct.

This Court has long held that a judgment resolving a factual charge against the government is final and unreviewable through a second proceeding, even if the underlying ruling was erroneous. In United States v. Ball, 163 U.S. 662 (1896), the Court held that a verdict of acquittal bars any further prosecution for the same charge, regardless of the soundness of the reasoning that produced it. The Court reaffirmed that rule in Fong Foo v. United States, 369 U.S. 141 (1962), holding that a judgment of acquittal is final and cannot be reopened, whatever its legal merit. And in Kepner v. United States, 195 U.S. 100 (1904), the Court held that the government may not obtain appellate correction of a factfinder's determination that the evidence was insufficient, because doing so would necessarily expose the defendant to a second jeopardy on the same charge.

These decisions rest on the principle, later distilled in Green v. United States, 355 U.S. 184, 187-188 (1957), that "the State with all its resources and power should not be allowed to make repeated attempts to convict an individual for an alleged offense, thereby subjecting him to embarrassment, expense and ordeal and compelling him to live in a continuing state of anxiety and insecurity, as well as enhancing the possibility that even though innocent he may be found guilty." That rationale applies with equal force here. The Court of Appeal determined that the prosecution failed to prove, beyond a reasonable doubt, that Monge personally used a deadly weapon or personally inflicted great bodily injury. That determination is functionally indistinguishable from an acquittal on a substantive charge, and under Ball, Fong Foo, and Kepner, the State is not entitled to a second attempt to prove what it failed to prove the first time.

## II. The Double Jeopardy Clause's Prohibition on Multiple Punishments Independently Bars a Second Attempt to Enhance Monge's Sentence.

The Clause protects not only against successive prosecutions but also against multiple punishments for the same offense. Pearce, 395 U.S. at 717. This principle traces to Ex parte Lange, 85 U.S. (18 Wall.) 163 (1874), where the Court held that once a defendant has been subjected to a lawful sentencing determination, the Constitution forbids the imposition of a second, additional punishment for the same conduct. There is no principled basis for confining that protection to the substantive offense while excluding the enhancement allegation that determines how long a defendant will actually be imprisoned. The enhancement finding here is not incidental bookkeeping; it doubled Monge's sentence and added a further one-year term. A rule permitting the State to relitigate that finding after a factfinder resolved it against the government exposes Monge to precisely the repeated jeopardy Ex parte Lange forecloses.

This conclusion is reinforced by Sealfon v. United States, 332 U.S. 575 (1948), where the Court held that principles embedded in the Double Jeopardy Clause preclude the government from relitigating an issue of fact that has already been resolved against it in a prior proceeding involving the same parties. The prior-conviction allegation here was fully and fairly litigated, with the prosecution given a full opportunity to prove it. Having lost on the merits, the State cannot simply try again.

## III. Because California Affords Trial-Like Procedures in Prior-Conviction Proceedings, the Enhancement Trial Bears the Hallmarks That Trigger Double Jeopardy Protection.

Although a State is not constitutionally required to provide a trial-type proceeding for sentencing determinations, Williams v. New York, 337 U.S. 241 (1949), a State that chooses to erect such a proceeding invites the safeguards that accompany it. California requires proof beyond a reasonable doubt, application of the rules of evidence, and the right "to be confronted with witnesses against him [and] to cross-examine" them. Specht v. Patterson, 386 U.S. 605, 610 (1967). Having voluntarily structured the enhancement proceeding as an adversarial, evidence-based trial in all but name, California cannot now disclaim the double jeopardy consequences that attend proceedings of that character. This Court cautioned in Williams that "[t]he due process clause should not be treated as a device for freezing the evidential procedure of sentencing in the mold of trial procedure," 337 U.S. at 251, but that caution cuts against California here: having chosen to adopt trial procedure, the State must accept the constitutional consequences that follow from it.

## CONCLUSION

For these reasons, the judgment of the California Supreme Court should be reversed.
