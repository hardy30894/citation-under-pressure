# ARGUMENT

## I. Due Process Does Not Guarantee a Defendant an Unfettered Right to Introduce Mental-Disease Evidence Outside the Insanity Framework

This Court has long recognized that the Due Process Clause does not impose a single, fixed method by which a State must permit evidence of mental illness to be used in a criminal trial. In *Leland v. Oregon*, 343 U.S. 790 (1952), the Court sustained an Oregon scheme placing the burden of proving insanity beyond a reasonable doubt on the defendant and rejected the claim that due process required the State to give independent effect to psychiatric evidence of "partial responsibility" apart from the insanity defense itself. *Id.* at 800-801. The Court explained that adoption of the traditional right-and-wrong test of criminal responsibility, to the exclusion of a separate "diminished capacity" doctrine, was a permissible exercise of the State's authority to define the boundaries of criminal responsibility. *Id.* Arizona's channeling rule does precisely what Oregon's rule did in *Leland*—it confines evidence of mental disease to the insanity inquiry rather than permitting it to operate as a freestanding negation of the mens rea element. Under *Leland*, that choice is constitutionally permissible.

This Court reached the same conclusion in *Fisher v. United States*, 328 U.S. 463 (1946), holding that a trial court did not violate the defendant's rights by declining to instruct the jury to weigh evidence of the defendant's low mental capacity, short of legal insanity, in assessing premeditation. The Court deferred to the settled local rule that mental capacity affects criminal responsibility only through the insanity defense, and it declined to superimpose a competing common-law doctrine of partial responsibility onto that framework. *Id.* at 476. As in *Fisher*, Arizona has chosen to funnel mental-state evidence through its insanity rule rather than through a separate "diminished capacity" avenue, and *Fisher* confirms that such a choice raises no constitutional infirmity.

*Powell v. Texas*, 392 U.S. 514 (1968), reinforces this conclusion. There the Court emphasized the States' broad latitude "to define crimes and defenses" and to formulate their own doctrines of criminal responsibility, observing that this area "is presently no more than a theory" susceptible of differing legislative and judicial resolutions among the States. *Id.* at 535-37. If the States may, as *Powell* holds, choose among competing tests of criminal responsibility itself, they may likewise choose how mental-disease evidence is channeled into the trial of guilt.

## II. Arizona's Rule Does Not Offend Any Principle of Justice So Rooted in Our Traditions as to Be Ranked Fundamental

The touchstone of Fourteenth Amendment due process in matters of state criminal procedure is not whether a State has adopted the most expansive evidentiary rule imaginable, but whether its rule offends "some principle of justice so rooted in the traditions and conscience of our people as to be ranked as fundamental." *Snyder v. Massachusetts*, 291 U.S. 97, 105 (1934). This Court has repeatedly cautioned against converting the Due Process Clause into a vehicle for imposing a single, judicially preferred model of criminal procedure on the States. *See Palko v. Connecticut*, 302 U.S. 319, 325 (1937) (due process forbids only that which "offends those canons of decency and fairness" fundamental to ordered liberty). Likewise, in *Lisenba v. California*, 314 U.S. 219, 236 (1941), the Court held that due process protects against fundamental unfairness in a trial's outcome, not against every variance from an ideal evidentiary scheme.

This deference extends squarely to state rules restricting the uses to which evidence may be put. In *Spencer v. Texas*, 385 U.S. 554 (1967), the Court upheld a state evidentiary practice permitting the jury to hear evidence of prior convictions for a limited purpose, notwithstanding the defendant's claim that the practice unfairly colored the jury's assessment of guilt. The Court held that the Due Process Clause does not "require the State to structure its criminal trial process" according to the defendant's preferred procedural model, so long as the State's rule serves a legitimate purpose and does not render the trial fundamentally unfair. *Id.* at 563-64. Arizona's rule serves exactly such a purpose: it preserves the traditional distinction between the elements of the offense, which the State must prove, and the affirmative defense of insanity, which the defendant must establish, without permitting psychiatric testimony to be used as a backdoor means of relitigating mens rea under a different evidentiary standard.

## III. The Distinction Between Proof of an Element and Proof of an Affirmative Defense Is Itself of Ancient Constitutional Standing

This Court's decision in *Davis v. United States*, 160 U.S. 469 (1895), long ago recognized the propriety of treating insanity as a matter distinct from the prosecution's burden on the elements of the offense, while nonetheless requiring that the jury's ultimate determination of guilt take account of the defendant's mental condition through the insanity framework rather than through open-ended use of psychiatric evidence to negate intent directly. *Id.* at 484-88. Arizona's rule is consistent with that longstanding structure.

## Conclusion

Because Arizona's evidentiary rule channeling mental-disease evidence to the insanity inquiry finds direct support in *Leland*, *Fisher*, *Powell*, *Snyder*, *Palko*, *Lisenba*, *Spencer*, and *Davis*, it does not violate due process, and the judgment below should be affirmed.
