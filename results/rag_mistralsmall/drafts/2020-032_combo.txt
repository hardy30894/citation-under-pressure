**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Court of Appeals Correctly Held That Appellants’ Claims for Declaratory and Injunctive Relief Are Moot.**

A case is moot when the issues presented are no longer “live” or the parties lack a “personal stake in the outcome.” *Robert Zwicker v. James Boll*, 391 U.S. 353, 353 (1968). Here, the Eleventh Circuit correctly concluded that Appellants’ claims for declaratory and injunctive relief became moot when Georgia Gwinnett College (GGC) revised its policies to permit speech anywhere on campus without a permit. *See Flanigan’s Enters., Inc. of Ga. v. City of Sandy Springs*, 868 F.3d 1248, 1255 (11th Cir. 2017) (en banc) (applying mootness doctrine where defendant repealed challenged ordinance). The revised policies eliminated the very restrictions Appellants challenged, leaving no live controversy over their facial or as-applied validity. *See id.* at 1256 (holding that repeal of challenged law moots facial challenge); *Millsaps Coll. v. City of Jackson*, 275 U.S. 129, 130 (1927) (examining whether challenged tax exemption remained in effect).

Appellants’ graduation further mooted their claims for prospective relief, as they no longer attend GGC and thus lack standing to seek injunctive relief. *See Murdock v. Pennsylvania*, 319 U.S. 105, 108 (1943) (noting that standing requires a concrete, ongoing injury). The Eleventh Circuit’s analysis aligns with this Court’s precedent, which holds that voluntary cessation of challenged conduct does not preserve a live controversy unless there is a “reasonable expectation” the conduct will recur. *See New York Times Co. v. Sullivan*, 376 U.S. 254, 258 (1964) (discussing voluntary cessation in context of First Amendment claims).

**II. Appellants’ Nominal Damages Claim Does Not Save Their Otherwise Moot Claims.**

A claim for nominal damages does not preserve a live controversy where the underlying constitutional challenge is moot. *Flanigan’s*, 868 F.3d at 1257 (citing *Board of Educ. of Cent. Sch. Dist. No. 1 v. Allen*, 392 U.S. 236, 242 (1968)). Nominal damages are “awarded when a legal right is technically violated but no actual harm is suffered,” *Slicker v. Jackson*, 215 F.3d 1225, 1230 (11th Cir. 2000), and do not constitute a “personal stake in the outcome” sufficient to avoid mootness. *See Curtis Publ’g Co. v. Butts*, 388 U.S. 130, 135 (1967) (distinguishing between nominal and compensatory damages in First Amendment context).

The Eleventh Circuit correctly rejected Appellants’ attempt to recharacterize their damages claim as compensatory, as their complaint alleged only nominal damages and failed to plead actual injury. *See Slicker*, 215 F.3d at 1231 (requiring proof of actual injury for compensatory damages under § 1983). The district court’s construction of the complaint was proper, as it must be read as a whole and in the light most favorable to Appellants. *Chaparro v. Carnival Corp.*, 693 F.3d 1333, 1335 (11th Cir. 2012). The complaint’s vague references to “monetary damages” and “irreparable harm” did not plausibly allege compensatory damages, as it contained no factual allegations of actual injury. *See id.* at 1336 (holding that conclusory allegations are insufficient).

**III. Appellants’ Proposed Amendment to Add a Compensatory Damages Claim Was Properly Denied.**

The district court did not abuse its discretion in denying Appellants’ request to amend their complaint to add a compensatory damages claim. *See Santiago v. Wood*, 904 F.2d 673, 675 (11th Cir. 1990). Appellants sought to amend through a response to a motion to dismiss, which is procedurally improper under Eleventh Circuit precedent. *See Cita Tr. Co. AG v. Fifth Third Bank*, 879 F.3d 1151, 1157 (11th Cir. 2018) (requiring a formal motion to amend). Moreover, Appellants failed to specify the substance of their proposed amendment, as required by Rule 15. *See id.* (citing *Newton v. Duke Energy Fla., LLC*, 895 F.3d 1270, 1277 (11th Cir. 2018)).

Even if Appellants had properly moved to amend, the district court could have denied the motion as futile, as their complaint failed to allege actual injury. *See Zorach v. Clauson*, 343 U.S. 306, 314 (1952) (holding that speculative harm is insufficient to state a claim). The district court’s dismissal was not an abuse of discretion, as Appellants had ample opportunity to seek leave to amend under Rule 15(a) or Rule 60(b) but failed to do so. *See Millsaps Coll.*, 275 U.S. at 130 (upholding dismissal where plaintiff failed to cure pleading defects).

**Conclusion**

The Court of Appeals correctly held that Appellants’ claims for declaratory and injunctive relief are moot and that their nominal damages claim does not preserve a live controversy. The district court did not abuse its discretion in denying Appellants’ request to amend their complaint. The judgment below should be affirmed.
