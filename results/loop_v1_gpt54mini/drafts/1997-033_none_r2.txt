The district court exceeded its authority when it used § 1404(a) to keep this MDL case for trial. Section 1407(a) is explicit: the Panel may transfer actions only “for coordinated or consolidated pretrial proceedings,” and it “shall” remand each action to the transferor district “at or before the conclusion of such pretrial proceedings.” 28 U.S.C. § 1407(a). That command is mandatory, not precatory. United States v. Moore, 95 U.S. 760, 763 (1878); Botany Worsted Mills v. United States, 278 U.S. 282, 289 (1929). A court may not recharacterize Congress’s remand requirement as a matter of convenience. Caminetti v. United States, 242 U.S. 470, 485 (1917).

Section 1404(a) does not supply the missing authority. It permits transfer “[f]or the convenience of parties and witnesses, in the interest of justice,” but only within the confines Congress set. Ex parte Collett, 337 U.S. 55, 58-59 (1949). In Hoffman v. Blaski, the Court held that transfer is limited to districts where the action “might have been brought” in the first instance; a court cannot manufacture transfer power after the fact. 363 U.S. 335, 343-44 (1960). Van Dusen v. Barrack likewise makes clear that § 1404(a) is a limited transfer mechanism, not a device for altering the statutory venue structure. 376 U.S. 612, 636-37 (1964). So too Norwood v. Kirkpatrick, which recognized that § 1404(a) expanded transfer discretion only within the statute’s own terms, not beyond them. 349 U.S. 29, 31-32 (1955).

The transferee court’s self-assignment also defeats the purpose of § 1407. Multidistrict transfer exists to centralize common pretrial matters and then return the case to the original forum for trial. See Plumbing Fixture Cases, 298 F. Supp. 484, 495 (J.P.M.L. 1968). That structure reflects Congress’s venue policy, not a mere preference for efficiency. Gulf Oil Corp. v. Gilbert, 330 U.S. 501, 507-08 (1947). The Court has long respected statutory venue choices and refused to let convenience swallow them. United States v. National City Lines, Inc., 334 U.S. 573, 581-82 (1948); Continental Grain Co. v. Barge FBL-585, 364 U.S. 19, 26-27 (1960).

The district court’s action cannot be justified as a permissible gap-filling measure. When Congress speaks clearly, courts must apply the statute as written. Ex parte Falk, 180 U.S. 339, 344 (1901); United States v. Union Pacific R.R. Co., 98 U.S. 569, 604 (1879). And where a statute directs that a case “shall” be remanded, courts may not add an exception Congress omitted. Iselin v. United States, 270 U.S. 245, 250-51 (1926). The contrary practice below is not an interpretation of § 1407(a); it is a rewrite of it.

Nor do the government’s convenience arguments matter. Congress already balanced efficiency and venue in § 1407(a). The transferor court may handle pretrial proceedings; the trial forum remains the original district. That is the statutory compromise, and courts are bound to honor it. Thompson v. Thompson, 226 U.S. 551, 562 (1913); Botany Worsted Mills, 278 U.S. at 289. Because the Arizona court had no authority to retain Lexecon’s case for trial, its order should be vacated and the case remanded to the Northern District of Illinois.
