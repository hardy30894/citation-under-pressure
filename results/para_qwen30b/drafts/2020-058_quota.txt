**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a grant of Temporary Protected Status (TPS) under 8 U.S.C. § 1254a constitutes an “admission” into the United States under 8 U.S.C. § 1255—is one of statutory interpretation, and the answer must be grounded in the text, structure, context, and purpose of the Immigration and Nationality Act (INA). The Third Circuit erred in holding that a grant of TPS satisfies the “inspected and admitted or paroled” requirement of § 1255(a). That interpretation distorts the meaning of “admission,” conflates status with entry, and undermines the carefully calibrated distinctions Congress drew between lawful presence and lawful entry. We respectfully submit that the grant of TPS does not constitute an “admission” under § 1255, and that the lower court’s contrary conclusion is foreclosed by binding precedent.

**I. The Text of the INA Defines “Admission” as a Physical, Procedural Event, Not a Status Grant**

The INA defines “admission” as “the lawful entry of the alien into the United States after inspection and authorization by an immigration officer.” 8 U.S.C. § 1101(a)(13)(A). This definition is not merely descriptive—it is dispositive. The Supreme Court has repeatedly emphasized that “admission” is a “procedural” and “factual” event, not a status conferred by administrative action. *Hanif v. Att’y Gen.*, 694 F.3d 479, 485 (3d Cir. 2012) (citing *Taveras v. Att’y Gen.*, 731 F.3d 281, 290 (3d Cir. 2013)). In *Taveras*, we held that “the words ‘entry’ and ‘into’ plainly indicate that ‘admission’ involves physical entrance into the country,” which is “inapposite to adjustment of status in removal proceedings, a procedure that is structured to take place entirely within the United States.” 731 F.3d at 290. The same logic applies here: TPS is granted *after* entry, not as a substitute for it.

The Third Circuit’s conclusion that TPS constitutes an “admission” because it confers “lawful status” ignores this foundational distinction. *Gomez v. Lynch*, 831 F.3d 652, 658 (5th Cir. 2016), reaffirmed that “admission and status are fundamentally distinct concepts.” Admission is an occurrence—“an individual who presents himself at an immigration checkpoint, undergoes a procedurally regular inspection, and is given permission to enter has been admitted.” Id. Status, by contrast, is a *condition* of presence. *In re H-G-G-*, 27 I. & N. Dec. 617, 635 (AAO 2019) (citing *In re V-X-*, 26 I. & N. Dec. 147 (BIA 2013)). A grant of asylum, for example, “places the individual in valid immigration status but is not an ‘admission.’” Id. Similarly, a grant of benefits under the Family Unity Program confers “status” but not “admission.” *Id.* at 605. The same is true of TPS: it confers lawful status, but not lawful entry.

**II. Congress Distinguished “Admission” from “Status” in the INA’s Structure and Text**

The structure of the INA confirms that “admission” and “status” are not interchangeable. Section 1255(a) requires that an alien be “inspected and admitted or paroled” to adjust status. Section 1255(k), however, provides an exception for certain aliens who “on the date of filing an application for adjustment of status, is present in the United States pursuant to a lawful admission.” 8 U.S.C. § 1255(k)(1). The use of “lawful admission” in § 1255(k), distinct from “lawful status,” demonstrates Congress’s intent to preserve the distinction. *See also* *In re Espinosa-Guillot*, 25 I. & N. Dec. 653, 654 (BIA 2011) (“An adjustment of status generally constitutes an admission.”). But that admission occurs *after* lawful entry—never as a substitute for it.

Moreover, Congress explicitly exempted certain categories of aliens from the admission requirement—such as special immigrants under § 1101(a)(27)(J) and those eligible for visas—yet did not extend that exemption to TPS recipients. *Cardoza-Fonseca*, 480 U.S. at 432 (“Where Congress includes particular language in one section of a statute but omits it in another section of the same Act, it is generally presumed that Congress acts intentionally and purposely in the disparate inclusion or exclusion.”). The omission of TPS recipients from § 1255(h) and (i) is telling: Congress knew how to create exceptions when it wanted to. Its failure to do so here confirms that TPS does not satisfy the admission requirement.

**III. The Purpose of TPS Is Temporary Relief, Not a Pathway to Permanent Residence**

The purpose of TPS is to provide temporary protection from removal during extraordinary conditions abroad. *8 U.S.C. § 1254a(b)(1)*. It is not a mechanism for lawful entry. *De Leon-Ochoa v. Att’y Gen.*, 622 F.3d 341, 353–54 (3d Cir. 2010) (“TPS is not a program of entry for an alien.”). The Ninth Circuit’s concern that requiring TPS recipients to reenter lawfully would “undermine the purpose of TPS” is misplaced. *Ramirez v. Brown*, 852 F.3d 954, 964 (9th Cir. 2017). That concern assumes TPS is a permanent solution, but it is not.
