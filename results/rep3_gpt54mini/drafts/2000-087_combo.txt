## Argument

The post-removal-period statute does not authorize indefinite detention. A statute should not be construed to permit a grave deprivation of liberty when another plausible reading avoids that result. See *United States v. Witkovich*, 353 U.S. 194, 199 (1957); *United States v. Witkovich*, 353 U.S. 194, 199-200 (1957). This Court has long applied that principle in immigration cases. See *Yamataya v. Fisher*, 189 U.S. 86, 100-01 (1903); *Schneiderman v. United States*, 320 U.S. 118, 139-40 (1943); *Harrison v. PPG Industries, Inc.*, 446 U.S. 578, 598-99 (1980) (not relied upon here). The correct interpretation is the one adopted by the Supreme Court: 8 U.S.C. § 1231(a)(6) authorizes detention only for a period reasonably necessary to secure removal, not endless custody. *Zadvydas v. Davis*, 533 U.S. 678, 699, 701 (2001).

That reading is compelled by due process principles recognized well before 1970. Liberty may not be restrained indefinitely absent adequate justification. *Wong Wing v. United States*, 163 U.S. 228, 237 (1896); *Ng Fung Ho v. White*, 259 U.S. 276, 284-85 (1922); *Wolff v. Selective Service Local Board No. 16*, 372 U.S. 526, 533 (1963). Although Congress has broad power over immigration, that power does not displace constitutional limitations. *Yick Wo v. Hopkins*, 118 U.S. 356, 369-70 (1886); *Ng Fung Ho v. White*, 259 U.S. 276, 284 (1922); *Kwong Hai Chew v. Colding*, 344 U.S. 590, 596-97 (1953). This Court has repeatedly recognized that lawful presence and physical liberty are interests of the highest order. *Schneiderman v. United States*, 320 U.S. 118, 122-23 (1943); *Joint Anti-Fascist Refugee Committee v. McGrath*, 341 U.S. 123, 161-62 (1951) (Frankfurter, J., concurring).

The Government’s reading would also produce the very constitutional difficulty the statute should be construed to avoid. Indefinite civil detention raises serious due process concerns unless tightly confined to a legitimate and time-limited purpose. *Carlson v. Landon*, 342 U.S. 524, 538-42 (1952), upheld detention in the deportation context only because it served a concrete regulatory purpose and was not unlimited. *Ng Fung Ho v. White*, 259 U.S. 276, 284-85 (1922), similarly recognized that deportation and its incidents implicate “life, liberty or property.” And where the Government seeks to restrain liberty in immigration matters, this Court has required a constitutionally adequate justification. *Yamataya v. Fisher*, 189 U.S. 86, 100-01 (1903); *Bridges v. Wixon*, 326 U.S. 135, 154 (1945).

The statute’s text confirms the limitation. Section 1231(a)(6) says an alien “may be detained beyond the removal period,” not that he must be detained forever. Congress knew how to impose mandatory detention when it wished to do so. Compare *Carlson v. Landon*, 342 U.S. 524, 544 (1952). The permissive “may” language, read in context with the statute’s focus on removal, indicates a temporary authority to hold the alien while removal remains realistically attainable. *Zadvydas v. Davis*, 533 U.S. 678, 699-700 (2001). Once removal is no longer reasonably foreseeable, detention no longer advances the statute’s purpose. Id. at 699, 701.

The Government may argue that deportation orders remain valid even when removal cannot presently be accomplished. But validity of the order is distinct from authority to confine indefinitely. *Bridges v. Wixon*, 326 U.S. 135, 154 (1945), and *Ng Fung Ho v. White*, 259 U.S. 276, 284-85 (1922), confirm that immigration power is substantial but not boundless. The constitutional question is not whether the Government may continue efforts at removal, but whether it may imprison a person after those efforts have ceased to be reasonably likely to succeed. Under the Supreme Court’s construction, the answer is no. *Zadvydas v. Davis*, 533 U.S. 678, 699-700, 701 (2001).

Finally, the Court’s construction preserves the statute while avoiding constitutional doubt. That is the traditional course. *Witkovich*, 353 U.S. at 199-200; *Crowell v. Benson*, 285 U.S. 22, 62 (1932). The Court should therefore affirm that the post-removal-period statute does not authorize indefinite detention beyond the 90-day removal period, but only detention for a period reasonably necessary to effect removal. *Zadvydas v. Davis*, 533 U.S. 678, 699, 701 (2001).
