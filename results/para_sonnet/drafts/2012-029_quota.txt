# ARGUMENT

Federal courts do not possess exclusive jurisdiction over every case that happens to require resolution of a question of patent law. Section 1338(a) extends federal jurisdiction only to cases in which "the plaintiff's right to relief necessarily depends on resolution of a substantial question of federal patent law, in that patent law is a necessary element of one of the well-pleaded claims." *Christianson v. Colt Indus. Operating Corp.*, 486 U.S. 800, 809 (1988). Necessity alone has never been sufficient. The state-law legal-malpractice claim brought by Respondent Minton against his former patent attorneys does not "arise under" federal patent law within the meaning of § 1338(a), and the Texas Supreme Court erred in holding otherwise.

## I. A Federal Issue Embedded in a State-Law Claim Confers "Arising Under" Jurisdiction Only if It Is Substantial and Does Not Disturb the State-Federal Balance.

This Court has long resisted opening "the 'arising under' door" merely because a state cause of action turns on some question of federal law. *Grable & Sons Metal Prods., Inc. v. Darue Eng'g & Mfg.*, 545 U.S. 308, 313 (2005). Rather than "adopt[ing] a bright-line rule," *id.* at 314, *Grable* requires courts to ask whether a state-law claim "necessarily raise[s] a stated federal issue, actually disputed and substantial, which a federal forum may entertain without disturbing any congressionally approved balance of federal and state judicial responsibilities." *Id.* This framework builds on decades of precedent recognizing that a suit does not "arise under" federal law "unless it really and substantially involves a dispute or controversy respecting the validity, construction, or effect of such a law, upon the determination of which the result depends." *Shulthis v. McDougal*, 225 U.S. 561, 569 (1912). As Justice Cardozo explained in *Gully v. First National Bank in Meridian*, 299 U.S. 109 (1936), federal jurisdiction demands "a selective process which picks the substantial causes out of the web and lays the other ones aside," *id.* at 117-18—precisely the analysis the *Grable* Court later codified. And this Court has cautioned that federal-question jurisdiction over state-created causes of action is reserved for a "special and small category" of cases. *Empire Healthchoice Assurance, Inc. v. McVeigh*, 547 U.S. 677, 699 (2006). Minton's malpractice claim does not belong in that category.

## II. Minton's Malpractice Claim Presents No Substantial Federal Issue Because It Requires Only a Backward-Looking, Hypothetical Application of Settled Patent Law to Case-Specific Facts.

Even accepting that resolution of the "experimental use" exception is *necessary* to Minton's malpractice claim and is *actually disputed*, the inquiry does not end there; *Grable* independently requires substantiality to the federal system, not merely to the parties. 545 U.S. at 313. The substantiality that matters is not "the importance of the issue to the particular parties in the immediate suit," but "the importance of the issue to the federal system as a whole." *Merrell Dow Pharm. Inc. v. Thompson*, 478 U.S. 804, 814 (1986) (recognizing that the presence of a federal element does not automatically confer jurisdiction).

Here, the patent question is entirely backward-looking. No actual patent will be issued, canceled, or amended as a result of this litigation; the '643 patent's validity was already conclusively adjudicated in the underlying federal infringement suit. *See Minton v. Nat'l Ass'n of Sec. Dealers, Inc.*, 226 F. Supp. 2d 845 (E.D. Tex. 2002), *aff'd*, 336 F.3d 1373 (Fed. Cir. 2003). What remains is a hypothetical "case within a case"—whether, had the experimental-use exception been timely raised, a different result might have followed. That inquiry resembles countless state-law malpractice actions requiring juries to reconstruct the probable outcome of an underlying federal claim, a task states routinely and competently perform. Cf. *Franchise Tax Bd. v. Construction Laborers Vacation Trust*, 463 U.S. 1, 26-27 (1983) (state law "is not transformed into federal law" simply because it "refers to" a federal statute).

Because the answer to the hypothetical patent question binds no one and precludes nothing in any future patent case, it lacks the sort of consequence that made the federal tax issue in *Grable* substantial to the federal system—there, the case "centrally involved the meaning of a federal tax provision" whose resolution had "significance for the entire operation of the federal tax scheme." 545 U.S. at 315. No comparable stakes exist here.

## III. Exercising Exclusive Federal Jurisdiction Would Disturb the Congressionally Approved Balance Between State and Federal Courts.

*Grable*'s final requirement independently forecloses exclusive federal jurisdiction. 545 U.S. at 314. Legal malpractice is a quintessential state-law tort, and the States possess the traditional and substantial interest in regulating the conduct of attorneys licensed within their borders and in providing a forum for their citizens' malpractice grievances. This Court has recognized the States' primacy in regulating the practice of law even where patent matters are implicated. *See Sperry v. Florida*, 373 U.S. 379, 402 (1963) (acknowledging the State's "substantial interest in regulating the practice of law within the State"). Converting every malpractice claim that touches on patent law into an exclusively federal matter would strip state courts of authority over a core area of state regulation while adding nothing to the uniformity of patent law itself, since no patent rights are actually adjudicated. That result cannot be reconciled with *Grable*'s admonition against upsetting the "sensitive judgments about the exercise of federal judicial power." 545 U.S. at 314; accord *Holmes Group, Inc. v. Vornado Air Circulation Sys., Inc.*, 535 U.S. 826, 831 (2002) (declining to expand § 1338 jurisdiction beyond its statutory text).

For these reasons, the judgment of the Supreme Court of Texas should be reversed.
