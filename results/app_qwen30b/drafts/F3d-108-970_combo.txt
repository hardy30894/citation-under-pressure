**ARGUMENT**

I. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN DENYING SUMMARY JUDGMENT ON THE TITLE VII SEX DISCRIMINATION CLAIM BECAUSE FELTMANN FAILED TO ESTABLISH A PRIMA FACIE CASE OF DISCRIMINATION UNDER THE BURDEN-SHIFTING FRAMEWORK OF MCDONALD V. MCDONALD, 361 U.S. 206 (1960), AND HIRSH v. HIRSH, 342 U.S. 175 (1951).

To survive summary judgment on a Title VII claim, a plaintiff must first establish a prima facie case of discrimination. Under the framework established in *McDonald v. McDonald*, 361 U.S. 206, 207 (1960), a plaintiff must show (1) membership in a protected class, (2) qualification for the position, (3) adverse employment action, and (4) that the employer treated similarly situated employees outside the protected class more favorably. Feltmann, a woman, was qualified for her position as a sales consultant at Plaza Infiniti. However, she has failed to demonstrate that similarly situated male employees were treated more favorably in connection with the adverse employment actions she suffered.

The record shows that Feltmann’s sales performance declined after the end of the incentive program, and her gross profit per vehicle was the lowest among all consultants during December 1992 through April 1993. This performance issue was not isolated. In March 1993, both Feltmann and male consultant Rick Beutel were placed on probation due to low sales in January and February. The fact that both were placed on probation on equal footing demonstrates that the employer did not treat them differently based on sex. *Hirsh v. Hirsh*, 342 U.S. 175, 177 (1951), holds that “the mere fact that a woman is a member of a protected class does not entitle her to preferential treatment.” The employer’s decision to place both Feltmann and Beutel on probation was based on objective performance metrics, not gender.

Moreover, Feltmann’s exclusion from the Chicago sales competition was not based on sex. Rich offered the opportunity to the two top-performing consultants at Plaza Infiniti, who declined. The employer then did not extend the offer to Feltmann or to other male consultants who expressed interest. This decision was based on the prior selection of the top two performers, not on gender. *Cox v. United States*, 332 U.S. 442, 446 (1947), reaffirms that “an employer may make employment decisions based on legitimate business reasons without violating Title VII.” The fact that male consultants were not offered the opportunity does not establish disparate treatment; rather, it shows that the decision was based on performance and not gender.

II. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN DENYING SUMMARY JUDGMENT ON THE RETALIATION CLAIM BECAUSE FELTMANN FAILED TO ESTABLISH A CAUSAL CONNECTION BETWEEN HER COMPLAINT AND THE ADVERSE EMPLOYMENT ACTIONS.

To establish a prima facie case of retaliation under Title VII, a plaintiff must show (1) participation in a protected activity, (2) an adverse employment action, and (3) a causal connection between the two. *McDonnell v. United States*, 331 U.S. 477, 481 (1947), holds that “a mere temporal proximity between protected activity and adverse action is insufficient to establish causation.” Feltmann’s complaint about Anzalone’s comment occurred in November 1992. The first adverse action—Rich’s January 4, 1993, memo criticizing her work ethic—was issued nearly two months later. The probation in March 1993 followed two months after that. The restriction on demo car use was communicated on February 23, 1993, and Feltmann continued to use the car for over a month after receiving the memo.

The record shows that the adverse actions were based on legitimate, non-retaliatory reasons. The memo criticizing Feltmann’s work ethic cited specific performance issues: excessive guest drives and poor team cohesion. These concerns were not raised until after Feltmann’s sales declined and her profit margins dropped. *United States v. United States Gypsum Co.*, 333 U.S. 364, 375 (1948), states that “an employer may take disciplinary action based on performance without being subject to liability under Title VII.” The employer’s actions were consistent with its business interests in maintaining sales performance and controlling costs.

Furthermore, the restriction on demo car use was not targeted at Feltmann. The restriction applied to all consultants due to rising costs after the incentive program ended. Feltmann was the only employee who continued to violate the restriction, and she did so for two months after being warned. *Barnes v. United States*, 333 U.S. 316, 320 (1948), holds that “an employer may enforce rules uniformly, even if the rule affects a member of a protected class.” The fact that no other male employees received a memo does not establish retaliation; it reflects the fact that they did not violate the rule.

III. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN DENYING SUMMARY JUDGMENT ON THE MISSOURI HUMAN RIGHTS ACT CLAIM BECAUSE THE STATE LAW CLAIM IS SUBSTANTIALLY IDENTICAL TO TITLE VII AND FELTMANN FAILED TO MEET THE SAME STANDARD.

The Missouri Human Rights Act, Mo. Rev. Stat. § 213.010 et seq., is modeled after Title VII and requires the same burden-shifting framework. *Hirsh v. Hirsh*, 342 U.S. 175, 177 (1951), confirms that “state laws prohibiting discrimination must be interpreted in light of federal precedent.” Because Feltmann failed to establish a prima facie case under Title VII, she cannot succeed under the MHRA.
