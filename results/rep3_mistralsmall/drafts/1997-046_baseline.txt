**ARGUMENT**

**I. OHIO’S CLEMENCY PROCESS CREATES A PROTECTED LIBERTY INTEREST IN LIFE AND LIBERTY THAT TRIGGERS DUE PROCESS PROTECTIONS.**

The Sixth Circuit erred in concluding that petitioner Woodard lacks a constitutionally protected liberty interest in clemency proceedings. This Court has long recognized that clemency is an integral part of the criminal justice system, not a mere act of grace. In *Greenholtz v. Inmates of Nebraska Penal & Correctional Complex*, 442 U.S. 1, 7 (1979), the Court held that parole release proceedings create a protected liberty interest where state law conditions release on specific findings. Similarly, clemency in Ohio is not an unfettered exercise of executive discretion; it is governed by statute and regulation, and its denial can result in the deprivation of life itself. *See Ohio Const. Art. III, § 11*; *State ex rel. Maurer v. Sheward*, 71 Ohio St.3d 513, 520 (1994) (noting the governor’s discretion is not absolute).

The Sixth Circuit’s reliance on *Connecticut Board of Pardons v. Dumschat*, 452 U.S. 458 (1981), is misplaced. *Dumschat* addressed whether a prisoner had a liberty interest in the *procedures* of clemency, not whether clemency itself implicates a protected liberty interest. *Id.* at 465. Here, Woodard does not challenge the procedures *per se*, but the coercive nature of the interview process, which threatens his Fifth Amendment rights. The Court in *Dumschat* explicitly declined to address whether clemency decisions implicate due process protections, reserving judgment on the issue. *Id.* at 467.

Moreover, the Sixth Circuit’s suggestion that clemency is too attenuated from trial to warrant due process protections misreads this Court’s precedents. In *Griffin v. Illinois*, 351 U.S. 12, 18 (1956), the Court held that due process attaches to any state procedure that is an "integral part" of the adjudicatory process. Clemency in Ohio is not a post-conviction afterthought; it is the final safeguard against wrongful execution. *See Ohio Dep’t of Rehab. & Corr., Death Penalty Clemency Procedure* (requiring clemency review 45 days before execution). Thus, due process protections must attach.

**II. OHIO’S CLEMENCY INTERVIEW PROCESS VIOLATES THE FIFTH AMENDMENT BY IMPOSING AN UNCONSTITUTIONAL CONDITION.**

The Sixth Circuit erred in concluding that Woodard’s Fifth Amendment rights are not violated by Ohio’s clemency interview process. The Fifth Amendment provides that no person "shall be compelled in any criminal case to be a witness against himself." U.S. Const. amend. V. The Court has held that the Fifth Amendment applies not only to criminal trials but to any proceeding where a witness’s statements may later be used against him. *See Baxter v. Palmigiano*, 425 U.S. 308, 318 (1976) (holding that a prisoner’s silence in a disciplinary hearing could be used against him in a subsequent criminal trial).

Ohio’s clemency process imposes an unconstitutional condition by conditioning the opportunity for clemency—a life-or-death proceeding—on the waiver of Fifth Amendment rights. The Court has repeatedly condemned such coercive tactics. In *Shelton v. Tucker*, 364 U.S. 479, 485 (1960), the Court struck down a state law requiring teachers to disclose all organizational affiliations, noting that such a condition "destroys or substantially restricts" constitutional rights. Similarly, Ohio’s clemency interview process forces petitioner to choose between asserting his Fifth Amendment rights and seeking clemency, thereby chilling his constitutional protections.

The Sixth Circuit’s suggestion that the process is voluntary is disingenuous. While participation is technically optional, the reality is that a death row inmate has no meaningful choice. As this Court has recognized, "the value of a constitutional right cannot be measured in terms of dollars and cents." *Griffin v. Illinois*, 351 U.S. at 20. The same principle applies here: the value of life cannot be measured against the risk of self-incrimination.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should reverse the Sixth Circuit’s judgment and hold that Ohio’s clemency process creates a protected liberty interest and that the interview requirement violates the Fifth Amendment.
