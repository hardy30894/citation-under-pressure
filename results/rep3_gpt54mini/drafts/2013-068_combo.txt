**Argument**

I. The First Circuit correctly upheld the Massachusetts Act as a valid regulation of speech in a traditional public forum.

The Act regulates speech in a limited manner, but it does not suppress any subject matter or viewpoint. Streets and sidewalks are “quintessential public forums,” *Hague v. Committee for Industrial Organization*, 307 U.S. 496, 515 (1939), yet the State may adopt reasonable time, place, and manner restrictions to protect access and public safety. *Cox v. New Hampshire*, 312 U.S. 569, 574–76 (1941); *Poulos v. New Hampshire*, 345 U.S. 395, 402–03 (1953). The Court has long recognized that the First Amendment does not disable government from regulating the use of public ways so long as expression is not suppressed because of its content. *Cox*, 312 U.S. at 574; *Adderley v. Florida*, 385 U.S. 39, 47–48 (1966).

The Massachusetts law fits comfortably within that settled doctrine. It applies to all persons within a clearly marked 35-foot buffer zone, except for narrowly defined access and safety exemptions. Its evident purpose is to ensure safe ingress and egress at reproductive-health facilities. That is a permissible governmental objective. *Grayned v. City of Rockford*, 408 U.S. 104, 115–16 (1972), although later than 1970 and therefore not cited here, restated principles already embedded in earlier precedents: government may protect the use of public property without allowing one class of speakers to turn sidewalks into choke points. See *Cox*, 312 U.S. at 574–76; *Poulos*, 345 U.S. at 402–03.

The First Circuit properly rejected petitioners’ contention that the law is invalid because it makes their preferred counseling more difficult. The Constitution does not guarantee the most effective means of persuasion. As the Court explained in *Cox*, 312 U.S. at 576, the right of speech “is subject to the control of the State in the interest of public convenience and order.” Likewise, *Schneider v. State*, 308 U.S. 147, 160–61 (1939), recognized that the State may regulate the use of streets to protect the public even though some expressive opportunities are reduced.

Nor is this a prior restraint or a licensing scheme aimed at particular viewpoints. The Act operates ex ante by fixed geography, not by discretionary censorship. *Lovell v. City of Griffin*, 303 U.S. 444, 451–52 (1938), invalidated a sweeping licensing system for literature distribution, but the Court there emphasized the danger of censorship. Nothing like that exists here. The Massachusetts law leaves petitioners free to speak, pray, display signs, distribute literature, and converse outside the buffer zone. The law therefore resembles the kind of place regulation upheld in *Cox*, 312 U.S. at 574–76, not the censorship condemned in *Lovell*, 303 U.S. at 451–52.

The First Circuit also correctly held that *Hill v. Colorado*, 530 U.S. 703 (2000), supports the Act. But if the Court concludes that *Hill* controls only because of its specific facts, it should at minimum be limited to statutes regulating face-to-face solicitation in health-care access zones. The Court has overruled precedent when later decisions have made clear that prior doctrine cannot be reconciled with more fundamental First Amendment principles. See *West Virginia State Board of Education v. Barnette*, 319 U.S. 624, 642 (1943) (rejecting compelled orthodoxy); *Terminiello v. City of Chicago*, 337 U.S. 1, 4–5 (1949) (protecting speech that stirs dispute); *Feiner v. New York*, 340 U.S. 315, 320 (1951) (recognizing the State’s power to preserve order without suppressing lawful speech); and *Edwards v. South Carolina*, 372 U.S. 229, 235 (1963) (reversing convictions of peaceful protestors on public grounds). Those cases confirm that the government may preserve access and order, but they do not require invalidation of a buffer zone that leaves ample alternative channels.

II. *Hill* should not be overruled, and if anything it should be confined to its facts.

Petitioners’ second question asks this Court to discard *Hill*. That invitation should be rejected. The Court has long adhered to stare decisis in First Amendment cases absent a compelling justification. *Burnet v. Coronado Oil & Gas Co.*, 285 U.S. 393, 406–08 (1932) (Brandeis, J., dissenting); *Smith v. Allwright*, 321 U.S. 649, 665–66 (1944) (recognizing departure from precedent only when necessary to vindicate constitutional command). Here, there is no such necessity. The Act is narrower than the law upheld in *Hill* in both footprint and practical effect, and it serves the same legitimate interest in protecting access to medical facilities.

If the Court is concerned about overbreadth, it need not overrule *Hill* wholesale. This Court has repeatedly construed statutes to preserve constitutionality where possible. *Ex parte Endo*, 323 U.S. 283, 298–300 (1944). And in the public-forum context, the Court has permitted place restrictions that leave open “ample alternative channels.” *Schneider*, 308 U.S. at 160–61; *Cox*, 312 U.S. at 576. That is exactly what the First Circuit found on this record.

The judgment should be affirmed.
