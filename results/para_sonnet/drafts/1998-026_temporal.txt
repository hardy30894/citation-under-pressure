**ARGUMENT**

**I. A trial court's failure to advise a defendant of his right to appeal, standing alone, does not entitle him to collateral relief.**

This Court has already confronted a case functionally identical to this one. In *Hill v. United States*, 368 U.S. 424 (1962), a sentencing judge failed to comply with the then-existing requirement of Rule 32(a) that a defendant be given an opportunity to speak before sentence was imposed. This Court held that such an omission, though a violation of the Rule, did not entitle the defendant to relief under 28 U.S.C. § 2255. The Court explained that "the failure of a trial judge to ask a defendant represented by counsel whether he wishes to exercise his right to make a statement before sentence is imposed is not of itself an error that can be raised by collateral attack." *Hill*, 368 U.S. at 428. The Court further emphasized that Rule 32 "is not derived from the Constitution," and that a violation of its terms is "not of the character or magnitude" required to justify relief upon a motion to vacate sentence. *Id.* at 429.

The logic of *Hill* controls this case with equal force. Just as the defendant in *Hill* suffered no cognizable prejudice from the judge's failure to invite him to speak, Peguero suffered none from the court's failure to recite what he already knew. A procedural rule promulgated to protect a right does not itself become the source of a substantive entitlement to relief when the right it was designed to protect has, by the defendant's own admission, already been secured. Rule 32's directive that the court "advise the defendant of his right to appeal" serves only an informational function; it exists to ensure that the defendant is not deprived of his opportunity to appeal through ignorance. Where the sentencing court's finding — undisturbed here — establishes that the defendant possessed that knowledge independently of the court's advisement, the very purpose the Rule was designed to serve has been fully accomplished.

**II. Any violation of Rule 32 in this case was harmless and does not warrant vacating the sentence.**

Rule 32's directive to advise a defendant of his appellate rights, like other rules of criminal procedure, is subject to the harmless-error principles this Court articulated in *Kotteakos v. United States*, 328 U.S. 750 (1946). There, the Court instructed that the inquiry on review is "not were there errors" in the proceedings below, but "whether the error itself had substantial influence" on the outcome. *Kotteakos*, 328 U.S. at 764–65. Only if a court, after examining the record, is left in "grave doubt" whether the error affected the outcome should relief be granted. *Id.* at 765.

Here there can be no such doubt. The District Court, after an evidentiary hearing, found as fact that Peguero knew of his right to appeal at the time of sentencing. That finding forecloses any argument that the omission of the formal advisement had the slightest "substantial influence" on Peguero's decision not to appeal. A defendant who already possesses the knowledge that a rule is designed to impart cannot claim that the failure to convey that same knowledge caused him any injury. The error, if it was one, was harmless in the most complete sense: it altered nothing in the defendant's understanding of his rights or his choices in exercising them.

This conclusion follows *a fortiori* from *Hill* itself, where the Court declined to find the Rule 32(a) violation cognizable notwithstanding the absence of any inquiry into the defendant's independent knowledge of his rights. If the mere failure to invite a statement — with no showing that the defendant knew of the right independently — was insufficient to warrant relief in *Hill*, then a technical failure to advise, coupled with an affirmative finding that the defendant already possessed the relevant knowledge, is necessarily insufficient here as well.

**III. Collateral relief is reserved for errors that undermine the fairness of the proceeding, not for technical omissions without consequence.**

This Court has long held that collateral attack is not a substitute for direct appeal and is not available to remedy every departure from procedural regularity. In *Sunal v. Large*, 332 U.S. 174 (1947), the Court explained that habeas corpus review "has traditionally been limited" to jurisdictional and constitutional defects and is not meant to serve as a vehicle for correcting mere procedural irregularities that could have been, but were not, raised on direct appeal. *Sunal*, 332 U.S. at 178–79. Likewise, in *Sunal* and in *Hill*, the Court recognized that not every deviation from the formalities of the Federal Rules rises to the level of a fundamental defect warranting the extraordinary remedy of vacating a final sentence.

Peguero's claim fits comfortably within the category of technical noncompliance that this Court's precedents have refused to elevate into a ground for collateral relief. The District Court's finding that Peguero knew of his appellate rights when sentence was imposed demonstrates that no fundamental unfairness attended his sentencing. To hold otherwise — to require automatic vacatur whenever a court's recitation of Rule 32 falls short, regardless of the defendant's actual knowledge — would convert a rule of orderly procedure into an independent source of substantive right, contrary to this Court's teaching in *Hill* that Rule 32 "is not derived from the Constitution" and does not carry that consequence. 368 U.S. at 429.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Court of Appeals should be affirmed.
