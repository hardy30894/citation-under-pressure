**ARGUMENT**

**I. Due Process Requires Only That a Defendant Be Competent Under the Standard of *Dusky v. United States*, Not Under a Heightened "Reasoned Choice" Test.**

This Court has long defined the constitutional floor of competency in a single, uniform way. In *Dusky v. United States*, 362 U.S. 402 (1960) (per curiam), the Court held that the "'test must be whether he has sufficient present ability to consult with his lawyer with a reasonable degree of rational understanding—and whether he has a rational as well as factual understanding of the proceedings against him.'" *Id.* at 402. *Dusky* did not purport to create a floor applicable only to standing trial; it announced the constitutional measure of mental competency itself. *Pate v. Robinson*, 383 U.S. 375 (1966), reaffirmed this same test, requiring that a defendant possess "a rational as well as factual understanding of the proceedings against him," *id.* at 385 (quoting *Dusky*), and made plain that the inquiry into capacity to consult with counsel and to comprehend the proceedings is the governing constitutional standard.

Nothing in the competency inquiry changes when the defendant, rather than proceeding to trial, chooses to plead guilty or to represent himself. The Ninth Circuit's demand for a distinct "reasoned choice" standard has no textual or doctrinal source in this Court's due process jurisprudence. It rests instead on the mistaken premise that the *decision* to waive rights requires greater rationality than the *capacity* to stand trial. But the competency inquiry has always asked whether the defendant possesses the mental capacity to understand and participate—not whether the choice he makes is wise, or is the choice a rational and counseled defendant would make. As this Court explained in *Massey v. Moore*, 348 U.S. 105 (1954), competency doctrine safeguards a defendant against being subjected to proceedings he cannot comprehend or in which he cannot rationally participate; it does not superintend the soundness of the particular decisions a competent defendant elects to make. *Id.* at 108.

**II. The Standards Governing Waiver of Counsel and Guilty Pleas Concern the Voluntariness and Knowledge of the Waiver, Not a Separate, Elevated Mental-Capacity Threshold.**

The decisions of this Court addressing waiver of counsel and guilty pleas confirm that the constitutional inquiry is whether the waiver was voluntary, knowing, and intelligent—not whether the defendant met some heightened capacity beyond *Dusky*. In *Johnson v. Zerbst*, 304 U.S. 458 (1938), the Court described waiver of counsel as "an intentional relinquishment or abandonment of a known right or privilege," *id.* at 464, and instructed courts to "indulge every reasonable presumption against waiver," *id.* at 464. That test asks about the defendant's knowledge and intent respecting his rights, a question distinct from the *Dusky* capacity inquiry, and one that in no way implies a separate, more demanding competency standard. *Von Moltke v. Gillies*, 332 U.S. 708 (1948), likewise required that a waiver of counsel be made "with eyes open," *id.* at 724, emphasizing the defendant's awareness of the risks of self-representation—again a voluntariness-and-knowledge inquiry, not a mental-capacity inquiry.

*Carter v. Illinois*, 329 U.S. 173 (1946), confirms that the focus is on whether the record shows the plea was made with understanding of its consequences, not on proof of some elevated capacity. There, the Court rejected the argument that the absence of counsel at arraignment supported an inference that the accused lacked the capacity to understand the proceedings leading to his conviction, holding instead that the relevant constitutional question turns on whether the record as a whole discloses that the defendant's plea was voluntary and understandingly made. And *Adams v. United States ex rel. McCann*, 317 U.S. 269 (1942), recognized that "the Constitution does not force a lawyer upon a defendant," *id.* at 279, because he may competently elect to conduct his own defense—an election this Court treated as a matter of intelligent waiver, not of superior mental competence. Similarly, *Boykin v. Alabama*, 395 U.S. 238 (1969), required that the record affirmatively show a guilty plea was voluntary and intelligent, explaining that "[s]everal federal constitutional rights are involved in a waiver that takes place" when a defendant pleads guilty, *id.* at 243, but *Boykin* nowhere suggested that the defendant must meet a heightened standard of rationality beyond that required to stand trial.

**III. *Westbrook v. Arizona* Does Not Establish a Substantive Competency Standard Higher Than *Dusky*.**

The Ninth Circuit's "reasoned choice" standard cannot be traced to *Westbrook v. Arizona*, 384 U.S. 150 (1966) (per curiam). *Westbrook* remanded because the record did not show that the trial court had determined "whether petitioner had the capacity to waive his constitutional right to the assistance of counsel," *id.* at 150, but the Court did not articulate any standard different from the one already governing competency generally, nor did it suggest that waiver of counsel demands a quantum of rationality beyond *Dusky*'s "reasonable degree of rational understanding." 362 U.S. at 402. *Westbrook* stands only for the unremarkable proposition that a court must separately consider whether a waiver occurred at all—a voluntariness question, not a new mental-capacity threshold.

**CONCLUSION**

The competency standard that governs whether a defendant may stand trial, articulated in *Dusky* and *Pate*, likewise governs whether he may plead guilty or waive counsel. The additional inquiries required by *Johnson*, *Von Moltke*, *Carter*, *Adams*, and *Boykin* concern the voluntariness and intelligence of the waiver, not a heightened competency threshold. The judgment of the Court of Appeals should be reversed.
