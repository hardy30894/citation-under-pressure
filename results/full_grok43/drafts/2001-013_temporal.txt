The Occupational Safety and Health Act reaches the working conditions aboard uninspected vessels such as Rig 52. Section 4(b)(1) withdraws coverage only where another federal agency has exercised statutory authority to prescribe or enforce standards affecting occupational safety or health. 29 U.S.C. § 653(b)(1). The Coast Guard’s general superintendence over safety of life and property on navigable waters, 14 U.S.C. § 2 (derived from Act of Jan. 28, 1915, ch. 20, § 1, 38 Stat. 800), does not constitute such an exercise when the vessel is uninspected and the agency has promulgated no comprehensive standards governing the particular hazards at issue.

Preemption of this character requires affirmative federal action that actually occupies the field. Where an agency possesses power yet has not issued regulations touching the precise subject, concurrent state or federal authority remains. Napier v. Atlantic Coast Line R. Co., 272 U.S. 605, 613 (1926) (“the intention to supersede the exercise of the state’s police power must be clearly manifested”); see also Minturn v. Maynard, 58 U.S. (17 How.) 477, 478 (1855) (admiralty jurisdiction does not displace other remedies absent specific federal regulation). The Coast Guard’s limited rules for uninspected vessels—confined to fire extinguishers, life preservers, flame arrestors, ventilation, and signaling equipment, 46 U.S.C. §§ 4102, 4103 (1958)—leave untouched the explosion-prevention and well-control procedures involved here. No “clear manifestation” of exclusive occupation therefore appears.

The statute’s text confirms the distinction. Congress conditioned preemption on the actual “exercise” of authority, not on the mere existence of a regulatory scheme. This language, read in light of contemporaneous decisions requiring concrete administrative action before displacement occurs, United States v. Grimaud, 220 U.S. 506, 521 (1911), preserves OSHA jurisdiction over uninspected vessels whose working conditions the Coast Guard has not addressed.

The Fifth Circuit’s contrary view would convert a limited maritime grant into an industry-wide exemption never enacted by Congress. Because the Coast Guard has not exercised the requisite authority, section 4(b)(1) does not bar the citations.
