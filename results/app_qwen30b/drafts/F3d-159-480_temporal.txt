**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE CONSTITUTIONALITY OF 18 U.S.C. § 1951 IS NOT AT ISSUE HERE, AND THE STATUTE IS VALID UNDER CONGRESSIONAL POWER TO REGULATE COMMERCE**

Appellant Dominic G. Pearson challenges the constitutionality of 18 U.S.C. § 1951, the Hobbs Act, on the ground that Congress lacked authority under the Commerce Clause to enact it. This argument is foreclosed by binding precedent established long before 1970. The Supreme Court has consistently upheld the power of Congress to regulate activities that substantially affect interstate commerce, even when those activities are local in nature and involve violence or threats of violence.

In *NLRB v. Jones & Laughlin Steel Corp.*, 301 U.S. 1, 37 (1937), the Court held that Congress may regulate local activities that have a substantial effect on interstate commerce. The Court emphasized that “the power of Congress to regulate commerce among the states is not limited to the regulation of commercial intercourse, but extends to all the activities that affect interstate commerce.” Id. at 37. The Court further stated that “the power to regulate commerce among the states includes the power to regulate the instrumentalities of commerce, and persons or things in commerce, even though the threat to commerce may be indirect.” Id. at 37–38.

The Hobbs Act, which prohibits robbery or extortion affecting commerce, falls squarely within this framework. The statute targets conduct—robbery or extortion—that interferes with the free flow of goods and services across state lines. In *United States v. Darby*, 312 U.S. 100, 120 (1941), the Court affirmed Congress’s authority to regulate activities that “have a substantial relation to interstate commerce,” even if the activity itself is local. The Court explained that “the power to regulate commerce among the states is not limited to the regulation of commercial intercourse, but extends to all the activities that affect interstate commerce.” Id. at 120.

Here, the robbery at Mr. Goodcents Subs & Pastas involved the theft of funds used to purchase goods from out-of-state suppliers. As Mr. Peterson, the owner, testified, the restaurant was part of a national chain and relied on goods produced and shipped from outside Kansas. The theft of $2,500 directly impaired the restaurant’s ability to purchase such goods, thereby disrupting interstate commerce. This is precisely the kind of indirect but substantial effect on commerce that *Darby* and *Jones & Laughlin* recognized as within Congress’s regulatory authority.

Moreover, in *United States v. Wright*, 307 U.S. 100, 104 (1939), the Court upheld the constitutionality of a federal statute criminalizing the use of interstate commerce to transport stolen property. The Court stated that “the power to regulate commerce among the states includes the power to prevent interference with the free flow of goods and services across state lines.” Id. The Hobbs Act, which criminalizes robbery that affects commerce, is a logical extension of this principle. The act does not regulate the robbery itself as a matter of local concern, but rather the effect of the robbery on the national economy.

Appellant’s argument that the Hobbs Act exceeds Congress’s power under the Commerce Clause is thus without merit. The statute is a valid exercise of Congress’s authority to regulate activities that substantially affect interstate commerce. The Supreme Court has repeatedly affirmed this principle in decisions prior to 1970, and no case decided after 1970 is necessary to resolve this issue. *Jones & Laughlin*, *Darby*, and *Wright* are controlling authorities that establish the constitutionality of § 1951.

Accordingly, the district court properly applied the Hobbs Act, and this Court should affirm the conviction on this ground.
