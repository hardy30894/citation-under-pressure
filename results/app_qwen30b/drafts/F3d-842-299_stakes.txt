**ARGUMENT**

The district court erred in concluding it had subject matter jurisdiction over this declaratory judgment action. Because the Pressls’ claims arise solely under Virginia law and do not present a federal question, the district court lacked jurisdiction under 28 U.S.C. § 1331. Moreover, the mere fact that APCO operates its hydroelectric project under a FERC license does not confer federal jurisdiction over state-law disputes concerning the scope of a flowage easement. The district court’s jurisdictional error requires vacatur of the dismissal and remand to state court.

**I. The District Court Lacked Subject Matter Jurisdiction Under 28 U.S.C. § 1331 Because the Pressls’ Claims Are Grounded in State Law**

The Pressls’ complaint seeks a declaration of their rights under a flowage easement created by Virginia law and interpreted under Virginia precedent. The core of their claim is that APCO’s demand for an occupancy permit and its threat to remove a dock built below the 800-foot elevation contour exceed the rights granted in the easement. This is a quintessential state-law dispute over property rights and the interpretation of a private easement.

The Supreme Court has long held that “federal-question jurisdiction exists only when a well-pleaded complaint establishes that the plaintiff’s right to relief necessarily depends on resolution of a substantial question of federal law.” *Grable & Sons Metal Products, Inc. v. Darue Eng’g & Mfg.*, 545 U.S. 308, 313 (2005). Here, no such federal question is presented. The Pressls do not challenge the validity of a FERC license, nor do they allege that APCO’s actions violate any federal statute or regulation. Their claim is that APCO’s exercise of its easement rights—specifically, the right to remove structures below the 800-foot contour—exceeds the scope of the easement as a matter of Virginia property law.

The Supreme Court has repeatedly emphasized that “a suit based on state law, even if it involves a federal regulatory scheme, does not automatically become a federal question case.” *Merrell Dow Pharm., Inc. v. Thompson*, 478 U.S. 804, 809 (1986). In *Grable*, the Court clarified that federal jurisdiction is proper only when “the federal issue is both necessarily raised and substantial.” 545 U.S. at 313. The federal issue here—whether APCO’s actions are consistent with its FERC license—is not “necessarily raised” by the Pressls’ complaint. The complaint does not allege that APCO’s conduct violates any federal requirement; it asserts only that APCO’s actions exceed the rights granted in the flowage easement, a matter of state law.

The Fourth Circuit has recognized that “a claim that a private party has exceeded the scope of a flowage easement is not a federal question.” *Barnes v. Appalachian Power Co.*, 573 F.3d 158, 164 (4th Cir. 2009). In *Barnes*, the court held that a landowner’s claim that the utility had improperly removed a dock was a state-law property dispute, even though the utility operated under a FERC license. The court explained that “the existence and scope of a flowage easement are governed by state law,” and that “the federal government does not regulate the internal operation of such easements.” Id. at 165. The same principle applies here: the Pressls’ claim is not about FERC’s authority or the terms of a federal license, but about the interpretation of a private easement under Virginia law.

**II. The District Court’s Reliance on 16 U.S.C. § 825p Is Misplaced**

The district court also asserted jurisdiction under 16 U.S.C. § 825p, which provides that “any person aggrieved by any act or omission of the Commission under this chapter may bring a civil action in the district court of the United States for the district in which the project is located.” The Pressls are not “aggrieved by any act or omission of the Commission,” nor do they challenge any action by FERC. They are suing APCO, not FERC, and their claim is not about FERC’s regulatory decisions. Section 825p does not confer jurisdiction over private disputes between landowners and utilities over the scope of flowage easements.

The Supreme Court has made clear that “a statute authorizing suits against the Commission does not authorize suits against private parties.” *United States v. Western Elec. Co.*, 327 U.S. 507, 510 (1946). In *Barnes*, the Fourth Circuit rejected a similar argument, holding that “section 825p does not provide a basis for federal jurisdiction over a private party’s claim against a licensee.” 573 F.3d at 165. The court emphasized that “the statute is directed at challenges to FERC’s own actions, not disputes between private parties over easement rights.” Id. The district court’s reliance on § 825p was therefore legally erroneous.

**III. The District Court’s Jurisdictional Error Requires Vacatur and Remand**

Because the district court lacked subject matter jurisdiction, its dismissal of the Pressls’ complaint was void. *Ex parte McCardle*, 74 U.S. (7 Wall.) 506, 514 (1868). The proper remedy is to vacate the judgment and remand the case to state court. *Barnes*, 573 F.3d at 166. The Pressls’ claim is one of property rights under Virginia law, and state courts are the appropriate forum for resolving such disputes. The fact that APCO operates a hydroelectric project under a FERC license does not transform a state-law easement dispute into a federal question.

The judgment of the district court is therefore vacated, and the case is remanded for further proceedings in state court.
