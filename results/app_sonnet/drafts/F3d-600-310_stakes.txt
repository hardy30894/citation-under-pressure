ARGUMENT

I. The Bankruptcy Court Lacked Subject-Matter Jurisdiction to Determine Post-Petition Interest and Collection Costs on a Nondischargeable Debt After the Chapter 13 Case Was Closed.

Bankruptcy jurisdiction is not general jurisdiction; it is confined to proceedings "arising under title 11, or arising in or related to cases under title 11." 28 U.S.C. § 1334(b). That grant, though broad, is not limitless. Celotex Corp. v. Edwards, 514 U.S. 300, 308 (1995) (jurisdiction extends to matters "conceivably" affecting the estate, but not to matters wholly extraneous to it). Where, as here, the bankruptcy case has closed, the debtor has been discharged, and the estate has been fully administered, a bankruptcy court's authority to resolve a dispute between a creditor and a former debtor contracts sharply.

This Court has recognized that once a plan is confirmed and a case winds toward closing, the touchstone for jurisdiction is whether the matter bears a "close nexus to the bankruptcy plan or proceeding sufficient to uphold bankruptcy court jurisdiction." In re Valley Historic Ltd. P'ship, 486 F.3d 831, 836 (4th Cir. 2007) (quoting In re Resorts Int'l, Inc., 372 F.3d 154, 166-67 (3d Cir. 2004)). The dispute over post-petition interest and collection costs on Kirkland's ECMC loan has no such nexus. Kirkland's student loan was excepted from discharge under 11 U.S.C. § 523(a)(8) absent a showing of undue hardship, a showing Kirkland never attempted. See Grogan v. Garner, 498 U.S. 279, 283-84 (1991) (discussing the "undue hardship" exception for educational loans). Because the debt survived the bankruptcy untouched by discharge, the amount ECMC could collect on it after the case closed is a matter of the parties' promissory note and non-bankruptcy law — precisely the kind of postclosing, non-estate dispute that lies outside the "arising in" and "related to" categories of § 1334(b). The Chapter 13 estate had already been fully administered, the Trustee's final report filed, and the case closed before this obligation and its associated interest and costs were even at issue; there was, by the time of the adversary proceeding, no estate left for the outcome of the interest calculation to affect. See Celotex, 514 U.S. at 308 n.6 (jurisdiction depends on the proceeding's potential effect on the estate).

Nor can jurisdiction be salvaged on the theory that the bankruptcy court was merely enforcing its own discharge order. Cf. Travelers Indem. Co. v. Bailey, 557 U.S. 137, 151 (2009) (bankruptcy court retains jurisdiction to interpret and enforce its own prior orders). ECMC's claim was never discharged, and the bankruptcy court did not purport to interpret the discharge order — it calculated, from scratch, a fresh sum of post-petition interest owed on a debt that had never been part of the discharged estate. That exercise is not enforcement of a bankruptcy order; it is adjudication of a garden-variety debt-collection dispute that Congress did not commit to the bankruptcy court once the case was closed.

II. Even If Jurisdiction Existed, the Bankruptcy Court Exceeded the Scope of the Adversary Proceeding Actually Before It.

Kirkland's Complaint to Determine Dischargeability of Debt asked the bankruptcy court to decide a single, narrow question: whether the principal on claim no. 9 had "been paid in full and discharged except to the extent of any interest that may have" accrued during the bankruptcy. J.A. 5. Kirkland expressly conceded that any interest accruing during the Chapter 13 term was "non-dischargeable" and that she remained responsible for it. Neither party asked the court to fix the dollar amount of that interest or to rule on collection costs; ECMC's position throughout was that the court could not adjudicate any aspect of the loan absent an undue-hardship finding. The bankruptcy court nonetheless went beyond the relief either side requested, awarding ECMC a specific interest figure — $184.40 — and separately addressing collection costs, matters outside the four corners of the pleadings and the parties' arguments.

A court's authority to resolve a case is bounded by the claims the parties place before it. The bankruptcy court's own order acknowledged that Kirkland and ECMC merely "agree that some amount of interest has accrued," J.A. 87 — a far cry from a joint request that the court quantify that amount or adjudicate collection costs neither side had briefed. Having found that no payment was ever made on claim no. 9 and that the Trustee "cannot unilaterally reduce the amount of an allowed claim," J.A. 86, the correct course was to hold that the debt — principal, interest, and any costs — remained ECMC's to collect outside the bankruptcy forum, not to convert an unlitigated ancillary issue into a final money judgment.

CONCLUSION

For these reasons, the bankruptcy court lacked jurisdiction to determine the post-petition interest and collection costs due on ECMC's claim after the Chapter 13 case closed, and, even if jurisdiction existed, it exceeded the scope of the proceeding actually before it. The judgment below should be reversed insofar as it purports to fix the amount of post-petition interest and to adjudicate collection costs.
