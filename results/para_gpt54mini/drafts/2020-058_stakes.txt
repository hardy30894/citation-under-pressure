**ARGUMENT**

Section 1254a(f)(4) does not itself make a TPS recipient “admitted,” but it does place the recipient in “lawful status as a nonimmigrant” for purposes of adjustment. 8 U.S.C. § 1254a(f)(4). That statutory instruction must be read together with § 1255(a), which allows adjustment only for an alien “inspected and admitted or paroled into the United States.” 8 U.S.C. § 1255(a). Congress often uses “admission” and “status” as distinct concepts. See *Hanif v. Att’y Gen.*, 694 F.3d 479, 485 (3d Cir. 2012) (“[t]he date of gaining a new status is not the same as the date of the physical event of entering the country”); *Taveras v. Att’y Gen.*, 731 F.3d 281, 290 (3d Cir. 2013) (explaining that “admission” involves “physical entrance into the country”); *Gomez v. Lynch*, 831 F.3d 652, 658 (5th Cir. 2016) (“Admission and status are fundamentally distinct concepts.”). But the question here is not whether status and admission are identical; it is whether Congress used TPS to satisfy § 1255(a)’s threshold requirement. It did.

The text supports petitioner. Congress defined “admission” as “the lawful entry of the alien into the United States after inspection and authorization by an immigration officer.” 8 U.S.C. § 1101(a)(13)(A). Yet Congress also directed that a TPS recipient “shall be considered as being in, and maintaining, lawful status as a nonimmigrant” for adjustment-of-status purposes. 8 U.S.C. § 1254a(f)(4). For an applicant who is already physically present in the United States under a federal immigration status conferred by statute, that command supplies the lawful admission needed to proceed under § 1255(a). See *Ramirez v. Brown*, 852 F.3d 954, 960–62 (9th Cir. 2017); *Flores v. United States Citizenship & Immigr. Servs.*, 718 F.3d 548, 553–55 (6th Cir. 2013). Congress knew how to say “admitted” when it meant to require an entry inspection; it chose instead to grant TPS recipients lawful nonimmigrant status that makes adjustment available. See *In re Espinosa-Guillot*, 25 I. & N. Dec. 653, 654 (B.I.A. 2011) (“An adjustment of status generally constitutes an admission.”).

That reading also fits the structure of § 1255. Subsection (a) sets out the baseline rule. Other provisions create exceptions, including § 1255(k), which refers to an alien “present in the United States pursuant to a lawful admission,” and § 1255(m)(1), which allows adjustment for “an alien admitted into the United States (or otherwise provided nonimmigrant status).” 8 U.S.C. § 1255(k)(1), (m)(1). Those provisions show that Congress understood lawful immigration status and admission as related but not mutually exclusive. See *In re H-G-G-*, 27 I. & N. Dec. 617, 635 (A.A.O. 2019) (recognizing that some grants of lawful status do not constitute an admission, while others may satisfy adjustment requirements by statute or regulation). Petitioner’s interpretation gives effect to § 1254a(f)(4) without collapsing it into surplusage. See *Hibbs v. Winn*, 542 U.S. 88, 101 (2004) (“A statute should be construed so that effect is given to all its provisions, so that no part will be inoperative or superfluous, void or insignificant.”).

Finally, the purpose of TPS confirms petitioner’s reading. TPS was enacted to protect nationals of designated countries from removal and to allow them to live and work lawfully in the United States while conditions abroad remain unsafe. See *De Leon-Ochoa v. Att’y Gen.*, 622 F.3d 341, 353–54 (3d Cir. 2010). Denying adjustment to TPS recipients who were later placed in lawful nonimmigrant status frustrates that remedial purpose by forcing them to remain in limbo despite Congress’s express grant of lawful status. Courts should avoid reading a humanitarian statute so narrowly that it defeats the relief Congress provided. See *Serrano v. Att’y Gen.*, 655 F.3d 1260, 1265 (11th Cir. 2011) (recognizing that TPS confers “lawful status as a nonimmigrant” for adjustment purposes).

Because petitioner was inspected, authorized to remain, and placed in lawful nonimmigrant status under § 1254a(f)(4), he satisfied § 1255(a)’s admission requirement. The judgment below should be reversed.
