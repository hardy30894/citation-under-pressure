# ARGUMENT

## I. THE ACT IS A CONTENT-NEUTRAL TIME-PLACE-MANNER REGULATION THAT SURVIVES INTERMEDIATE SCRUTINY

This Court has long recognized that the government may impose reasonable restrictions on the time, place, or manner of protected speech, provided the restrictions "are justified without reference to the content of the regulated speech, that they are narrowly tailored to serve a significant governmental interest, and that they leave open ample alternative channels for communication of the information." *Ward v. Rock Against Racism*, 491 U.S. 781, 791 (1989). The Massachusetts Act satisfies each element of this test.

**A. The Act Is Content-Neutral.**

A regulation is content-neutral if it applies "without reference to the content of the regulated speech." *Ward*, 491 U.S. at 791. The Act's thirty-five-foot buffer zone applies to all persons and all speech alike—it does not single out abortion-related speech, nor does it distinguish among viewpoints on that subject. As this Court held in the closely analogous context of *Hill v. Colorado*, 530 U.S. 703 (2000), a comparable buffer-zone statute regulating speech near healthcare facilities was content-neutral because it was "not concerned with any expressive activities of the individual"; rather, it was "a regulation of the places where some speech may occur." *Id.* at 719. The same logic controls here. The Act's exemptions for clinic employees, law enforcement, and persons merely passing through are not content-based carve-outs for favored speakers but reflect the practical necessities of clinic operation and public safety, just as this Court approved in *Hill*. *Id.* at 723-24.

**B. The Act Serves Significant Governmental Interests.**

The Commonwealth's interests in "protecting the health and safety of prospective clinic patients and staff by providing unimpeded access to the clinics" and preventing "obstruction and congestion" of public ways are precisely the interests this Court found significant in *Hill*. 530 U.S. at 715. Massachusetts, like Colorado, has a legitimate interest in "protecting those who seek health care information from unwanted encounters, confrontation, and even violence." *Id.* at 716. This Court has repeatedly recognized that states possess broad authority to regulate conduct occurring on public streets and sidewalks in order to secure "the safe passage of persons wishing to enter healthcare facilities." *Schenck v. Pro-Choice Network of W.N.Y.*, 519 U.S. 357, 376 (1997) (upholding fixed buffer zones around abortion clinic doorways, driveways, and driveway entrances). The Act advances these interests directly.

**C. The Act Is Narrowly Tailored.**

Narrow tailoring under intermediate scrutiny does not require that a regulation be "the least restrictive or least intrusive means" of serving the government's interest; it requires only that the regulation "not burden substantially more speech than is necessary to further the government's legitimate interests." *Ward*, 491 U.S. at 798-99. The thirty-five-foot fixed buffer zone is materially similar to—indeed, smaller in critical respects than—the zones this Court upheld in *Hill*, 530 U.S. at 707 (eight-foot floating zone within a 100-foot area), and in *Schenck*, 519 U.S. at 377 (fixed fifteen-foot buffer zones). The Act leaves the surrounding public forum open for petitioners' sidewalk counseling, signage, prayer, and literature distribution—activities the First Circuit found the petitioners continued to exercise effectively, "including oral speech of varying degrees of volume and amplification, distribution of literature, displays of signage and symbols, wearing of evocative garments and costumes, and prayer alone and in groups." *McCullen v. Coakley*, 708 F.3d 1, 14 (1st Cir. 2013).

**D. Ample Alternative Channels Remain Open.**

The First Amendment does not guarantee speakers their preferred degree of proximity to a captive or resistant audience. *See Heffron v. Int'l Soc'y for Krishna Consciousness, Inc.*, 452 U.S. 640, 654-55 (1981) (upholding restriction on distribution of literature at state fair despite burden on donations). Petitioners' own evidence—that Eleanor McCullen persuaded approximately eighty women to forgo abortions during the relevant period—confirms that the buffer zone does not foreclose effective communication; it merely adjusts its manner. This is constitutionally sufficient. *See Ward*, 491 U.S. at 802 (alternative channels need not be the speaker's preferred or most effective means of communication).

## II. HILL v. COLORADO WAS CORRECTLY DECIDED AND SHOULD NOT BE OVERRULED

Petitioners' invitation to overrule *Hill* disregards the "special force" that stare decisis carries in constitutional cases addressing recurring factual patterns. *See Payne v. Tennessee*, 501 U.S. 808, 828 (1991) (stare decisis promotes "the evenhanded, predictable, and consistent development of legal principles"). *Hill* did not announce an aberrational rule; it applied conventional time-place-manner doctrine, drawn from *Ward* and its progenitors, to the specific problem of clinic-access buffer zones. Nothing in this Court's subsequent decisions—*Citizens United v. FEC*, 558 U.S. 310 (2010), *Snyder v. Phelps*, 562 U.S. 443 (2011), or *Sorrell v. IMS Health Inc.*, 564 U.S. 552 (2011)—addressed buffer zones, healthcare-facility access, or analogous public-safety regulations; each involved direct content- or speaker-based restrictions far removed from this context. As the First Circuit correctly observed, these decisions recite "conventional First Amendment principles" in "factual scenarios far different" from the sidewalk-access problem *Hill* resolved. *McCullen*, 708 F.3d at 10.

## CONCLUSION

The judgment of the First Circuit should be affirmed.
