The issue presented is whether the rule announced in Johnson v. United States, regarding what constitutes a violent offense, applies retroactively. However, the actual case of Johnson v. The Armed Career Criminal Act (ACCA) imposes a minimum 15-year sentence on individuals with three prior convictions for violent felonies or serious drug offenses. The definition of "violent felony" under the ACCA includes crimes that have as an element the "use, attempted use, or threatened use of physical force against the person of another" or crimes that "otherwise involve conduct that presents a serious potential risk of physical injury to another." The latter clause, known as the residual clause, was held to be unconstitutionally vague in Johnson v. United States.

To determine whether the rule applies retroactively, we look to the principles established by the Supreme Court in cases decided before 1970. In Linkletter v. Walker, 381 U.S. 618 (1965), the Court addressed the retroactivity of its decisions, holding that the Constitution neither prohibits nor requires retrospective effect for its decisions (Linkletter, 381 U.S. at 628-629). The Court further elaborated on this principle in Tehan v. United States ex rel. Shott, 382 U.S. 406 (1966), stating that the retroactivity of a decision depends on a balancing of the purpose of the rule, the extent of reliance on the prior rule, and the effect of retroactivity on the administration of justice (Tehan, 382 U.S. at 410).

The principle that the Supreme Court's decisions interpreting statutes are fully retroactive is supported by earlier cases. In United States v. Schooner Peggy, 5 U.S. 103 (1801), the Court held that a change in the law during the pendency of an appeal must be applied by the appellate court (United States v. Schooner Peggy, 5 U.S. at 110). Similarly, in Vandenbark v. Owens-Illinois Glass Co., 311 U.S. 538 (1941), the Court reaffirmed that a change in the law, including a Supreme Court decision interpreting a statute, applies to cases pending on appeal (Vandenbark, 311 U.S. at 541).

Furthermore, the Court has long recognized that due process requires fair notice of what conduct is prohibited. In Lanzetta v. New Jersey, 306 U.S. 451 (1939), the Court struck down a statute as unconstitutionally vague because it failed to provide adequate notice of the conduct it proscribed (Lanzetta, 306 U.S. at 453). Similarly, in Connally v. General Construction Co., 269 U.S. 385 (1926), the Court held that a statute must be sufficiently definite to inform individuals of the conduct required or prohibited (Connally, 269 U.S. at 391).

The vagueness doctrine, as applied in cases like Lanzetta and Connally, underpins the reasoning in Johnson v. United States regarding the residual clause's unconstitutional vagueness. Given the importance of fair notice and the prevention of arbitrary enforcement, as emphasized in these earlier cases, it is reasonable to argue that the rule announced in Johnson should apply retroactively to ensure that individuals are not sentenced under a vague and arbitrary provision.

Additionally, the Court's decision in Griffin v. Illinois, 351 U.S. 12 (1956), highlights the importance of equal justice and the need to correct past injustices through the retroactive application of new rules (Griffin, 351 U.S. at 19). While Griffin dealt with the right to appellate review, its underlying principle supports the retroactive application of significant constitutional or statutory interpretations.
