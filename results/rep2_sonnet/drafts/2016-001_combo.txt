# ARGUMENT

## I. Section 289's Text and History Confine "Total Profit" to the Profit Attributable to the Infringed Article of Manufacture, Not Necessarily the Entire End Product

The question presented turns on the meaning of "article of manufacture" in 35 U.S.C. § 289, which permits recovery of an infringer's "total profit" from "the manufacture or sale" of "any article of manufacture to which [a patented] design or a colorable imitation thereof has been applied." The Federal Circuit's rule—that the relevant "article of manufacture" must always be the end product sold to consumers—finds no support in the statute's text and cannot be reconciled with this Court's longstanding design-patent jurisprudence.

This Court has repeatedly recognized that a design patent protects only the specific ornamental design claimed, not the underlying functional article as a whole. In *Gorham Co. v. White*, 81 U.S. (14 Wall.) 511 (1871), this Court explained that a design patent protects "the appearance," not "the elements of the compound of shape and material." Id. at 525. The design itself—not the product to which it is applied—is the invention. This distinction between the patented design and the product embodying it is fundamental to design-patent law, and it forecloses any presumption that the "article of manufacture" under § 289 must be coextensive with the entire commercial product.

The predecessor damages provisions confirm this understanding. In *Dobson v. Dornan*, 118 U.S. 10 (1886), and its companion case *Dobson v. Hartford Carpet Co.*, 114 U.S. 439 (1885), this Court construed the 1887 Act's precursor and held that a patentee must prove what profit is actually attributable to the patented design, rather than presuming the entirety of the defendant's profit flows from the design. The Court in *Dobson v. Hartford Carpet Co.* rejected the notion that all profits on a carpet necessarily derived from its ornamental pattern, explaining that the patentee bears the burden of showing "what profit or damage was due to the design." 114 U.S. at 444. Congress responded to the evidentiary difficulties that *Dobson* created not by declaring that profits must always be measured against the whole end product, but by enacting the total-profit remedy of the 1887 Act—now § 289—as a measure of damages attributable to the design's application to whatever article the design is applied.

## II. The Apportionment Principle Embedded in Patent Damages Law Applies With Equal Force Here

This Court's broader patent-damages jurisprudence confirms that profit awards must be tethered to the value actually contributed by the patented feature, not by the product as a whole merely because the product happens to incorporate the patented feature. In *Garretson v. Clark*, 111 U.S. 120 (1884), the Court articulated the now-familiar apportionment rule for utility patents, holding that a patentee "must in every case give evidence tending to separate or apportion the defendant's profits and the patentee's damages between the patented feature and the unpatented features." Id. at 121. The Court explained that damages must be limited to "the extent of the difference between the patented device or process and what was well known before," id. at 122, a principle that resists any categorical rule locking profit calculations to an entire multi-component product.

Although *Garretson* concerned utility-patent damages under a differently worded statute, this Court has recognized that the animating concern—that a patentee should not receive a windfall attributable to features it did not invent—pervades patent-remedies law generally. In *Seymour v. McCormick*, 57 U.S. (16 How.) 480 (1854), the Court cautioned against damages awards that would give the patentee "the whole profit of the whole machine," id. at 491, when the patented improvement contributed only a portion of the product's value. The same logic applies to design patents embodied in complex multi-component devices such as smartphones. A modern smartphone comprises hundreds of components—processors, antennae, sensors, glass, and software—only a handful of which relate to the claimed ornamental designs at issue here (a rounded rectangular front face, a bezel, and a grid of colorful icons). Extending "total profit" to the entire phone, rather than to the "article of manufacture" the design is actually applied to, is inconsistent with the apportionment principle undergirding patent-remedies doctrine as a whole.

## III. The Federal Circuit's Rule Ignores the Multi-Component Reality of Modern Devices

This Court has long recognized that damages remedies must be adapted to the technological realities of the products before it. In *Cimiotti Unhairing Co. v. American Fur Refining Co.*, 198 U.S. 399 (1905), the Court refused to award profits attributable to a manufacturing process where the patented feature contributed only incrementally to a larger commercial product, explaining that recovery must reflect the "advantage" the infringer derived specifically from the patented feature, not from the whole article. Id. at 409–10. Likewise, in *Elizabeth v. Pavement Co.*, 97 U.S. 126 (1878), the Court cautioned that patent remedies could not be so construed as to hand patentees a windfall disconnected from the actual contribution of the patented improvement.

A smartphone is the paradigmatic multi-component product these precedents anticipated. Where, as here, the patented design covers only a portion of the product's overall appearance—here, specific exterior design elements rather than the internal engineering, network functionality, or unrelated components of Samsung's smartphones—§ 289's "article of manufacture" should permit the factfinder to identify the relevant component to which the design was applied, consistent with the apportionment principles this Court has applied across a century of patent-damages decisions from *Seymour* through *Cimiotti*.

## IV. The Remedy Should Be a Remand for Proper Identification of the Relevant Article

Because the courts below applied a categorical rule equating "article of manufacture" with the entire smartphone, they never permitted the jury to consider whether the relevant article was some component thereof. This error requires reversal and remand for a properly instructed damages proceeding consistent with *Dobson*, *Garretson*, and their progeny.
