**ARGUMENT**

I. A court of appeals conducting plain-error review under Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 52(b) may examine the entire record, not merely the trial evidence, to determine whether an error affected substantial rights or the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of the proceedings.

Rule 52(b) authorizes correction of a plain error that affects substantial rights. United States v. Olano, 507 U.S. 725, 731-32 (1993). The third prong requires the defendant to show prejudice, i.e., “a reasonable probability that, but for the error, the outcome of the proceeding would have been different.” Molina-Martinez v. United States, 578 U.S. 189, 194 (2016) (quoting United States v. Dominguez Benitez, 542 U.S. 74, 82 (2004)). That probability is assessed by reference to “the record as a whole,” not an artificially truncated subset. Johnson v. United States, 520 U.S. 461, 469 (1997) (considering “the evidence against” the defendant outside the specific element omitted from the jury charge).

The same record-wide inquiry governs the fourth prong. In United States v. Young, 470 U.S. 1, 16 (1985), this Court held that an appellate court must “view the error in the context of the entire record” before deciding whether it “seriously affect[ed] the fairness, integrity or public reputation of judicial proceedings.” Olano, 507 U.S. at 736 (quoting United States v. Atkinson, 297 U.S. 157, 160 (1936)). Nothing in Rule 52(b) or these precedents confines the inquiry to evidence actually admitted before the jury.

II. The Eleventh Circuit therefore acted properly when it consulted Greer’s prior convictions and the full record to conclude that the Rehaif error neither prejudiced him nor undermined the integrity of the proceedings.

Greer’s five prior aggravated-burglary convictions, all of which carried sentences exceeding one year, were undisputed matters of public record. Their existence made it “overwhelming[ly]” improbable that Greer was unaware of his felon status when he possessed the firearm. See Johnson, 520 U.S. at 469-70 (no plain-error relief where omitted element was “essentially uncontroverted”). Because the record as a whole supplied no reasonable probability of a different outcome, and because the error did not “seriously affect[] the fairness, integrity or public reputation of judicial proceedings,” Olano, 507 U.S. at 736, the court of appeals correctly denied relief.

The judgment should be affirmed.
