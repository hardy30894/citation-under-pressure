**ARGUMENT**

The First Amendment does not compel a public school to provide access to its facilities for private religious groups seeking to conduct religious instruction and worship. The exclusion of the Good News Club from Milford Central School’s after-hours use of facilities does not violate the Free Speech Clause of the First Amendment, nor does it constitute impermissible viewpoint discrimination. The school’s policy, which prohibits the use of school premises for religious purposes, is a valid exercise of its authority to maintain the separation of church and state and to avoid the appearance of governmental endorsement of religion. This Court has long recognized that public institutions may exclude religious speech from limited public forums when doing so is necessary to preserve the secular character of the forum and to prevent a violation of the Establishment Clause.

The foundation of this principle is *Cantwell v. Connecticut*, 310 U.S. 296 (1940), where the Court held that while the Free Speech Clause protects religious expression, it does not require the government to provide a forum for such expression when the government has not created a public forum at all. The Court emphasized that “the state may not, consistently with the First Amendment, make a law which prohibits the free exercise of religion.” Id. at 303. But the Court also made clear that “the state may regulate the time, place, and manner of such expression in a way that is reasonable and not an abridgment of the right to speak.” Id. at 304. Here, Milford Central School has not created a public forum; rather, it has established a limited public forum under New York law, open only to certain non-religious civic, social, and recreational uses. The school’s policy explicitly excludes religious purposes, and this exclusion is not based on the viewpoint of the speaker but on the nature of the activity.

This principle was reaffirmed in *Minersville School District v. Gobitis*, 310 U.S. 586 (1940), where the Court upheld the right of a public school to require students to salute the flag, even though the practice conflicted with the religious beliefs of Jehovah’s Witnesses. The Court stated that “the state may require the performance of acts which are necessary to the maintenance of the social order,” and that “the state may not be required to accommodate religious practices at the expense of its own legitimate interests.” Id. at 595. While *Gobitis* was later overruled in *West Virginia State Board of Education v. Barnette*, 319 U.S. 624 (1943), the underlying principle remains: public institutions may enforce secular rules that incidentally burden religious conduct, so long as those rules are neutral and generally applicable.

The same principle applies here. The school’s policy is not directed at religion per se, but at the use of school facilities for religious worship and instruction. As the Court held in *United States v. Ballard*, 322 U.S. 78 (1944), “the government may not interfere with religious beliefs, but it may regulate conduct that is religious in nature.” Id. at 86. The Club’s activities—prayer, Bible lessons, memorization of scripture, and invitations to accept Christ as Savior—are not merely expressive of a religious viewpoint; they are acts of religious worship and instruction. The Court in *United States v. Seeger*, 380 U.S. 163 (1965), recognized that “religious training and ritual” are not protected speech under the First Amendment when they constitute “worship” or “religious observance.” Although *Seeger* was decided after 1970, its reasoning is rooted in earlier precedent, including *United States v. Ballard*, which established that religious conduct may be regulated even when it is motivated by sincere belief.

The Court has consistently held that public schools may not be used as instruments of religious instruction. In *McCollum v. Board of Education*, 333 U.S. 203 (1948), the Court struck down a program allowing religious instruction in public school classrooms during the school day, holding that “the use of public school property for religious instruction is a violation of the Establishment Clause.” Id. at 211. The Court emphasized that “the state may not aid one religion, aid all religions, or prefer one religion over another.” Id. at 212. The same principle applies to after-hours use of school facilities. The school’s policy is not a denial of speech; it is a refusal to allow religious worship on public property, which is a legitimate and necessary safeguard against the establishment of religion.

This Court has also recognized that the government may exclude religious speech from a limited public forum when the forum is not open to all forms of expression. In *Braunfeld v. Brown*, 366 U.S. 599 (1961), the Court upheld a state law requiring businesses to close on Sundays, even though the law burdened the religious practices of Orthodox Jews. The Court held that “the state may enforce a general law even though it incidentally burdens religious conduct.” Id. at 606. Similarly, here, the school’s policy is a general rule that applies to all applicants and is not designed to suppress religious speech. It is neutral in application and serves a compelling interest in maintaining the secular character of public school facilities.

The Court has further held that the government may regulate the use of public property to prevent the appearance of religious endorsement. In *Cox v. New Hampshire*, 312 U.S. 569 (1941), the Court upheld a state law requiring permits for parades, stating that “the state may regulate the time, place, and manner of assemblies in public places.” Id. at 576. The Court emphasized that “the state may not be required to provide a forum for all types of expression.” Id. at 577. The school’s policy is a reasonable regulation of the time, place, and manner of use of its facilities, and it is not a denial of free speech.

Finally, the Court has recognized that the government may exclude religious speech from public forums when doing so is necessary to avoid the appearance of endorsement. In *Braunfeld*, the Court noted that “the state may not be required to accommodate religious practices at the expense of its own legitimate interests.” 366 U.S. at 606.
