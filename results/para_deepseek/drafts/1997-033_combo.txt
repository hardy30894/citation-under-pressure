**ARGUMENT**

The district court’s order retaining Lexecon’s case for trial under 28 U.S.C. § 1404(a) violates the plain command of 28 U.S.C. § 1407(a). Section 1407(a) mandates that after pretrial proceedings conclude, the Judicial Panel on Multidistrict Litigation “shall” remand each transferred action to its originating district. This mandatory language—coupled with the limited scope of a transferee court’s authority—forbids the district court from using § 1404(a) to keep the case for trial.

**I. Section 1407(a) Requires Mandatory Remand, Leaving No Discretion to Retain the Case**

The statute’s use of “shall” is unequivocal. This Court has long held that “shall” in a federal venue statute imposes a duty, not a choice. *Ex parte Collett*, 337 U.S. 55, 60 (1949) (construing “may” in § 1404(a) as permissive but noting that “shall” is mandatory). Section 1407(a) provides that after pretrial proceedings, “each action so transferred shall be remanded by the panel.” No exception exists for a transferee court to reassign the case to itself. The district court’s order thus directly contravenes the statute’s text.

**II. The District Court Lacked Authority to Transfer the Case to Itself Under § 1404(a)**

Section 1404(a) permits a district court to transfer a civil action “to any other district or division where it might have been brought.” A transfer to the same court is not a change of venue—it is a nullity. *In re Josephson*, 218 F.2d 600, 602 (1st Cir. 1954) (holding that § 1404(a) cannot be used to transfer a case to the same district because that would be “a mere change of judge, not a change of venue”). Here, the District of Arizona was the very court where the case was pending. The attempted “transfer” was therefore invalid.

Moreover, even if a self-transfer were permissible in theory, the action could not “have been brought” in the District of Arizona within the meaning of § 1404(a). *Hoffman v. Blaski*, 363 U.S. 335, 343–44 (1960) (holding that “might have been brought” refers to the district where the plaintiff could have originally filed suit, not where venue becomes proper through later events). Lexecon filed suit in the Northern District of Illinois; the case was sent to Arizona only by the Panel’s § 1407 order. That subsequent transfer did not make Arizona a district where the action “might have been brought” originally. *See also United States v. National City Lines*, 337 U.S. 78, 82 (1949) (transfer under § 1404(a) is limited to districts where venue would have been proper at the time of filing). The district court thus lacked the statutory predicate for a § 1404(a) transfer.

**III. The Transferee Court’s Authority Is Limited to Pretrial Proceedings**

Section 1407(a) empowers the transferee court only to conduct “coordinated or consolidated pretrial proceedings.” A motion under § 1404(a) is not a pretrial proceeding; it is a venue determination that governs the location of trial. The term “pretrial proceedings” in § 1407(a) encompasses discovery, motions practice, and other matters that prepare a case for trial, not the ultimate decision of where trial will occur. *See, e.g., Hickman v. Taylor*, 329 U.S. 495, 500–01 (1947) (describing pretrial discovery as part of the “pretrial” stage). By using § 1404(a) to retain the case for trial, the district court exceeded the limited authority Congress granted it.

**IV. The Judicial Panel’s Rule Cannot Override the Statute**

The district court relied on Multidistrict Litigation Rule 14(b), which purports to authorize a transferee court to transfer the action to itself for trial. But an administrative rule cannot supersede a statute. *Manhattan General Equipment Co. v. Commissioner*, 297 U.S. 129, 134 (1936) (agency regulation “must be consistent with the statute under which it is promulgated”). Here, Rule 14(b) directly contradicts the mandatory remand requirement of § 1407(a). *See United States v. Grimaud*, 220 U.S. 506, 518 (1911) (regulations must be “within the scope of the authority conferred”). The rule is therefore void.

**V. The District Court’s Action Subverts the Statutory Scheme**

Congress designed § 1407(a) to balance efficiency with plaintiffs’ forum choice. Pretrial consolidation is temporary; trial must return to the original district. *Van Dusen v. Barrack*, 376 U.S. 612, 616–17 (1964) (recognizing that § 1404(a) is a “change of venue” statute, not a mechanism for permanent reassignment). *Norwood v. Kirkpatrick*, 349 U.S. 29, 32 (1955) (noting that § 1404(a) discretion must be exercised within the “framework of the statute”). The district court’s order frustrates that balance, effectively permitting the Panel to assign cases for trial—a power Congress withheld.

**Conclusion**

The district court lacked authority to invoke § 1404(a) to retain the case for trial. The plain language of § 1407(a) requires remand, and the court’s attempted self-transfer is ultra vires. This Court should reverse the judgment of the Ninth Circuit and direct the district court to remand the case to the Northern District of Illinois.
