**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Montana Supreme Court Correctly Applied the Established Constitutional Test That a River’s Navigability for Title Purposes Must Be Determined as of the Date of Statehood.**

This Court has long held that the "equal footing doctrine" grants a State title to the beds of rivers that were navigable at the time of statehood. *United States v. Utah*, 283 U.S. 64, 75 (1931). The critical inquiry is whether the river was "navigable in fact" when the State entered the Union. *The Daniel Ball*, 10 Wall. 557, 563 (1871). A river is navigable in fact when it is "used, or susceptible of being used, in their ordinary condition, as highways for commerce, over which trade and travel are or may be conducted in the customary modes of trade and travel on water." *Id.* The Montana Supreme Court applied precisely this test, examining historical evidence of river use at the time of Montana's statehood in 1889. App. 51a-52a.

PPL mischaracterizes the legal standard when it argues that a trial court must conduct a "segment-by-segment" analysis for title purposes. This Court has never required such a rigid approach. In *United States v. Oregon*, 295 U.S. 1, 17 (1935), this Court recognized that navigability may be established by evidence of "the use of the river as a highway for commerce," even if certain sections were obstructed by rapids or falls. The key inquiry is whether the river as a whole served as a continuous highway for commerce. *See Utah*, 283 U.S. at 82.

Here, the Montana Supreme Court thoroughly reviewed the historical record—including accounts of steamboat traffic, fur trading, and military transport on the Missouri, Madison, and Clark Fork Rivers—and concluded that each river was navigable as a "continuous highway for commerce" at statehood. App. 52a-58a. This fact-intensive determination is entitled to deference. *Utah*, 283 U.S. at 77.

**II. The Montana Supreme Court Properly Rejected PPL’s Preemption and Federal Navigational Servitude Arguments.**

PPL argues that the Federal Power Act and the federal navigational servitude preempt Montana’s Hydroelectric Resources Act. This argument fails because this Court has consistently recognized that the FPA "establishes a dual system of control" between state and federal authorities, preserving state property rights. *First Iowa Hydro-Electric Cooperative v. Federal Power Commission*, 328 U.S. 152, 167 (1946). Section 9(b) of the FPA requires license applicants to demonstrate compliance with state law regarding "beds and banks and to the appropriation, diversion, and use of water for power purposes." 16 U.S.C. § 802(b). This provision explicitly incorporates state property law into the federal licensing scheme. *See California v. United States*, 438 U.S. 645, 674 (1978).

Moreover, the federal navigational servitude does not extinguish state ownership of riverbeds. In *Montana v. United States*, 450 U.S. 544, 551 (1981), this Court held that the servitude applies only to "navigable waters," not to state-owned land. The State is not asserting a right to obstruct navigation; it is merely seeking compensation for the use of its property. The FPA expressly contemplates that licensees will compensate property owners for the use of their land. *Federal Power Commission v. Niagara Mohawk Power Corp.*, 347 U.S. 239, 251 (1954).

**III. The Montana Supreme Court Correctly Held That the State Owns the Riverbeds at Issue.**

Under the equal footing doctrine, Montana acquired title to the beds of navigable rivers upon statehood in 1889. *Shively v. Bowlby*, 152 U.S. 1, 26-27 (1894). The historical evidence demonstrates that the Missouri, Madison, and Clark Fork Rivers were used for commercial navigation at that time. App. 58a-63a. PPL’s contrary argument relies on a mistaken view that navigability must be assessed in each "discrete segment" of the river. Yet this Court has never endorsed such a fragmented approach. In *Utah*, 283 U.S. at 82, the Court examined the river as a whole, considering its "susceptibility" for commercial use in the context of "the customary modes of trade and travel" at the time of statehood. *See also Oregon*, 295 U.S. at 17.

The Montana Supreme Court’s conclusion that the rivers were navigable in fact at statehood is amply supported by evidence of steamboat traffic, railroad supply routes, and military expeditions. App. 58a-63a. This Court should affirm.
