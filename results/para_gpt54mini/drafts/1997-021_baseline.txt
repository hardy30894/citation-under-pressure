### Argument

#### I. The Board’s “good-faith reasonable doubt” polling standard is not rationally tied to the Act.

The National Labor Relations Act does not prescribe a special test for employer polling. The Board created the polling doctrine as an administrative accommodation to the Act’s protection of majority rule and stable bargaining relationships. See *Texas Petrochemicals Corp.*, 296 N.L.R.B. 1057, 1061-63 (1989); *Allentown Mack Sales & Serv., Inc. v. NLRB*, 522 U.S. 359, 371-74 (1998). Because the Act itself is silent, the Board’s rule must at least be rational and consistent with the statute. *Beth Israel Hosp. v. NLRB*, 437 U.S. 483, 500-01 (1978); *NLRB v. Curtin Matheson Scientific, Inc.*, 494 U.S. 775, 786 (1990).

It is not. The Board’s polling standard allows an employer to poll only if it already has objective evidence sufficient to raise a reasonable doubt about the union’s majority status—the same showing the Board requires for withdrawal of recognition. *Allentown Mack*, 522 U.S. at 370-74. But the Board simultaneously says polling is permissible only to “test” that very status. *Texas Petrochemicals*, 296 N.L.R.B. at 1061-63. That makes polling nearly superfluous. As this Court recognized, an employer who already has enough evidence to justify withdrawal of recognition has little need for an unsupervised poll. *Allentown Mack*, 522 U.S. at 370-74. The Board’s answer—that polling can still help an employer act “with confidence and certainty”—cannot justify a rule that makes polling available only when the employer already has enough evidence to act without polling. Id. at 373.

The courts of appeals that rejected the Board’s standard understood the problem correctly. The Fifth Circuit held that, if polling is to be a “useful and legitimate tool,” the employer need only have “substantial, objective evidence of a loss of union support,” even if insufficient to withdraw recognition. *A.W. Thompson, Inc. v. NLRB*, 651 F.2d 1141, 1145 (5th Cir. 1981). The Ninth Circuit adopted the same rule in *Mingtree Restaurant, Inc. v. NLRB*, 736 F.2d 1295, 1299 (9th Cir. 1984), and the Sixth Circuit did likewise in *Thomas Industries, Inc. v. NLRB*, 687 F.2d 863, 867-68 (6th Cir. 1982). Those decisions reflect the Act’s basic policy of allowing employers some practical means to test majority support without forcing either bargaining with a minority union or withdrawal based on uncertainty. *Allentown Mack*, 522 U.S. at 369-74.

The Board’s contrary position also conflicts with the policy favoring Board-supervised elections as the preferred method for resolving representation questions. *Curtin Matheson*, 494 U.S. at 787; *Fall River Dyeing & Finishing Corp. v. NLRB*, 482 U.S. 27, 41 n.8 (1987). If the Board truly prefers elections, it makes little sense to require an employer to meet the same evidentiary threshold for an informal poll as for the much more reliable mechanisms of withdrawal or an RM election. *Allentown Mack*, 522 U.S. at 372-74. The better rule is the one adopted by the courts of appeals: polling should be allowed where the employer has substantial, objective evidence of a loss of support and complies with the *Struksnes* safeguards. *Struksnes Constr. Co.*, 165 N.L.R.B. 1062 (1967); *Thomas Industries*, 687 F.2d at 867-68; *Mingtree Restaurant*, 736 F.2d at 1299.

#### II. The Board’s finding that Allentown Mack lacked a reasonable doubt is unsupported by substantial evidence.

Even under the Board’s own standard, its factual finding cannot stand. The substantial-evidence standard requires a reasoned account of the record as a whole, not selective reliance on equivocal statements while discounting contrary evidence without adequate explanation. *Universal Camera Corp. v. NLRB*, 340 U.S. 474, 488 (1951); *Allentown Mack*, 522 U.S. at 377-84.

Here, the evidence showed significant employee dissatisfaction with the union. Three employees made direct antiunion statements; two more expressed strong skepticism or predicted the union would lose; and the poll, conducted under strict safeguards, produced a 19-to-13 antiunion result. *Allentown Mack*, 522 U.S. at 362-64, 377-84. The Board discounted some statements as too equivocal, too remote, or secondhand. But its treatment was inconsistent and implausibly narrow. For example, statements by employees during interviews were dismissed because they were made in a nonunion context, yet the Board simultaneously treated other interview-era statements as probative when they favored the union. *Allentown Mack*, 522 U.S. at 378-81. Likewise, the Board refused to credit the testimony of a steward predicting the union would lose, even though such a statement from a union official would ordinarily be powerful evidence of declining support. Id. at 382-84.

Most importantly, the Board counted only seven employees as antiunion, but the record showed more substantial evidence of disaffection than the Board acknowledged. Id. at 383-84. When an agency’s conclusion rests on a cramped and internally inconsistent reading of the record, it cannot survive substantial-evidence review. *Universal Camera*, 340 U.S. at 488; *Allentown Mack*, 522 U.S. at 377-84.

Because the Board’s legal standard is irrational and its factual finding is unsupported, the order should be reversed.
