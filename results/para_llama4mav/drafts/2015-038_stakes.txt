The Alaska National Interest Lands Conservation Act of 1980 (ANILCA) does not prohibit the National Park Service (NPS) from exercising regulatory control over state, Native, corporate, and private Alaska land physically located within the boundaries of the National Park System. The plain text of ANILCA § 103(c) supports the NPS's authority to regulate activities within Conservation System Units (CSUs), including non-federal lands.

The Ninth Circuit correctly held that Sturgeon's interpretation of § 103(c) is foreclosed by the plain text of the statute. The statute provides that "only those public lands within the boundaries of ... conservation system units ... shall be deemed to be included as a portion of such unit." 16 U.S.C. § 3103(c). This language does not limit the NPS's regulatory authority to only federal lands within CSUs. Instead, it ensures that non-federal lands within CSUs are considered part of the unit for certain purposes.

The NPS's regulatory authority is derived from its power to manage and protect the resources within CSUs. As the Supreme Court has recognized, the federal government retains significant authority to regulate activities within national parks, even on non-federal lands. See, e.g., United States v. Riverside Bayview Homes, Inc., 474 U.S. 121, 133-34 (1985) (recognizing the federal government's authority to regulate wetlands under the Clean Water Act).

In Katie John III, this Court held that ANILCA § 103(c) does not preclude the NPS from regulating activities on non-federal lands within CSUs. 720 F.3d 1214, 1226-27 (9th Cir. 2013). The Court's decision was based on the plain text of the statute and the legislative history, which indicates that Congress intended to preserve the NPS's regulatory authority over non-federal lands within CSUs. Id. at 1227.

Sturgeon's argument that the NPS lacks authority to regulate his use of a hovercraft on state-owned waters within the Yukon-Charley Rivers National Preserve is unpersuasive. The NPS's hovercraft ban is a regulation that applies to all lands and waters within CSUs, regardless of ownership. 36 C.F.R. § 2.17(e). As the Ninth Circuit held, this regulation is not limited to federal lands within CSUs, and the NPS has authority to enforce it on non-federal lands, including state-owned waters.

The State of Alaska's claim that the NPS's regulations interfere with its sovereign interests is also without merit. As the Ninth Circuit held, Alaska lacks standing to bring this challenge because it has not demonstrated a concrete and particularized injury. The State's alleged injuries are speculative and do not meet the requirements for standing under Article III. See Lujan v. Defenders of Wildlife, 504 U.S. 555, 560-61 (1992).

In conclusion, the NPS has authority under ANILCA to regulate activities within CSUs, including non-federal lands. The plain text of § 103(c) and the legislative history support this interpretation. The Ninth Circuit's decision should be affirmed, and Sturgeon's claim should be denied.
