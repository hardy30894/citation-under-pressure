**ARGUMENT**

The decision below perpetuates an unwarranted distinction between procedural and jurisdictional dismissals that finds no support in the statutory scheme or in this Court’s precedents. When an employee alleges both an adverse action appealable to the Merit Systems Protection Board and discrimination, the resulting “mixed case” must be reviewed in district court—regardless of the ground on which the Board dismisses the appeal. The D.C. Circuit’s contrary conclusion, premised on its decision in *Powell v. Department of Defense*, cannot survive this Court’s settled teaching that jurisdiction depends on the allegations in the initial pleading, not on the agency’s ultimate disposition.

**I. A Mixed Case Is Defined by the Employee’s Allegations, Not by the Board’s Jurisdictional Ruling**

It is a cornerstone of federal jurisdiction that “the party who brings a suit is master to decide what law he will rely upon,” and that jurisdiction is determined by the “well-pleaded complaint.” *The Fair v. Kohler Die & Specialty Co.*, 228 U.S. 22, 25 (1913). This principle applies with equal force to administrative appeals. Anthony Perry filed a claim with the Board alleging that he was subjected to an adverse employment action—a thirty-day suspension and forced retirement—and that the action was motivated by discrimination based on his race, age, and disability. By any measure, his claim was a “mixed case” because it involved both an action “appealable to the Board” and a colorable allegation of discrimination. The Board’s later conclusion that the underlying action was voluntary did not retroactively alter the character of the case as originally pleaded. *Cf. St. Paul Mercury Indemnity Co. v. Red Cab Co.*, 303 U.S. 283, 288-89 (1938) (jurisdictional amount determined by complaint, not eventual recovery).

The D.C. Circuit erred by allowing the Board’s substantive ruling to reclassify Perry’s case. The phrase “action which [the employee] may appeal to the Board” in 5 U.S.C. § 7702(a)(1)(A) refers to the type of action alleged, not to whether that action ultimately falls within the Board’s jurisdiction. This Court has long instructed that statutes should be interpreted according to their plain meaning, and that meaning is determined by the “import of the language used.” *United States v. American Trucking Ass’ns*, 310 U.S. 534, 542-43 (1940). The statutory text speaks of the action the employee “may appeal,” not the action the Board “has jurisdiction to review.” A suspension and a retirement are actions that an employee is permitted to appeal to the Board; the Board’s decision that the appeal is jurisdictionally barred does not erase that essential feature.

**II. The Distinction Between Procedural and Jurisdictional Dismissals Has No Statutory Foundation**

No provision of the Civil Service Reform Act distinguishes between procedural and jurisdictional dismissals for purposes of judicial review. The D.C. Circuit’s reliance on such a distinction is an unwarranted judicial gloss. This Court has consistently rejected efforts to read exceptions into statutes that do not contain them. *See, e.g., Crowell v. Benson*, 285 U.S. 22, 60-61 (1932) (rejecting implied limitations on judicial review of agency action). The Act provides that in a mixed case, the employee “may file a civil action” in district court. 5 U.S.C. § 7703(b)(2). There is no carve-out for cases in which the Board first decides that it lacks jurisdiction.

The D.C. Circuit attempted to justify its distinction by noting that jurisdictionally barred appeals were never “appealable to the MSPB.” That analysis conflates the Board’s authority to hear an appeal with the employee’s right to file one. An employee “may appeal” any action listed in § 7512, regardless of whether the Board later determines that the action was voluntary. The Board’s jurisdiction over involuntary actions is a substantive condition, not a limitation on the employee’s right to present the appeal. When the Board decides that condition is unmet, it resolves a contested issue that lies at the heart of the case—often overlapping with the merits of the discrimination claim. This Court has recognized that when an agency’s jurisdiction is “intertwined with the merits,” the reviewing court must independently examine the underlying facts. *Crowell*, 285 U.S. at 60-61. The district court, not the Federal Circuit, is the proper forum for that examination.

**III. The District Court Is the Appropriate Forum for Review of Mixed-Case Jurisdictional Rulings**

This Court has long held that an agency’s determination of its own jurisdiction is not binding on the reviewing court. *Estep v. United States*, 327 U.S. 114, 120-21 (1946). In *Estep*, the Court explained that courts must have the authority to review “whether the [agency] has acted within its jurisdiction,” and that a contrary rule would “deprive the [employee] of any real opportunity to challenge the agency’s action.” *Id.* at 121. The same principle applies here. Perry’s claim that his retirement was coerced—and thus involuntary—is a question that the Board resolved against him. That resolution is not entitled to deference, and the district court must be free to decide it anew.

Moreover, the district court is the traditional forum for resolving discrimination claims. This Court has emphasized that the “right to be free from invidious discrimination” is best adjudicated in a forum “which is fully competent to grant appropriate remedies.” *See, e.g., Bailey v. Patterson*, 369 U.S. 31, 32-33 (1962) (per curiam). The Federal Circuit, by contrast, is a specialized court with limited jurisdiction. Routing Perry’s discrimination claim to a court that does not routinely hear such cases would frustrate the statutory purpose of providing a single, unified review in district court. The D.C. Circuit’s contrary decision would force employees to litigate jurisdictional issues in one court and discrimination claims in another, a result that “no statute commands.” *See United States v. Wittek*, 337 U.S. 346, 350 (1949) (courts should avoid interpretations that create procedural chaos).

**IV. The D.C. Circuit’s Reliance on Its Own Precedent Cannot Override This Court’s Guidance**

The D.C. Circuit acknowledged that its decision in *Powell* “is materially indistinguishable from this case” but concluded that it remained bound by that precedent because this Court’s intervening decision in *Kloeckner* did not explicitly overrule it. That reasoning elevates circuit precedent over the statutory command. This Court has repeatedly instructed courts of appeals to interpret statutes “according to their plain meaning, not according to the gloss that lower courts have placed upon them.” *American Trucking Ass’ns*, 310 U.S. at 542. When a statute is clear, a court may not “refuse to give it effect based on its own prior decisions.” *Erie R.R. Co. v. Tompkins*, 304 U.S. 64, 78 (1938). The plain language of § 7702(a)(1) makes Perry’s case a mixed case, and the plain language of § 7703(b)(2) places review in district court.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the D.C. Circuit should be reversed, and the case remanded with instructions to transfer to the appropriate federal district court.
