**ARGUMENT**

**I. The District Court Erred in Holding That the Severance Releases Bar Appellants’ WARN Act Claims Because the Releases Are Not Knowing and Voluntary.**

The WARN Act, 29 U.S.C. §§ 2101–2109, requires employers to provide 60 days’ advance notice before ordering a “plant closing” or “mass layoff.” The Act’s remedial purpose—to afford workers time to adjust to job loss—would be undermined if employers could secure waivers of WARN rights through coercive, hurried, and uninformed “agreements.” While the WARN Act does not expressly prohibit waivers, any release of federal statutory rights must be “knowing and voluntary.” *Pierce v. Atchison, Topeka & Santa Fe Ry. Co.*, 65 F.3d 562, 571 (7th Cir. 1995) (applying this standard to releases of ADEA claims); *see also* *EEOC v. Waffle House, Inc.*, 534 U.S. 279, 293–94 (2002) (waivers of federal rights are disfavored and must be clear and deliberate). The releases here fail that test.

**A. The Circumstances of the Releases Were Inherently Coercive.**

DHL and the Union presented the severance packages with a take-it-or-leave-it ultimatum. Drivers opting for the ten-week plan had only two days—from December 9 to December 11, 2008—to decide and simultaneously complete a job-rebid form. This compressed timeframe, coupled with the threat of losing all severance, deprived workers of a meaningful opportunity to consider their options or consult counsel—despite the release’s boilerplate advice to do so. “A release is not ‘knowing and voluntary’ if the employee lacked a meaningful choice.” *O’Shea v. Commercial Credit Corp.*, 930 F.2d 358, 362 (4th Cir. 1991) (cited favorably by the Seventh Circuit in *Pierce*). Moreover, the release was a condition of receiving any severance; workers faced the choice of signing away their WARN rights or receiving nothing—even though they were already owed WARN damages for DHL’s failure to give timely notice. See *United Paperworkers Int’l Union v. Alden Corrugated Container Corp.*, 901 F. Supp. 426, 432 (D. Mass. 1995) (waiver of WARN claims invalid when obtained under economic pressure). The mere presence of a seven-day revocation period cannot cure the coercion inherent in an irrevocable election made in two days.

**B. The Release Did Not Provide Separate, Adequate Consideration for the Waiver of WARN Rights.**

The release recites that the severance payments are “beyond anything to which I am already or otherwise entitled.” But if DHL violated the WARN Act, appellants were already entitled to back pay and benefits for up to 60 days. By giving severance that may be less than the full WARN remedy, DHL cannot claim that the consideration was “additional.” A release of federal statutory rights must be supported by independent consideration—not merely the payment of what is already owed. *See* *McDonald v. City of West Branch*, 466 U.S. 284, 291 (1984) (waiver of § 1983 claim requires separate consideration). Because the severance packages may have been a partial substitute for WARN damages, they fail this requirement.

**C. The Release Purports to Waive Claims That Had Not Yet Accrued, Rendering It Void as Against Public Policy.**

The release waives claims “arising out of my employment or the termination of my employment,” including WARN Act claims. At the time of signing (December 2008), the actual terminations were not scheduled until January 30, 2009, and some workers had not yet been laid off. A waiver of future, unknown WARN claims violates the Act’s command that notice be given before the layoff—not after the employee has already been compelled to resign. *Cf.* *Barrentine v. Arkansas-Best Freight Sys., Inc.*, 450 U.S. 728, 740 (1981) (statutory rights cannot be waived in advance). The WARN Act’s protections are not subject to unilateral erosion by contract.

**II. DHL Violated the WARN Act as a Matter of Law.**

DHL’s November 10 announcement that domestic shipping would cease on January 30, combined with the massive reduction in force at five Chicagoland facilities, constituted a “plant closing” or “mass layoff” under 29 U.S.C. § 2101(a)(2)–(3). DHL failed to give 60 days’ notice before the first employment losses, which began in August 2008. *See* *Local 1239, Int’l Bhd. of Elec. Workers v. N.C. Elec. Membership Corp.*, 104 F. App’x 847, 851–52 (4th Cir. 2004) (notice must precede any employment loss). The severance agreements were not a lawful substitute for WARN notice. Accordingly, the waivers cannot bar appellants’ claims, and the district court’s order must be reversed.
