ARGUMENT

I. The OCSLA Workers’ Compensation Provision Requires a Situs-of-Injury Test

The Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act (“OCSLA”) extends workers’ compensation coverage under the Longshore and Harbor Workers’ Compensation Act (“LHWCA”) to injuries occurring “as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf for the purpose of exploring for, developing, removing, or transporting by pipeline the natural resources . . . of the outer Continental Shelf.” 43 U.S.C. § 1333(b). A plain reading of this provision indicates that the injury must be connected to operations on the outer Continental Shelf. The Supreme Court's commentary on OCSLA's "situs requirement" in Offshore Logistics, Inc. v. Tallentire, is instructive, see 477 U.S. 207, 219 (1986).

The Fifth Circuit has adopted a situs-of-injury test for OCSLA claims, requiring that the injury occur on an outer continental shelf platform or on the waters above the outer continental shelf, Mills v. Dir., Office of Workers’ Comp. Programs, 177 F.2d 356 (5th Cir. 1947) (en banc). This interpretation is consistent with the LHWCA, which requires that the injury occur on “navigable waters of the United States,” 33 U.S.C. § 903(a); see Swanson v. Marra Bros., 328 U.S. 1 (1946); Nogueira v. New York, N.H. & H.R. Co., 281 U.S. 128 (1930); State Industrial Comm’n v. Nordenholt Corp., 259 U.S. 263 (1922). The LHWCA's situs requirement has been consistently applied by the courts, see London Guarantee & Accident Co. v. Indus. Accident Comm’n, 279 U.S. 109 (1929); T. Smith & Son, Inc. v. Taylor, 276 U.S. 179 (1928).

The OCSLA's statutory language and legislative history support a situs-of-injury test. The statute was enacted to provide a comprehensive framework for the development of the outer Continental Shelf, and the workers' compensation provision is an integral part of this framework. Requiring a situs-of-injury test ensures that the OCSLA's workers' compensation provision is applied consistently with the statute's overall purpose, see Parker v. Motor Boat Sales, 314 U.S. 244 (1941); Davis v. Dep’t of Labor & Indus., 317 U.S. 249 (1942).

II. La Conchita is Not a Maritime Situs

The LHWCA requires that the injury occur on a maritime situs, defined as “navigable waters of the United States, including any adjoining pier, wharf, dry dock, terminal, building way, marine railway, or other adjoining area customarily used by an employer in loading, unloading, repairing, or building a vessel,” 33 U.S.C. § 903(a). La Conchita does not meet this definition, as it is not adjacent to navigable waters and is not used for maritime purposes, see Minnie v. Port Huron Terminal Co., 295 U.S. 647 (1935); T. Smith & Son, Inc. v. Taylor, 276 U.S. 179 (1928). The facility's proximity to the coastline is insufficient to establish it as a maritime situs, given its separation from the water by a highway and railroad tracks.

The Ninth Circuit applies a "functional relationship" test to determine whether a facility is a maritime situs, considering factors such as the site's suitability for maritime uses, proximity to the waterway, and use as an integral part of longshoring operations, Brady-Hamilton Stevedore Co. v. Herron, 568 F.2d 137 (9th Cir. 1978). Under this test, La Conchita is not a maritime situs, as it is not used for maritime purposes and is not adjacent to navigable waters. The primary purpose of La Conchita is to process crude oil slurry, a non-maritime activity, Herb’s Welding, Inc. v. Gray, 766 F.2d 898 (5th Cir. 1985) (citing Herb’s Welding, Inc. v. Gray, 470 U.S. 414 (1985)).

CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the Court should affirm the BRB's denial of benefits under the OCSLA and LHWCA.
