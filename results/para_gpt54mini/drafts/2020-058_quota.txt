Petitioner respectfully submits that the Third Circuit erred. The proper reading of 8 U.S.C. § 1254a(f)(4) is that a grant of Temporary Protected Status places a noncitizen “in, and maintaining, lawful status as a nonimmigrant” for purposes of adjustment of status under § 1255; it does not create a separate “admission” event. The statute’s text, structure, and purpose confirm that Congress used different words to mean different things, and here it expressly linked TPS to lawful status while withholding an express statement that TPS counts as admission. See 8 U.S.C. §§ 1101(a)(13)(A), 1254a(f)(4), 1255(a).

First, § 1254a(f)(4) is a specific statutory instruction that must do work. Congress provided that a TPS recipient “shall be considered as being in, and maintaining, lawful status as a nonimmigrant” for purposes of adjustment. 8 U.S.C. § 1254a(f)(4). This Court has repeatedly recognized that immigration statutes distinguish “admission” from “status.” In Hanif v. Attorney General, 694 F.3d 479, 485 (3d Cir. 2012), the court explained that “the date of gaining a new status is not the same as the date of the physical event of entering the country.” Likewise, Taveras v. Attorney General, 731 F.3d 281, 290 (3d Cir. 2013), held that “admission” involves physical entry into the country. Those cases confirm that a later conferral of lawful status can satisfy a statutory eligibility requirement without rewriting the separate admission prerequisite in § 1255(a).

Second, the INA’s definition of “admission” does not foreclose Petitioner’s reading. Section 1101(a)(13)(A) defines admission as “the lawful entry of the alien into the United States after inspection and authorization by an immigration officer.” But a statutory deeming provision can supply the legal effect Congress intended. The Board has recognized that “An adjustment of status generally constitutes an admission.” In re Espinosa-Guillot, 25 I. & N. Dec. 653, 654 (BIA 2011). And the agency has likewise acknowledged that status-conferral provisions can be treated as admission equivalents in context. See In re H-G-G-, 27 I. & N. Dec. 617, 635 (A.A.O. 2019). The question, therefore, is not whether TPS is literally an airport inspection, but whether Congress meant TPS to operate as the legal equivalent of an admission for § 1255 purposes. Section 1254a(f)(4) answers yes by deeming TPS recipients to be in lawful nonimmigrant status for adjustment.

Third, the broader statutory structure supports Petitioner. Congress knows how to exclude or include admission expressly. In I.N.S. v. Cardoza-Fonseca, 480 U.S. 421, 432 (1987), the Court relied on the presumption that Congress acts intentionally when it includes language in one section but omits it in another. Here, Congress did not merely confer lawful status; it tied that status to adjustment of status under § 1255. It also enacted separate adjustment provisions that expressly reference admission and lawful status together. See 8 U.S.C. §§ 1255(k)(1), (m)(1). Under Hibbs v. Winn, 542 U.S. 88, 101 (2004), statutes should be construed so no part is rendered “inoperative or superfluous.” Treating § 1254a(f)(4) as irrelevant to § 1255(a) would deprive the TPS deeming clause of meaningful effect.

Fourth, the government’s position is incompatible with the remedial purpose of TPS. TPS was enacted to protect nationals of designated countries from the consequences of unsafe return. See 8 U.S.C. § 1254a(b)(1). This Court has recognized that TPS is “not a program of entry for an alien.” De Leon-Ochoa v. Attorney General, 622 F.3d 341, 353–54 (3d Cir. 2010). But that observation does not answer whether Congress later provided TPS recipients a lawful-status bridge into adjustment. It did. As the Eleventh Circuit explained, “lawful status” and “admission” are “fundamentally distinct concepts,” but Congress may assign legal consequences to status that satisfy later immigration requirements. Gomez v. Lynch, 831 F.3d 652, 658 (5th Cir. 2016); see also Ramirez v. Brown, 852 F.3d 954, 960 (9th Cir. 2017). Denying TPS recipients any adjustment pathway despite the express status deeming in § 1254a(f)(4) would frustrate the statute’s humanitarian design.

Finally, the government’s reading cannot be reconciled with the principle that immigration provisions should be read harmoniously. Pereira v. Sessions, 138 S. Ct. 2105, 2114 (2018), instructs that courts must give effect to the words Congress chose. Here, Congress chose to say that TPS recipients are “considered” to be in lawful nonimmigrant status for adjustment purposes. 8 U.S.C. § 1254a(f)(4). That language is best understood as satisfying the threshold eligibility component of § 1255, even for those who initially entered without inspection.

For these reasons, the Court should hold that TPS constitutes an admission for purposes of adjustment of status under § 1255, or at minimum that § 1254a(f)(4) renders TPS recipients eligible to adjust notwithstanding their manner of entry.
