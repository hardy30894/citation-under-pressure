# ARGUMENT

## I. MILFORD'S POLICY CONSTITUTES REASONABLE, VIEWPOINT-NEUTRAL REGULATION OF A LIMITED PUBLIC FORUM

Milford Central School has not opened its facilities as a traditional public forum but has instead created a limited public forum for "social, civic and recreational meetings and entertainment events" pertaining to "the welfare of the community." Because Milford is under no obligation to allow persons to engage in every type of speech, it may "be justified in reserving its forum for certain groups or for the discussion of certain topics." *Rosenberger v. Rector & Visitors of Univ. of Va.*, 515 U.S. 819, 829 (1995). The relevant constitutional inquiry is therefore not whether Milford discriminated against the Club's religious identity, but whether its exclusion of "religious purposes"—including "religious instruction and prayer"—constitutes reasonable subject-matter regulation rather than viewpoint discrimination.

This Court has long recognized that the line between subject and viewpoint discrimination "is not a precise one," *Rosenberger*, 515 U.S. at 831, but the distinction remains essential to preserving government's ability to define forums for their intended purposes. In *Cornelius v. NAACP Legal Defense & Educational Fund, Inc.*, 473 U.S. 788 (1985), this Court upheld the exclusion of legal defense and political advocacy organizations from a limited forum created for charitable solicitation, holding that "the First Amendment does not forbid a viewpoint-neutral exclusion of speakers who would disrupt a nonpublic forum." *Id.* at 811. Milford's policy operates identically: it excludes an entire category of activity—religious worship and instruction—regardless of which religion, denomination, or theological perspective is being expressed.

The Second Circuit correctly recognized this distinction below, holding that the Club's proposed activities were "quintessentially religious" and fell "outside the bounds of pure 'moral and character development.'" *Good News Club v. Milford Cent. Sch.*, 202 F.3d 502, 510 (2d Cir. 2000). This is not a case where Milford permitted secular discussions of morality while excluding religious perspectives on that same subject. Rather, Milford excluded an entire category of conduct—proselytizing religious worship, complete with altar calls inviting children to "receive Jesus as their personal Savior"—that is categorically different from the moral-development programming offered by the Boy Scouts, Girl Scouts, and 4-H Club.

## II. MILFORD'S EXCLUSION DOES NOT DISCRIMINATE AGAINST RELIGIOUS VIEWPOINT

Petitioner's reliance on *Lamb's Chapel v. Center Moriches Union Free School District*, 508 U.S. 384 (1993), is misplaced. There, the school district permitted use of facilities for the discussion of family values and childrearing but denied a church's request to show a film addressing those same topics from a religious perspective. This Court held that exclusion "discriminate[d] on the basis of viewpoint" because the district had opened its forum to the subject matter of family values, then excluded only the religious viewpoint on that subject. *Id.* at 393.

Milford's policy differs fundamentally. Milford does not permit discussion of "moral development" while singling out religious moral perspectives for exclusion. Instead, Milford excludes religious *worship* and *instruction* as a category, just as it would exclude a Democratic Party club's political advocacy meetings or an AA-style substance-counseling group's therapeutic sessions, regardless of the theological or ideological content of any particular presentation. The Club's own submissions confirm that its activities include prayer, evangelization, and scriptural instruction directed at securing children's conversion—activities that constitute religious exercise itself, not merely a viewpoint on some other permitted subject.

This Court's decision in *Widmar v. Vincent*, 454 U.S. 263 (1981), reinforces rather than undermines Milford's position. *Widmar* involved a university that had created a forum broadly open to *all* student groups for meetings, then attempted to exclude only religious groups from that broadly available forum. *Id.* at 267–68. Milford's forum is narrower by design, and its exclusion of religious worship applies to all groups equally—no group, religious or secular, may use Milford's facilities to conduct worship services or comparable devotional exercises.

## III. EVEN IF THE COURT FINDS A FREE SPEECH VIOLATION, MILFORD'S EXCLUSION WAS JUSTIFIED BY LEGITIMATE ESTABLISHMENT CLAUSE CONCERNS

Should this Court determine that Milford's policy effected viewpoint discrimination, Milford's exclusion nonetheless survives scrutiny because permitting the Club's activities would risk violating the Establishment Clause. This Court has consistently held that avoiding Establishment Clause violations constitutes a compelling governmental interest justifying speech restrictions that might otherwise raise Free Speech Clause concerns. *See Lamb's Chapel*, 508 U.S. at 394 (leaving open whether Establishment Clause concerns could justify exclusion in some circumstances).

The Club's meetings, conducted immediately after school hours on school property, involving elementary-age children as young as six, create precisely the risk of perceived school endorsement of religion that concerned this Court in *Lee v. Weisman*, 505 U.S. 577 (1992). There, this Court emphasized the "heightened concerns with protecting freedom of conscience from subtle coercive pressure in the elementary and secondary public schools," recognizing that "adolescents are often susceptible to pressure from their peers towards conformity." *Id.* at 592. Young children attending a program advertised as a "fun time" immediately following the school day, held in school classrooms under the auspices of school facilities, may reasonably perceive endorsement of the religious message being conveyed—particularly where, as here, that message includes direct entreaties to accept Jesus Christ as personal savior and to pray for salvation.

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should affirm the judgment of the Court of Appeals for the Second Circuit.
