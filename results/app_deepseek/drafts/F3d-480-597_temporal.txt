## ARGUMENT

### I. The Government Failed to Prove That Ms. Nieves-Castaño Knew the Weapon Was a Machine Gun.

The conviction under 18 U.S.C. § 922(o) cannot stand because the prosecution presented no evidence that the defendant knew the AK-47 rifle was a machine gun—i.e., capable of automatically firing more than one shot with a single trigger pull. The common-law presumption that criminal liability requires a “guilty mind” applies with full force to this statute. *Morissette v. United States*, 342 U.S. 246, 250 (1952). That presumption is “no provincial or transient notion”; it is a “universal and persistent” principle of criminal law. *Id.* at 250. Where, as here, the proscribed conduct—possessing a rifle—is not “inherently wrongful,” the government must prove the defendant knew the facts that made that conduct illegal. *Lambert v. California*, 355 U.S. 225, 228 (1957); *see also Boyce Motor Lines v. United States*, 342 U.S. 337, 341 (1952) (requiring “knowledge” of regulation’s specific terms for liability).

Possession of a rifle is lawful in most circumstances. Only when that rifle is a “machine gun” does it become contraband under § 922(o). The statute criminalizes not mere possession of a firearm, but possession of a *particular type* of firearm with a specific mechanical characteristic. Because the defendant’s conduct was facially innocent, the government must prove she knew the weapon possessed that characteristic. *See United States v. Hession*, 244 F.2d 763, 766 (2d Cir. 1925) (possession of morphine required knowledge that substance was a narcotic); *United States v. On Lee*, 193 F.2d 306, 309 (2d Cir. 1951) (possession of silencer required knowledge it was a silencer).

The evidence here is fatally insufficient. The defendant told police she once opened the golf bag and saw a rifle, and that she knew it was an “AK-47.” But an AK-47 is a design commonly manufactured as a semi-automatic rifle—a weapon that fires only one shot per trigger pull. Nothing in the record shows that the defendant knew this particular AK-47 had been modified or manufactured to fire automatically, or that she understood the difference between semi-automatic and fully automatic fire. The government’s case established only that she knew she possessed a rifle; it did not establish that she knew she possessed a machine gun. Under *Morissette*, that is not enough.

The district court’s instruction compounded the error. The court charged the jury that it must find “knowing possession” of the firearm, but it did not expressly require the jury to find that the defendant knew the weapon was a machine gun. Where a statute criminalizes what would otherwise be innocent conduct, the jury must be instructed on the specific fact that makes the conduct illegal. *See, e.g., United States v. Klock*, 151 F.2d 296, 298 (2d Cir. 1945) (instruction on knowledge of counterfeit character required). The absence of such an instruction here allowed the jury to convict based solely on possession of a rifle, which is not a crime.

### II. The School-Zone Statute Is Unconstitutionally Void for Vagueness.

18 U.S.C. § 922(q)(2)(A) prohibits possessing a firearm within 1,000 feet of a school, but it provides no method for measuring that distance. The statute fails to specify whether the measurement is by straight-line “as the crow flies,” by the shortest walking path, or by some other standard. This ambiguity violates the Fifth Amendment’s Due Process Clause because it does not give “fair notice” of the prohibited conduct. *Connally v. General Construction Co.*, 269 U.S. 385, 391 (1926); *Lanzetta v. New Jersey*, 306 U.S. 451, 453 (1939). A law that leaves such a critical measurement to arbitrary enforcement is void for vagueness.

### Conclusion

For these reasons, this Court should reverse the convictions.
