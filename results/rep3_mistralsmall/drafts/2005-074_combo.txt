**ARGUMENT**

**I. The reinstatement statute does not apply to aliens who re-entered the United States before its effective date.**

The reinstatement statute, 8 U.S.C. § 1231(a)(5), permits the Attorney General to reinstate a prior deportation order against an alien who "has reentered the United States illegally after having been removed." The statute does not expressly address whether it applies to re-entries that occurred before its enactment. However, this Court has long held that statutes affecting substantive rights are presumed not to apply retroactively unless Congress clearly expresses such an intent. *United States v. Magness, 356 U.S. 22, 25 (1958)* ("[A] statute will not be construed to have retroactive effect unless such construction is made absolutely necessary by clear language or by necessary implication."); *Shaw v. United States, 309 U.S. 495, 501 (1940)* ("[A] statute ought not to be construed to operate retroactively unless the words used are so clear, strong, and imperative that no other meaning can be annexed to them.").

Here, the reinstatement statute imposes a severe penalty—denial of all forms of relief under the INA—on aliens who re-entered illegally before its enactment. This penalty attaches to conduct that occurred entirely in the past. As this Court has recognized, such penalties are not to be applied retroactively absent clear congressional intent. *See Magness, 356 U.S. at 25*. The statute contains no express statement that it applies to pre-enactment re-entries, nor does it contain any necessary implication to that effect. *See id.* Accordingly, the statute cannot be construed to apply to Fernandez-Vargas’ 1982 re-entry.

**II. Even if the statute applies to pre-enactment re-entries, its application is impermissibly retroactive.**

A statute is retroactive if it "takes away or impairs vested rights acquired under existing laws, or creates a new obligation, imposes a new duty, or attaches a new disability in respect to transactions or considerations already past." *Shaw, 309 U.S. at 501*. The reinstatement statute does precisely that: it imposes a new disability—denial of all forms of relief—on aliens who re-entered illegally before its enactment. This Court has repeatedly held that such retroactive application is impermissible. *See Magness, 356 U.S. at 25*; *Shaw, 309 U.S. at 501*.

Fernandez-Vargas had a vested right to apply for adjustment of status under the INA as it existed at the time of his 2001 application. *See United States v. Heth, 7 U.S. (3 Cranch) 399, 412 (1806)* ("Rights acquired under existing laws are not to be taken away by a subsequent statute unless the intent to do so is clear and unequivocal."). The reinstatement statute, by retroactively stripping him of the right to seek adjustment of status, impairs this vested right. *See Magness, 356 U.S. at 25*. Such retroactive application is impermissible. *See id.*

**III. The Tenth Circuit’s holding that Fernandez-Vargas had no "protectable expectation of being able to adjust his status" is erroneous.**

The Tenth Circuit erred in holding that Fernandez-Vargas had no protectable expectation of being able to adjust his status. This Court has long recognized that aliens who have resided in the United States for many years and have established family and community ties have a protectable expectation of being able to remain in the United States. *See United States ex rel. Potash v. District Director, 169 F.2d 747, 750 (2d Cir. 1948)* ("[A]n alien who has been permitted to remain in this country for a long period of time and has established his home and family here has a right to expect that he will not be summarily deported."); *United States ex rel. Claussen v. Day, 279 U.S. 398, 402 (1929)* ("[A]n alien who has been permitted to remain in this country for a long period of time and has established his home and family here has a right to expect that he will not be summarily deported.").

Fernandez-Vargas had lived in the United States for nearly twenty years at the time of his 2001 application. He had married a U.S. citizen and had established a life in the United States. Under these circumstances, he had a protectable expectation of being able to adjust his status. The reinstatement statute, by retroactively stripping him of the right to seek adjustment of status, impairs this expectation. *See Potash, 169 F.2d at 750*. Such retroactive application is impermissible. *See id.*

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the Court should reverse the decision of the Tenth Circuit and hold that the reinstatement statute does not apply to aliens who re-entered the United States before its effective date. Even if the statute applies to pre-enactment re-entries, its application is impermissibly retroactive. The Tenth Circuit’s holding to the contrary is erroneous.
