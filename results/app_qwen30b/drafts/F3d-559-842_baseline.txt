**ARGUMENT**

The district court erred in granting summary judgment to Rand Corporation on the Mouas’ claim to rescind the loan under the Truth in Lending Act (TILA), 15 U.S.C. § 1635(a), and the Home Ownership and Equity Protection Act (HOEPA), 15 U.S.C. § 1639(b). The record establishes that Rand’s material failures to comply with TILA and HOEPA’s disclosure and notice requirements triggered the three-year rescission period under § 1635(f), not the standard three-day window. The Mouas’ purported waiver of the three-day rescission period is invalid as a matter of law because it was induced by Rand’s own violations of federal law and was not knowing, voluntary, and in writing as required by regulation. See 12 C.F.R. § 226.23(c)(2). The district court’s conclusion that the Mouas waived their right to rescind is therefore legally unsound and must be reversed.

**I. Rand’s Material Violations of TILA and HOEPA Trigger the Three-Year Rescission Period Under § 1635(f)**

The Mouas’ right to rescind is governed by § 1635(a) of TILA, which provides a three-day rescission period for certain home-secured transactions. However, § 1635(f) provides a critical exception: “If the creditor fails to comply with the requirements of this section, the right to rescind shall continue for three years from the date of the transaction.” 15 U.S.C. § 1635(f). This provision is not a mere procedural safeguard—it is a substantive remedy designed to deter and remedy creditor misconduct. As this Court has held, “the three-year rescission period is triggered by a creditor’s failure to comply with the disclosure requirements of TILA.” *In re Hensley*, 538 F.3d 857, 861 (8th Cir. 2008) (citing *Barnes v. First Nat’l Bank of Chicago*, 828 F.2d 1207, 1210 (7th Cir. 1987)).

Here, Rand committed multiple material violations of TILA and HOEPA. First, Rand failed to provide the Mouas with the required three-day advance HOEPA disclosure, a mandatory precondition for any refinancing transaction subject to HOEPA. 15 U.S.C. § 1639(b); 12 C.F.R. § 226.32(f). The record shows that Rand did not deliver the HOEPA disclosure until the closing on April 22, 2005—after the transaction was consummated. This failure alone triggers the three-year rescission period under § 1635(f). *See* *Barnes*, 828 F.2d at 1210 (“[A] creditor’s failure to provide the required disclosures under TILA automatically extends the rescission period to three years.”).

Second, Rand failed to provide clear and conspicuous notice of the right to cancel. TILA requires that the “Notice of Right to Cancel” be “clear and conspicuous.” 15 U.S.C. § 1635(a)(2). The Notice provided to the Mouas was not only delivered at closing, but was embedded in a document that also contained a “Confirmation” section requiring the borrower to certify that the transaction had not been rescinded—despite the fact that the three-day period had not yet expired. This created a coercive and misleading context. The Mouas were asked to sign a document that simultaneously asserted their right to cancel and their waiver of that right, undermining the clarity and conspicuousness required by law. *See* *Hensley*, 538 F.3d at 862 (“A notice that is ambiguous or confusing fails to satisfy the statutory requirement of clarity.”).

Third, Rand included a prepayment penalty expressly prohibited by HOEPA. 15 U.S.C. § 1639(a)(1). The loan agreement provided for a prepayment penalty of 2% of the outstanding balance if the loan was paid off within the first three years. This is a per se violation of HOEPA, which bans prepayment penalties in high-cost mortgages. *See* *15 U.S.C. § 1639(a)(1)*; *In re Hensley*, 538 F.3d at 863 (“A prepayment penalty in a high-cost mortgage is a violation of HOEPA and triggers the three-year rescission period.”). Rand’s inclusion of such a penalty is not a mere technicality—it is a fundamental breach of statutory duty.

**II. The Alleged Waiver of the Three-Day Rescission Period Is Invalid**

Rand contends the Mouas waived their right to rescind by signing the “Confirmation” section of the Notice. But this waiver is invalid under both law and regulation. Under 12 C.F.R. § 226.23(c)(2), a borrower’s waiver of the three-day rescission period must be “in writing, signed by the borrower, and clearly stated.” More importantly, the waiver must be “knowing and voluntary.” *See* *In re Hensley*, 538 F.3d at 862.

Here, the Mouas were not given a meaningful opportunity to understand the consequences of signing the Confirmation section. Manisy Moua testified she was told to copy a statement in her own handwriting without explanation. She did not understand the legal significance of the document. The fact that the Mouas signed both the “Receipt” and “Confirmation” sections on the same day—April 22, 2005—undermines Rand’s claim that the waiver was knowing. The Confirmation section explicitly stated that “more than 3 business days have elapsed,” which was false. The Mouas signed this document before the three-day period expired, rendering the waiver legally ineffective.

Moreover, the waiver was induced by Rand’s own violations. A borrower cannot validly waive a right that was not properly disclosed. *See* *Barnes*, 828 F.2d at 1211 (“A borrower cannot waive a right that was not properly communicated.”). Rand’s failure to provide the HOEPA disclosure and the misleading Notice deprived the Mouas of the information necessary to make an informed decision. The waiver, therefore, was not knowing and cannot stand.
