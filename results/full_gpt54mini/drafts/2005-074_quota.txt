**Argument**

I. **Section 241(a)(5) does not apply to Fernandez-Vargas because Congress did not clearly make it reach aliens who had already reentered before IIRIRA’s effective date.**

The reinstatement provision, 8 U.S.C. § 1231(a)(5), carries a severe consequence: it not only reinstates an old removal order, but also forecloses “any relief” under the INA. *Fernandez-Vargas v. Gonzales*, 548 U.S. 30, 34-35 (2006). Because statutes are presumed to operate prospectively absent clear congressional direction, courts do not lightly read new laws to attach new legal consequences to past conduct. *Landgraf v. USI Film Products*, 511 U.S. 244, 265-73 (1994); *INS v. St. Cyr*, 533 U.S. 289, 316-20 (2001).

The text of § 1231(a)(5) speaks in the present tense to an alien who “has reentered” unlawfully, but it does not expressly state that it reaches aliens whose reentry was complete before April 1, 1997. Congress knows how to make immigration amendments apply to preexisting conduct when it wishes to do so. *St. Cyr*, 533 U.S. at 318-20. Where Congress has not “clearly manifested” retroactive intent, the statute is construed against retroactivity. *Landgraf*, 511 U.S. at 280; *Hughes Aircraft Co. v. United States ex rel. Schumer*, 520 U.S. 939, 947-51 (1997).

The Court’s retroactivity cases turn on whether the statute attaches new legal consequences to completed conduct or newly impairs vested rights and settled expectations. *Landgraf*, 511 U.S. at 269-70; *Martin v. Hadix*, 527 U.S. 343, 357-58 (1999). Here, Fernandez-Vargas illegally reentered in 1982, well before § 1231(a)(5) existed. By the time the statute took effect, the reentry was a completed past event. Reading § 1231(a)(5) to reach him would transform a previously existing factual status into a new ground for mandatory removal. That is the sort of retroactive effect disfavored by *Landgraf* and *Hughes Aircraft*, 520 U.S. at 947-51.

II. **Even if § 1231(a)(5) can reach pre-enactment reentrants, applying it here is impermissibly retroactive because Fernandez-Vargas took concrete steps to pursue relief before the statute became effective.**

The Court in *Fernandez-Vargas* held that the statute could apply to an alien who “voluntarily chose to remain” unlawfully after IIRIRA and who did nothing before the effective date to change his status. 548 U.S. at 44-46. But the retroactivity inquiry is case-specific. A statute has an impermissible retroactive effect when it “attaches new legal consequences to events completed before its enactment.” *Landgraf*, 511 U.S. at 269-70. That principle is especially forceful where a party has acted in reliance on prior law by pursuing an available form of relief. *St. Cyr*, 533 U.S. at 321-26.

Here, Fernandez-Vargas married a United States citizen and applied to adjust status while that relief remained available. Under pre-IIRIRA law, adjustment of status was a lawful pathway to regularize an unlawful presence. See *St. Cyr*, 533 U.S. at 325-26. The new statute eliminated that avenue and made his prior deportation order immediately executable. That is not merely a change in procedure; it alters the legal effect of a past reentry and deprives him of relief he could seek under the law then in force. See *St. Cyr*, 533 U.S. at 314-26; *Vartelas v. Holder*, 566 U.S. 257, 264-73 (2012).

The government will rely on *Fernandez-Vargas*, 548 U.S. 30 (2006), but that decision does not compel affirmance here. The Court there emphasized the petitioner’s “continuing violation” of remaining unlawfully in the country and his failure to take steps to obtain status before IIRIRA. 548 U.S. at 44-46. It distinguished cases where a statute upsets settled expectations created by affirmative reliance on prior law. Id.; see also *St. Cyr*, 533 U.S. at 321-26. Fernandez-Vargas’s adjustment application places him on the *St. Cyr* side of the line, not the *Fernandez-Vargas* side.

Nor can the government avoid retroactivity by recasting the statute as merely “prospective” because the formal reinstatement decision occurred after 1997. A law is retroactive when it changes the legal consequences of earlier conduct, even if later events trigger enforcement. *Landgraf*, 511 U.S. at 269-70; *Vartelas*, 566 U.S. at 273-75. The operative past conduct here is Fernandez-Vargas’s 1982 reentry and his long residence under the prior legal regime. Applying § 1231(a)(5) to that completed history imposes a new disability the old law did not impose. *Martin*, 527 U.S. at 357-58.

For those reasons, the Court should hold that § 1231(a)(5) does not reach Fernandez-Vargas, and if it does, its application in this case is impermissibly retroactive.
