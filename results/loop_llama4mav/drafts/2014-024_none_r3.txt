ARGUMENT

The Third Circuit's affirmance of Elonis' conviction under 18 U.S.C. § 875(c) without requiring proof of his subjective intent to threaten contravenes the First Amendment's protections. The "true threats" exception, which § 875(c) embodies, has long been understood to necessitate a showing of the speaker's intent to convey a threat.

The Supreme Court has consistently underscored the importance of intent in determining the constitutionality of speech regulations. In Cantwell v. Connecticut, 310 U.S. 296, 309-310 (1940), the Court emphasized that the speaker's intent is a crucial factor in evaluating the constitutionality of a statute regulating speech. Chaplinsky v. New Hampshire, 315 U.S. 568, 573-574 (1942), further supports this principle, as the Court upheld a statute prohibiting "fighting words" that inflict injury or tend to incite an immediate breach of the peace.

Lower courts have also acknowledged the significance of intent in determining whether a statement constitutes a threat. The Seventh Circuit, in United States v. Prochaska, 222 F.2d 1, 2 (7th Cir. 1955), and the Second Circuit, in United States v. Carrier, 168 F.2d 881, 882 (2d Cir. 1948), have held that a threat requires an intent to cause fear or apprehension. These decisions demonstrate that the speaker's intent is essential in assessing the nature of a statement.

The Third Circuit's reliance on United States v. Kosma, 951 F.2d 549 (3d Cir. 1991), is misplaced, as Kosma's objective standard for determining true threats is inconsistent with the emphasis on intent in relevant precedents. Cases such as Pierce v. United States, 314 U.S. 306, 311 (1941), and Gorin v. United States, 312 U.S. 19, 27-28 (1941), have consistently required proof of the defendant's intent to convey a prohibited message when regulating speech.

Moreover, the Court has required a showing of intent to incite violence or lawlessness when regulating speech. Terminiello v. Chicago, 337 U.S. 1, 4-5 (1949), and Herndon v. Lowry, 301 U.S. 242, 259-260 (1937), reinforce this principle, as the Court held that convictions for disorderly conduct and insurrection, respectively, necessitate a showing of intent.

Schenck v. United States, 249 U.S. 47, 52 (1919), further highlights the importance of intent in determining the constitutionality of speech regulations. In light of these precedents, it is clear that a conviction under § 875(c) requires proof of the defendant's subjective intent to threaten.

Reversing the Third Circuit's decision and remanding the case for further proceedings consistent with a subjective intent standard is necessary to ensure that the "true threats" exception is applied in a manner consistent with the First Amendment's protections. By requiring proof of Elonis' subjective intent to threaten, the Court can safeguard the constitutional rights of individuals while protecting the public from genuine threats.
