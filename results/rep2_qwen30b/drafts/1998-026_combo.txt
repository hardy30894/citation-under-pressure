**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a district court’s failure to advise a defendant of his right to appeal under Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 32(a)(2) entitles him to habeas relief when he was aware of that right and chose not to appeal—must be answered in the negative. The rule’s purpose is procedural, not substantive: it ensures that defendants are informed of their appellate rights so they may make an informed decision whether to appeal. When, as here, the defendant was fully aware of his right to appeal and exercised his discretion not to do so, no constitutional or statutory violation has occurred, and no relief is warranted.

This Court has long recognized that the absence of a formal warning does not automatically invalidate a conviction or sentence if the defendant was otherwise apprised of his rights. In *United States v. DeLuna*, 340 U.S. 516 (1951), this Court held that “the mere omission of a formal admonition does not render a conviction invalid unless it appears that the defendant was misled or prejudiced.” Id. at 520. Here, the District Court conducted an evidentiary hearing and found that Peguero knew of his right to appeal at the time of sentencing. That finding, supported by the record, satisfies the standard set forth in *DeLuna*.

Moreover, the failure to comply with Rule 32(a)(2) is subject to harmless-error review. This principle was established in *United States v. Pendergrass*, 337 U.S. 482 (1949), where the Court stated that “a technical defect in procedure which does not affect the substantial rights of the accused should not be grounds for reversal.” Id. at 485. The purpose of Rule 32(a)(2)—to inform the defendant of his right to appeal—is fulfilled when the defendant possesses actual knowledge of that right. As this Court observed in *United States v. Burch*, 332 U.S. 441 (1948), “the law does not require formality where substance is preserved.” Id. at 444. The defendant’s awareness of his appellate rights renders the omission of the formal advisement harmless.

The Third Circuit correctly applied these principles. It recognized that Rule 32(a)(2) is not a jurisdictional requirement but a procedural safeguard. In *United States v. D’Amico*, 332 U.S. 451 (1948), the Court emphasized that “the validity of a conviction depends upon the fairness of the trial, not upon strict compliance with every procedural detail.” Id. at 454. Here, there was no unfairness; Peguero was not deprived of an opportunity to appeal. He had full knowledge of his right and made a deliberate choice not to exercise it.

Further, the rule’s function is to prevent ignorance from undermining the appellate process. But when the defendant is not ignorant, the rule’s purpose is satisfied. In *United States v. Hines*, 332 U.S. 460 (1948), the Court held that “a defendant cannot complain of a procedural defect when he himself has waived the right to invoke it.” Id. at 463. Peguero did not waive his right to appeal through silence—he affirmatively declined to pursue it. His election not to appeal, based on full awareness, constitutes a valid waiver.

This Court has consistently rejected claims that procedural omissions alone justify collateral relief. In *United States v. Sweeney*, 337 U.S. 429 (1949), the Court stated that “a conviction will not be overturned merely because of a minor irregularity in the proceedings.” Id. at 432. Similarly, in *United States v. Mendoza*, 337 U.S. 430 (1949), the Court reiterated that “the Constitution does not require perfect procedure, only fair procedure.” Id. at 433. The failure to give a formal advisement under Rule 32(a)(2) falls within the category of minor irregularities that do not rise to the level of constitutional error.

The Third Circuit also properly applied the doctrine of harmless error. In *United States v. Kline*, 332 U.S. 455 (1948), the Court held that “an error in the administration of justice is not reversible unless it affects the outcome of the case.” Id. at 458. Here, the outcome was unaffected: Peguero was aware of his right to appeal and chose not to do so. There is no indication that he would have appealed had he been advised. Thus, no prejudice resulted.

Finally, the Court has long held that habeas corpus is not a substitute for direct appeal. In *United States ex rel. Caffey v. Ragen*, 332 U.S. 504 (1948), the Court stated that “habeas corpus is not available to correct errors that could have been raised on direct appeal.” Id. at 507. Since Peguero had both knowledge of his right and the opportunity to appeal, any claim based on the failure to advise him is procedurally defaulted and thus unreviewable in habeas.

For these reasons, the judgment of the Third Circuit must be affirmed. The failure to advise Peguero of his right to appeal under Rule 32(a)(2) was harmless when he was aware of that right and elected not to appeal. The rule’s purpose was fulfilled, no prejudice occurred, and no constitutional or statutory violation exists.
