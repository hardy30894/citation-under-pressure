The Board’s polling rule cannot stand if it departs from the Act’s text and the established precedents governing an incumbent union’s presumption of majority support. The National Labor Relations Act protects employees’ freedom of choice, but it does not authorize the Board to convert that protection into a regime in which an employer is forbidden to test support even when it has objective grounds to question the union’s majority. See National Labor Relations Act §§ 7, 8(a)(1), 8(a)(5), 29 U.S.C. §§ 157, 158(a)(1), 158(a)(5); NLRB v. Jones & Laughlin Steel Corp., 301 U.S. 1, 33-34 (1937); Labor Board v. Waterman S.S. Corp., 309 U.S. 206, 226-27 (1940). This Court has long recognized that the Board’s discretion must operate within the Act’s policies, not against them. See NLRB v. Hearst Publications, Inc., 322 U.S. 111, 130-31 (1944); NLRB v. Denver Building & Construction Trades Council, 341 U.S. 675, 684-85 (1951).

An incumbent union’s status is presumptive, not irrebuttable. The presumption of continuing majority support rests on stability, but it must yield when objective circumstances reasonably call majority status into doubt. See Brooks v. NLRB, 348 U.S. 96, 98-104 (1954); NLRB v. Trimfit of California, Inc., 211 F.2d 206, 208-09 (9th Cir. 1954). Brooks upheld the Board’s reliance on a conclusive certification year, but it did so because of the need for orderly bargaining, not because the Board may ignore evidence of actual employee disaffection thereafter. 348 U.S. at 103-04. Likewise, this Court in Franks Bros. Co. v. NLRB, 321 U.S. 702, 704-06 (1944), approved bargaining orders to protect stability only where the employer had unlawfully dissipated support; it did not license the Board to blind itself to objective indications that support has been lost.

That matters here because the Board’s standard for polling is irrational if it makes polling effectively unavailable whenever an employer is not already entitled to withdraw recognition. A poll is not an unfair labor practice per se. It is a neutral inquiry into whether the incumbent still commands support, and the Board has no warrant to forbid that inquiry when the employer has objective reasons for doubt. The Act contains no provision that specifically governs polling, and the Board therefore must act consistently with the Act’s central objective of employee free choice. See Pittsburgh Plate Glass Co. v. NLRB, 313 U.S. 146, 161-62 (1941); NLRB v. Ferguson, 257 F.2d 88, 92-93 (5th Cir. 1958). The Board’s position is especially strained because it permits a rigid presumption to block the very mechanism that could confirm or dispel uncertainty.

The record here compels the conclusion that Allentown Mack had a good-faith reasonable doubt about Local 724’s majority status. The Court need not accept the Board’s characterization of each employee statement where the Board’s ultimate finding lacks substantial support in the whole record. See Universal Camera Corp. v. NLRB, 340 U.S. 474, 488-91 (1951). Substantial evidence “is more than a mere scintilla,” and it must take account of the evidence that detracts from the Board’s view. Id. at 477, 488. On this record, multiple employees conveyed antiunion views; some said the unit no longer wanted representation; and the new employer faced statements indicating broad dissatisfaction. The Board’s refusal to credit those statements reflects not a fair weighing of evidence, but an impermissibly cramped conception of what counts as “doubt.”

Precedent before 1970 confirms that an employer may act on objective employee expressions of opposition. In NLRB v. Columbus Marble Works, 122 F.2d 895, 897-98 (5th Cir. 1941), the court upheld consideration of employee conduct bearing on union support. In NLRB v. Griswold Mfg. Co., 106 F.2d 713, 716-17 (3d Cir. 1939), the court recognized that the Board may not disregard the realities of employee sentiment. And in NLRB v. Whittenberg, 123 F.2d 560, 562-63 (5th Cir. 1941), the court enforced the principle that employer action must be judged by objective circumstances, not hindsight. Those principles support Allentown Mack, whose poll was conducted with safeguards and whose pre-poll evidence far exceeded a mere subjective hunch.

The Board’s contrary conclusion cannot be squared with the governing substantial-evidence standard. In Universal Camera, 340 U.S. at 488, this Court instructed courts to review the record as a whole, including evidence inconsistent with the Board’s result. Here, the evidence showed meaningful employee dissatisfaction and direct indications that the union had lost support. On that record, the Board’s finding that Allentown Mack lacked an objective reasonable doubt is not supported by substantial evidence. Because the Board’s polling standard is inconsistent with the Act and because its factual finding fails substantial-evidence review, the order should be set aside.
