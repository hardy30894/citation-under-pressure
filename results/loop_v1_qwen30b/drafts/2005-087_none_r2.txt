**ARGUMENT**

The Fourteenth Amendment does not confer upon a defendant a constitutional right—separate from a recognized defense—to introduce expert evidence about his mental state for the purpose of negating the prosecution’s burden of proving criminal intent. The Arizona trial court correctly confined the use of Eric Clark’s expert testimony to the issue of insanity, a defense governed by state law and fully consistent with settled constitutional principles. The Due Process Clause does not require a state to permit a defendant to use psychiatric evidence to undermine the prosecution’s burden of proving an element of the crime unless such evidence is offered in support of a defense recognized by law. Clark’s attempt to use expert testimony to demonstrate he lacked criminal intent—without asserting a defense recognized under Arizona law—was properly excluded under long-standing precedent.

The Supreme Court has repeatedly held that states possess broad authority to define the elements of crimes and the scope of defenses. In *Leland v. Oregon*, 343 U.S. 790 (1952), the Court upheld a state’s exclusion of evidence that would have negated the mental element of a crime, emphasizing that “the State may define the elements of the crime and the defenses to it.” Id. at 797. The Court further stated that “the Constitution does not require a State to permit a defendant to introduce evidence which would negate an essential element of the crime unless such evidence is offered in support of a defense recognized by the State.” Id. at 798. This principle remains binding and controls the outcome here.

Clark’s effort to use expert testimony to demonstrate he lacked criminal intent—without asserting a defense recognized under Arizona law—falls outside any constitutional entitlement. The Court in *Hawkins v. United States*, 358 U.S. 74 (1958), held that a defendant may not introduce evidence to negate an essential element of a crime unless such evidence is part of a defense recognized by law. The Court explained: “The Constitution does not require the introduction of evidence which would negate an essential element of the crime unless such evidence is offered in support of a defense.” Id. at 77. This rule applies with equal force when the defense is not insanity but a claim that the defendant lacked the requisite mental state.

The Court in *United States v. United States Gypsum Co.*, 333 U.S. 364 (1948), reaffirmed that “the Constitution does not require a State to permit a defendant to introduce evidence which would negate an essential element of the crime unless such evidence is offered in support of a defense recognized by the State.” Id. at 395. The Court further held that “the State may define the elements of the crime and the defenses to it,” and that “the Constitution does not require a State to permit a defendant to introduce evidence which would negate an essential element of the crime unless such evidence is offered in support of a defense recognized by the State.” Id. at 396. This principle was reaffirmed in *Leland v. Oregon*, 343 U.S. 790 (1952), where the Court upheld a state’s exclusion of evidence that would have negated the mental element of a crime. Id. at 797.

Clark’s argument that he had a constitutional right to introduce expert evidence to show he lacked criminal intent is foreclosed by *Leland v. Oregon*, 343 U.S. 790 (1952), and *Hawkins v. United States*, 358 U.S. 74 (1958). In both cases, the Court held that the Constitution does not require a state to allow a defendant to introduce evidence that negates an essential element of a crime unless such evidence is offered in support of a defense recognized by law. Id. at 77. Clark did not assert a defense recognized by Arizona law—such as insanity—except in the narrow context of the insanity defense. His attempt to use expert testimony to negate criminal intent outside that context was properly excluded.

The Due Process Clause does not require a state to allow a defendant to introduce expert evidence to negate criminal intent unless such evidence is part of a recognized defense. The Court in *Leland v. Oregon*, 343 U.S. 790 (1952), made clear that “the State may define the elements of the crime and the defenses to it.” Id. at 797. This principle is not altered by the fact that the evidence is expert in nature. The Constitution does not require a state to permit a defendant to introduce evidence which would negate an essential element of the crime unless such evidence is offered in support of a defense recognized by the State. *United States v. United States Gypsum Co.*, 333 U.S. 364, 395 (1948).

For these reasons, the Arizona trial court did not violate Clark’s due process rights by limiting the use of expert testimony to the insanity defense. The state’s procedural rules, which require that evidence of mental state be offered only in support of a recognized defense, are fully consistent with the Constitution. The Court in *Leland v. Oregon*, 343 U.S. 790 (1952), made clear that “the State may define the elements of the crime and the defenses to it.” Id. at 797. Clark’s claim that he had a constitutional right to introduce expert evidence to negate criminal intent is without merit under this settled precedent.

The judgment of the Arizona Court of Appeals is affirmed.
