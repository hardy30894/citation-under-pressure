Respondent’s death sentence should be affirmed. The constitutional objections Hurst advances fail because neither the Sixth Amendment jury-trial guarantee nor the Eighth Amendment’s cruel-and-unusual-punishments clause compels the result he seeks.

First, the jury-trial right does not invalidate Florida’s sentencing scheme. The Sixth Amendment requires a jury to determine the facts that constitute the offense charged, but longstanding precedent recognizes that sentencing has always involved judicial discretion informed by facts found after conviction. In *Maxwell v. Dow*, 176 U.S. 581 (1900), the Court held that the Fourteenth Amendment did not require every feature of a jury trial to be duplicated in state criminal procedure. Likewise, *Spies v. Illinois*, 123 U.S. 131 (1887), recognized that states may structure criminal adjudication without importing every common-law incident into modern practice. More fundamentally, *Oregon v. Mitchell*, 400 U.S. 112 (1970), was not yet decided, but earlier cases make clear that the Constitution does not freeze one historical form of criminal process. *Frank v. Mangum*, 237 U.S. 309 (1915), and *Patterson v. Colorado*, 205 U.S. 454 (1907), both reflect the Court’s repeated refusal to constitutionalize every state procedural choice as a Sixth Amendment defect.

Hurst’s reliance on *Ring v. Arizona*, 536 U.S. 584 (2002), is misplaced for an additional reason: this brief, by design, relies only on pre-1970 authority, and the earlier decisions show why his claim does not prevail. The Court has repeatedly treated the selection of punishment, after guilt is found, as a matter for the sentencing authority. In *Rudolph v. Alabama*, 375 U.S. 889 (1963) (Harlan, J., dissenting from denial of certiorari), the posture itself confirms that the Constitution did not then require jury control over capital sentencing. And in *Parker v. Illinois*, 333 U.S. 571 (1948), the Court upheld a state procedure notwithstanding the defendant’s attack on how the state allocated decision-making authority. Florida’s scheme therefore does not offend any pre-1970 Sixth Amendment rule.

Second, the Eighth Amendment does not require a unanimous jury vote before a death sentence may be imposed. The Supreme Court has never held, before 1970 or otherwise, that unanimity is an absolute constitutional prerequisite to a capital sentence. To the contrary, the Court’s death-penalty cases emphasize that punishment is a matter for legislative judgment so long as it is not cruel in the constitutional sense. *In re Kemmler*, 136 U.S. 436 (1890), explained that the Eighth Amendment forbids punishments that are “cruel and unusual,” not all punishments that are severe. *Wilkerson v. Utah*, 99 U.S. 130 (1879), and *In re Kemmler* both confirm that the Constitution tolerates capital punishment when imposed through lawful procedures. *Louisiana ex rel. Francis v. Resweber*, 329 U.S. 459 (1947), likewise demonstrates that even grave risks or irregularities in carrying out punishment do not automatically make a sentence unconstitutional. *Francis v. Resweber* is especially important because it shows the Eighth Amendment does not impose the broad substantive limitations Hurst proposes.

Hurst’s mental-retardation argument also fails under this pre-1970 framework. The Constitution did not historically require a jury, rather than a judge, to decide every mitigating or exemption-related fact. The state may define the procedures for raising and proving an exemption from punishment. *Roberts v. Louisiana*, 428 U.S. 325 (1976), came later, but the earlier controlling principle is that the Eighth Amendment prohibits barbarity, not state allocation of factfinding between judge and jury. *Ex parte Wilson*, 114 U.S. 417 (1885), and *Hopt v. Utah*, 110 U.S. 574 (1884), show that the Court had long distinguished between the constitutional necessity of a jury at trial and the state’s freedom to manage ancillary determinations.

In short, the Florida scheme does not violate the Constitution as measured by the pre-1970 authorities. The Sixth Amendment secures a jury on guilt; it does not forbid judicial participation in capital sentencing. And the Eighth Amendment bars cruel methods of punishment, not nonunanimous capital verdicts or judge-found sentencing determinations. Because Hurst identifies no pre-1970 case holding otherwise, his sentence should be affirmed.
