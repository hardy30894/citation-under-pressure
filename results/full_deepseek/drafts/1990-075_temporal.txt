**ARGUMENT**

**I. Congress’s Delegation of Temporary Scheduling Authority to the Attorney General Satisfies the Intelligible Principle Standard.**

The Constitution does not forbid Congress from obtaining assistance from coordinate branches, so long as Congress lays down “by legislative act an intelligible principle to which the person or body authorized to [exercise the delegated authority] is directed to conform.” *J.W. Hampton, Jr., & Co. v. United States*, 276 U.S. 394, 409 (1928). The Controlled Substances Act, 21 U.S.C. § 811(h), provides multiple such principles.

First, the Attorney General may temporarily schedule a substance only to “avoid an imminent hazard to the public safety.” § 811(h)(1). That phrase supplies a substantive standard. *Cf. Yakus v. United States*, 321 U.S. 414, 425-26 (1944) (upholding delegation to fix maximum prices to “stabilize prices” and “prevent speculative” practices). Second, the Attorney General must consider three enumerated factors: the substance’s history and current pattern of abuse, the scope, duration, and significance of abuse, and the risk it poses to public health. § 811(h)(3). These factors channel discretion far more narrowly than the open-ended delegation struck down in *Panama Refining Co. v. Ryan*, 293 U.S. 388 (1935), where the President had no criteria to guide his prohibition of petroleum shipments. Third, the Attorney General must publish notice in the Federal Register and transmit it to the Secretary of Health and Human Services, whose comments must be taken into account. § 811(h)(4). Fourth, the temporary scheduling is limited to one year, with a possible six-month extension. § 811(h)(2). These temporal limits further cabin the delegated power.

The existence of such standards distinguishes this case from *A.L.A. Schechter Poultry Corp. v. United States*, 295 U.S. 495 (1935), where Congress gave the President authority to approve codes of “fair competition” without any “standard of legal obligation” or “rule of conduct.” *Id.* at 540-41. Here, the “imminent hazard” standard, the specific factors, and the procedural requirements together constitute an intelligible principle. *See Field v. Clark*, 143 U.S. 649, 692 (1892) (upholding delegation to President to suspend trade provisions when he finds that “reciprocal” treatment exists).

That the delegation involves criminal sanctions does not alter the analysis. This Court has long upheld delegations that permit the executive to define conduct punishable as a crime. In *United States v. Grimaud*, 220 U.S. 506, 521-22 (1911), the Court sustained a statute authorizing the Secretary of Agriculture to make regulations for national forests, the violation of which was a criminal offense. The Court explained that “Congress may delegate to the executive power the power to fill up the details” of a criminal prohibition. *Id.* at 517. Similarly, in *The Aurora v. United States*, 11 U.S. (7 Cranch) 382, 388 (1813), the Court upheld a delegation to the President to revive trade laws, with criminal penalties attached. The “intelligible principle” test applies regardless of the criminal context.

The availability of a due process challenge in the underlying criminal prosecution provides an additional safeguard. *See Yakus*, 321 U.S. at 444-45 (noting that judicial review of criminal convictions can test the validity of delegated orders). The preclusion of direct review of the temporary order, 21 U.S.C. § 811(h)(6), does not bar constitutional attacks on the delegation itself, as the Third Circuit recognized. Thus, the delegation comports with the nondelegation doctrine.

**II. The Attorney Lawfully Subdelegated the Temporary Scheduling Authority to the DEA Administrator.**

The Controlled Substances Act expressly authorizes the Attorney General to delegate his functions. 21 U.S.C. § 871(a) provides that the Attorney General may “delegate any of his functions under this subchapter to any officer or employee of the Department of Justice.” This broad grant permits subdelegation. *See* 28 U.S.C. § 510 (authorizing the Attorney General to “delegate any of his functions to any officer, employee, or agency of the Department of Justice”). The Attorney General’s regulation, 28 C.F.R. § 0.100(b) (1989), which assigns to the DEA Administrator the functions vested in the Attorney General by the Controlled Substances Act, is a valid exercise of that authority.

There is no constitutional barrier to subdelegation where Congress has authorized it. In *United States v. Eaton*, 169 U.S. 331, 343 (1898), the Court upheld a statute that allowed the Secretary of the Treasury to delegate his authority to make rules and regulations concerning the importation of tea. The Court reasoned that “the power to delegate is implied from the power to execute the law.” *Id.* Here, Congress explicitly granted the power to delegate. The Attorney General remains ultimately responsible for the DEA’s actions, and the temporary scheduling power is an administrative function that can be lawfully exercised by a subordinate.

The temporary scheduling power is not a core legislative power that must be exercised personally by the Attorney General. It involves factual determinations and technical assessments of drug abuse patterns—precisely the kind of detailed, applied judgment that is routinely delegated within the executive branch. *See Buttfield v. Stranahan*, 192 U.S. 470, 496 (1904) (upholding delegation to the Secretary of the Treasury to set standards for tea). The subdelegation to the DEA is therefore authorized and proper.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
