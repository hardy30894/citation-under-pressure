**ARGUMENT**

The Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act extends workers’ compensation coverage only to injuries occurring on the outer Continental Shelf itself. The plain text of the statute, this Court’s settled precedent, and the uniform rule of statutory construction all compel reversal of the Ninth Circuit’s decision.

**I. The Statutory Text Requires a Situs-of-Injury Requirement**

Section 1333(b) of OCSLA provides that LHWCA benefits are payable for “disability or death of an employee resulting from any injury occurring as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf.” 43 U.S.C. § 1333(b). The phrase “operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf” unambiguously modifies the entire preceding clause. An injury must both (1) occur “as the result of” such operations, and (2) be an injury that itself occurs in connection with those shelf-based operations. The natural reading of the statute is that the injury must bear a geographic relationship to the shelf.

This Court has long held that OCSLA’s coverage is “determined principally by locale, not by the status of the individual injured or killed.” *Offshore Logistics, Inc. v. Tallentire*, 477 U.S. 207, 219 (1986). Although that case addressed a different OCSLA provision, the Court expressly noted that “only one provision of OCSLA superimposes a status requirement on the otherwise determinative OCSLA situs requirement; § 1333(b) makes compensation for the death or injury of an ‘employee’ resulting from certain operations on the Outer Continental Shelf payable under the Longshoremen’s and Harbor Workers’ Compensation Act.” *Id.* at 219 n.2. The Court’s observation that § 1333(b) is the *only* provision superimposing a status requirement confirms that the situs requirement remains otherwise determinative. The Ninth Circuit erred in dismissing this language as “unconsidered dictum.” This Court’s considered statements about the structure of OCSLA are entitled to great weight.

**II. This Court’s Pre-1970 Precedent Confirms the Geographic Limitation**

Before 1970, this Court consistently interpreted OCSLA to require that the injury occur on the shelf or its fixed structures. In *Rodrigue v. Aetna Casualty & Surety Co.*, 395 U.S. 352 (1969), the Court held that OCSLA’s choice-of-law provision applied only to “the subsoil and seabed of the outer Continental Shelf, and artificial islands and fixed structures erected thereon.” *Id.* at 355. The Court emphasized that OCSLA “adopts the law of the adjacent State as the law of the United States for the subsoil and seabed of the outer Continental Shelf and for artificial islands and fixed structures erected on such shelf.” *Id.* at 355-56. The Court rejected the argument that OCSLA coverage extended to injuries occurring on vessels or in the waters above the shelf. *Id.* at 359-60.

Similarly, in *Pure Oil Co. v. Snipes*, 293 F.2d 60 (5th Cir. 1961), the court held that OCSLA did not apply to an injury occurring on a drilling platform located in state territorial waters, because the platform was not on the outer Continental Shelf. *Id.* at 64-65. The court reasoned that OCSLA’s coverage is “limited to the outer Continental Shelf and to artificial islands and fixed structures erected thereon.” *Id.* at 65.

These decisions reflect the fundamental principle that OCSLA is a statute of limited geographic reach. Congress did not intend to create a nationwide workers’ compensation scheme for all employees whose work is somehow related to offshore operations. Rather, Congress extended LHWCA benefits only to those employees who are injured on the shelf itself.

**III. The Ninth Circuit’s “Substantial Nexus” Test Finds No Support in Pre-1970 Law**

The Ninth Circuit adopted a “substantial nexus” test that would extend OCSLA coverage to any injury with a sufficient connection to shelf operations, regardless of where the injury occurs. This test finds no support in any pre-1970 decision of this Court or any other federal court.

In *Curtis v. Schlumberger Offshore Service, Inc.*, 849 F.2d 805 (3d Cir. 1988), the Third Circuit applied a “but for” test, holding that an employee injured in a car accident on his way to meet a helicopter that would take him to an offshore platform was eligible for OCSLA benefits. *Id.* at 809-11. But that decision conflicts with the plain text of the statute and with this Court’s holding in *Tallentire* that OCSLA coverage is “determined principally by locale.” 477 U.S. at 219.

The Fifth Circuit’s approach in *Mills v. Director, Office of Workers’ Compensation Programs*, 877 F.2d 356 (5th Cir. 1989) (en banc), is correct. The Fifth Circuit held that an OCSLA claimant must show that the injury occurred on an outer continental shelf platform or on the waters above the outer continental shelf, in addition to satisfying a causal connection. *Id.* at 362. This approach respects the statutory text and this Court’s precedent.

**IV. The Facts of This Case Demonstrate the Need for a Clear Geographic Rule**

Juan Valladolid was killed at an onshore oil-processing facility located 250-300 feet from the coast. He was not on the outer Continental Shelf when he was injured. His injury bore no geographic connection to the shelf. Under the Ninth Circuit’s test, however, his widow could recover OCSLA benefits simply because his work involved scrap metal that originated from offshore platforms. This result would expand OCSLA coverage far beyond what Congress intended.

This Court has long recognized that OCSLA is a “carefully crafted” statute that “draws a clear geographic limitation.” *Herb’s Welding, Inc. v. Gray*, 470 U.S. 414, 422 (1985). The Court should reject the Ninth Circuit’s attempt to rewrite that limitation.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
