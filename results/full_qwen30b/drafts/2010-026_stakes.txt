**ARGUMENT**

The Ninth Circuit erred in holding that documents relating to the effects of explosions at Naval Magazine Indian Island—specifically, Explosive Safety Quantity Distance (ESQD) arc maps and associated technical data—are exempt from disclosure under FOIA’s Exemption 2 because they are “predominantly” for internal use and pose a risk of circumventing agency regulation. The court’s adoption of a broad, unmoored “predominantly internal” standard, divorced from the statutory text and Supreme Court precedent, improperly expands Exemption 2 beyond its narrow scope and undermines the foundational principle that FOIA exemptions must be “interpreted narrowly.” See *U.S. Dep’t of Justice v. Tax Analysts*, 492 U.S. 136, 151, 109 S. Ct. 2841, 2850 (1989). The documents at issue are not “internal personnel rules and practices” within the meaning of § 552(b)(2), nor do they meet the threshold for exemption under any other FOIA provision. The Navy’s claim of risk of circumvention is speculative and unsupported by the record, and the agency’s failure to classify the information under Exemption 1 further undermines its assertion of national security harm.

**I. The ESQD Documents Are Not “Internal Personnel Rules and Practices” Under Exemption 2**

Exemption 2 applies only to information “related solely to the internal personnel rules and practices of an agency.” 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(2). The Supreme Court has made clear that this exemption is not a catch-all for sensitive government information. In *Rose*, the Court emphasized that Exemption 2 “is not a general exemption for all information that might be sensitive or potentially harmful if disclosed,” but rather “a narrow exception” designed to protect only those materials that are “internal” in nature and “related solely” to agency operations. 425 U.S. at 361, 96 S. Ct. at 1599. The Court further explained that the exemption “does not extend to information that is of public interest or that relates to the agency’s external functions.” Id. at 363, 96 S. Ct. at 1600.

The Ninth Circuit’s adoption of the “predominantly internal” standard—borrowed from *Crooker v. Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco & Firearms*, 670 F.2d 1051 (D.C. Cir. 1981)—is inconsistent with this narrow construction. The D.C. Circuit’s approach, which permits withholding of information based on a subjective assessment of its “predominance” in internal use, has been rejected by the Supreme Court as overly expansive. In *Tax Analysts*, the Court reiterated that FOIA exemptions “are explicitly exclusive,” meaning that agencies may not rely on them to shield information not clearly covered by their terms. 492 U.S. at 151, 109 S. Ct. at 2850. The Court has never endorsed a “predominantly internal” test, nor has it suggested that technical safety data used in facility planning and risk mitigation qualifies as “internal personnel rules and practices.”

Here, the ESQD arc maps are not internal personnel rules. They are technical safety calculations used to determine safe storage distances for explosives, to design storage facilities, and to mitigate the risk of sympathetic detonations. As Commander Whitbred acknowledged, the Navy uses these maps “to design, array, and construct ammunition storage facilities, and to organize ammunition operations for risk mitigation and enhanced safety.” This is not a personnel practice; it is a core operational function related to public safety and infrastructure planning. The maps are not used to govern employee conduct, training, or internal procedures. They are not internal to the agency in any meaningful sense—they are tools for managing a public hazard.

The Ninth Circuit’s conclusion that these documents are “predominantly internal” is unsupported by any meaningful analysis. The court did not assess whether the documents were created for internal use, nor did it consider whether their primary purpose was to guide agency operations or to inform the public. Instead, it relied on a broad, formulaic application of the “predominantly internal” standard, which effectively allows agencies to withhold any technical data they deem sensitive, regardless of its actual function. This approach transforms Exemption 2 into a de facto national security exemption, which Congress did not intend.

**II. The Risk of Circumvention Is Speculative and Not Supported by the Record**

Even if the documents were internal, Exemption 2 requires a showing that disclosure would “present a risk of circumvention of agency regulation.” *Morley v. CIA*, 508 F.3d 1108, 1124 (D.C. Cir. 2007). The burden is on the agency to demonstrate this risk with specificity. *U.S. Dep’t of State v. Ray*, 502 U.S. 164, 173, 112 S. Ct. 541, 548 (1991). The Navy’s claim rests entirely on the affidavit of Commander Whitbred, who asserts that the maps could be used to “plan an attack or disrupt operations” at NMII. But this is a bare assertion, unsupported by any evidence of prior attempts to exploit such information, or of actual vulnerabilities in the Navy’s security posture.

Moreover, the Navy has already disclosed similar ESQD maps for the Bangor Naval Base—located just four to five miles from the nearest town—without any reported security incident. The fact that the Navy voluntarily released comparable data for Bangor, but refuses to do so for NMII, strongly suggests that the risk is not real, but political. The proximity of NMII to Port Townsend and Port Hadlock—less than two miles across open water—makes the public interest in this information particularly compelling. The community has a right to know the potential risks posed by the storage of explosives in their backyard.

The dissent’s suggestion that the Navy should be allowed to classify the maps under Exemption 1 is misplaced. Exemption 1 applies only to information “kept secret in the interest of national defense or foreign policy.” 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(1). The Navy has not classified these documents, nor has it provided any evidence that their disclosure would jeopardize national security.
