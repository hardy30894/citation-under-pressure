## ARGUMENT

The D.C. Circuit erred in holding that Guam’s 2004 consent decree with the Environmental Protection Agency triggered CERCLA § 113(f)(3)(B), thereby foreclosing Guam’s timely § 107 cost-recovery claim. The decision misreads the statutory text, conflicts with this Court’s precedents, and creates a circuit split on a critical question of CERCLA interpretation.

### I. The Consent Decree Did Not “Resolve” Guam’s Liability for a CERCLA Response Action

Section 113(f)(3)(B) permits contribution only by a party that “has resolved its liability to the United States or a State for some or all of a response action or for some or all of the costs of such action.” 42 U.S.C. § 9613(f)(3)(B). The consent decree here resolved nothing of the sort. The EPA’s underlying complaint alleged only Clean Water Act violations—discharges of untreated leachate without a permit. It did not seek recovery of CERCLA response costs or invoke CERCLA liability. The decree itself required Guam to pay a civil penalty, close the dump, and install a cover system—measures designed to abate ongoing water pollution, not to remediate hazardous substance releases under CERCLA.

This Court has held that a consent decree “resolves” liability only when it “definitively fixes the settling party’s share of response costs or otherwise allocates liability.” *Asarco LLC v. Atlantic Richfield Co.*, 866 F.3d 1108, 1122 (9th Cir. 2017). The decree here contained no such allocation. It expressly disclaimed any finding or admission of liability, stating it was “based on the pleadings, before taking testimony or adjudicating any issue of fact or law, and without any finding or admission of liability against or by the Government of Guam.” J.A. 92. As the Sixth Circuit recognized, a “disclaimer of liability may weigh against the conclusion that the parties intended to resolve liability.” *Florida Power Corp. v. FirstEnergy Corp.*, 810 F.3d 996, 1002 (6th Cir. 2015). The D.C. Circuit dismissed this language as insufficient, but that approach conflates a refusal to admit liability with a failure to resolve it. The decree resolved nothing about Guam’s CERCLA liability; it merely compelled compliance with the Clean Water Act.

Moreover, the decree did not address “response action” costs as defined by CERCLA. The statute defines “response” as “remove, removal, remedy, and remedial action,” 42 U.S.C. § 9601(25), and “remedial action” includes “the collection of leachate and runoff,” *id.* § 9601(24). But the decree’s cover system requirement was designed to stop ongoing discharges, not to clean up preexisting contamination. The D.C. Circuit’s contrary conclusion ignores the distinction between abating current violations and remediating past hazardous substance releases—a distinction Congress carefully maintained in CERCLA.

### II. Guam’s § 107 Claim Is Independent and Not Precluded by the Availability of § 113

This Court has made clear that a PRP may bring a § 107 cost-recovery claim even if it could also seek contribution under § 113. In *United States v. Atlantic Research Corp.*, 551 U.S. 128, 139 (2007), the Court held that “a PRP that has incurred response costs may recover under § 107(a)(4)(B) without any prior establishment of liability.” The only limitation is that a PRP cannot use § 107 to recover costs it has already recouped through a § 113 action. *Id.* at 140. Here, Guam never brought a § 113 claim; it incurred its own response costs as an owner/operator compelled to act by EPA. The D.C. Circuit’s rule—that a settlement triggering § 113 automatically bars § 107—contradicts *Atlantic Research* and this Court’s holding in *Cooper Industries, Inc. v. Aviall Services, Inc.*, 543 U.S. 157, 168 (2004), that § 113 provides an *additional* remedy, not an exclusive one.

The D.C. Circuit relied on the principle that a PRP that has “resolved its liability” must proceed under § 113. But that principle applies only when the settlement actually resolves CERCLA liability. As the Seventh Circuit explained, “the mere fact that a party refused to admit liability is not enough to exempt a consent decree from the reach of section 113(f)(3)(B)” only when the decree’s substantive terms otherwise allocate liability. *Refined Metals Corp. v. NL Industries*, 937 F.3d 928, 931 (7th Cir. 2019). Here, the decree’s substantive terms did not allocate CERCLA liability; they addressed only Clean Water Act compliance. Guam therefore retains its right to seek full cost recovery under § 107.

### III. The D.C. Circuit’s Decision Creates an Unworkable Rule

Under the D.C. Circuit’s approach, any settlement with EPA that requires a PRP to take action at a contaminated site—even if the settlement is under a different statute and disclaims CERCLA liability—will trigger § 113(f)(3)(B) and its three-year statute of limitations. This forces PRPs to choose between complying with EPA orders and preserving their right to seek contribution from other responsible parties. Congress could not have intended such a result. As this Court has recognized, CERCLA’s “overarching purpose” is to “promptly clean up hazardous waste sites and to ensure that responsible parties foot the bill.” *General Electric Co. v. Jackson*, 610 F.3d 110, 114 (D.C. Cir. 2010). The D.C. Circuit’s rule undermines that purpose by penalizing PRPs that cooperate with EPA.

The judgment should be reversed.
