**ARGUMENT**

**I. The District Court Erred in Dismissing Moll’s Hostile Work Environment Claim by Failing to Consider the Totality of the Circumstances, Including Facially Sex-Neutral Incidents**

A hostile work environment claim requires a plaintiff to show that “the workplace is permeated with discriminatory intimidation, ridicule, and insult” that is “sufficiently severe or pervasive to alter the conditions of the victim’s employment and create an abusive working environment.” *Harris v. Forklift Sys., Inc.*, 510 U.S. 17, 21 (1993). In evaluating such a claim, courts must examine “the totality of the circumstances,” including “the frequency of the discriminatory conduct; its severity; whether it is physically threatening or humiliating, or a mere offensive utterance; and whether it unreasonably interferes with an employee’s work performance.” *Id.* at 23.

The district court erred by confining its analysis to only those allegations it deemed “sexually offensive” and by refusing to consider the many facially sex-neutral incidents that Moll alleged. This Court has squarely held that “facially sex-neutral incidents may be included among the totality of the circumstances that courts consider in any hostile work environment claim.” *Alfano v. Costello*, 294 F.3d 365, 375 (2d Cir. 2002). Indeed, “the plaintiff need not show that the harassment was explicitly sexual in nature.” *Id.* at 373. Rather, the relevant question is whether the conduct, viewed in its entirety, “constitute[s] discrimination because of sex.” *Id.* at 374.

Here, Moll alleged a continuous course of conduct from 1998 through 2004 that included: inappropriate notes from Irving, repeated late-night phone calls to her hotel room, being forced to communicate with Irving only in person, being denied promotion opportunities while male colleagues were promoted, being placed on a counseling plan, being denied the ability to work from home while male counterparts were permitted to do so, being denied vacation time that was granted to less-tenured male colleagues, and being excluded from work-related social events. The district court dismissed the hostile work environment claim because it found no “sexually offensive acts” within the limitations period. But that approach improperly isolated individual incidents and ignored the cumulative, synergistic effect of the entire course of conduct. *See Nat’l R.R. Passenger Corp. v. Morgan*, 536 U.S. 101, 116 (2002) (holding that a hostile work environment claim “is comprised of a series of separate acts that collectively constitute one unlawful employment practice” and that courts must consider the “entire scope of the claim, including conduct outside the limitations period, so long as an act contributing to the claim occurs within the filing period”).

The district court’s piecemeal approach also contravenes the continuing violation doctrine. Moll filed her EEOC charge on September 19, 2003. The district court concluded that the only sexually explicit conduct—the 1998–1999 notes and the 2001 shower note—occurred outside the limitations period. But Moll alleged that the hostile environment continued well into 2003 and 2004 through the facially neutral acts described above. Because those acts were part of the same unlawful employment practice, the district court was required to consider them in their totality. *See Morgan*, 536 U.S. at 118. Its failure to do so was error.

**II. The District Court Erred in Applying the “Sham Issue of Fact” Doctrine to Disregard a Non-Party Witness’s Subsequent Testimony**

The district court also erred in granting summary judgment by disregarding the subsequent testimony of a non-party witness on the ground that it conflicted with that witness’s prior statement. The “sham issue of fact” doctrine is narrow: it permits a court to disregard a party’s own contradictory affidavit submitted in opposition to summary judgment when the contradiction is unexplained and is designed to create a genuine issue of fact. *See, e.g., Hayes v. N.Y. City Dep’t of Corr.*, 84 F.3d 614, 619 (2d Cir. 1996). The doctrine exists to prevent a party from “defeating a motion for summary judgment by contradicting his own prior testimony.” *Id.*

But this Court has never extended the doctrine to non-party witnesses. To the contrary, the rationale for the doctrine—that a party should not be permitted to manufacture a factual dispute by contradicting his own sworn statements—does not apply to a witness who has no stake in the litigation. A non-party witness’s subsequent testimony, even if inconsistent with an earlier statement, goes to the weight and credibility of that testimony, not its admissibility. Credibility determinations are for the jury, not the court on summary judgment. *See Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, Inc.*, 477 U.S. 242, 255 (1986). The district court therefore erred in disregarding the witness’s testimony and in granting summary judgment on that basis.

**Conclusion**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the district court should be vacated insofar as it granted Verizon’s motion for summary judgment on Moll’s hostile work environment claim, and the case should be remanded for further proceedings consistent with this Court’s decision.
