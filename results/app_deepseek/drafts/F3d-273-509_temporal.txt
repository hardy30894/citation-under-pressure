**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Release Does Not Bar Appellant’s Claims Because It Only Covers Claims Arising from the Decision to Close the Bank, Not from the FDIC’s Subsequent Conduct as Receiver.**

The release executed by Bancorp on June 6, 1988, expressly covers claims “arising from or with respect to the decision of the [OCC] to close the Bank on June 21, 1985.” Appellant’s four claims—unjust enrichment for post-insolvency interest, breach of fiduciary duty in selling assets below value, corporate waste in charging expenses, and inadequate accounting—all challenge the FDIC’s independent actions as receiver after the closure. None of these claims arises from the OCC’s decision to close the bank; they arise from the FDIC’s own conduct in administering the receivership.

A release must be construed according to the intent of the parties, and ambiguous language is strictly construed against the drafter. *Mellon v. Mississippi Wire Glass Co.*, 77 F.2d 266, 268 (2d Cir. 1935) (release of claims “arising out of” a specific contract does not cover claims based on separate transactions). The FDIC drafted the release; any ambiguity must be resolved against it. *Id.* Moreover, a release cannot bar claims that did not exist at the time of its execution. *United States v. William Cramp & Sons Ship & Engine Building Co.*, 206 U.S. 118, 128 (1907) (release of past breaches does not waive future breaches). The FDIC’s challenged actions—including its first post-insolvency interest payment on March 15, 1991—occurred years after the 1988 release. The release’s narrow language cannot be stretched to encompass later, independent misconduct. *Callen v. Pennsylvania R. Co.*, 332 U.S. 625, 630 (1948) (release of personal injuries does not bar claims for later injuries not within the contemplation of the parties). Accordingly, the release does not bar appellant’s claims.

**II. The Statute of Limitations Did Not Begin to Run Until the Termination of the Receivership on November 1, 1995, Because the FDIC Acted as a Fiduciary and Its Duty to Account Continued Until That Date.**

Appellant’s claims for unjust enrichment, breach of fiduciary duty, and corporate waste arise from the FDIC’s role as receiver—a fiduciary. Under New York law, a cause of action for breach of fiduciary duty accrues when the fiduciary repudiates the trust or when the beneficiary has actual knowledge of the breach. *Matter of Estate of Bache*, 246 N.Y. 83, 87 (1927) (statute of limitations for breach of trust runs from the time the trustee “openly repudiates” the trust). A receiver, like a trustee, owes a continuing duty to account to the beneficiaries until the receivership is terminated. *Jackson v. Smith*, 254 U.S. 586, 589 (1921) (receiver must account for all assets and cannot be discharged until final accounting). Until the FDIC rendered its final accounting and terminated the receivership on November 1, 1995, appellant could not know the full extent of the FDIC’s alleged misconduct. The FDIC’s self-dealing—paying itself interest, charging expenses—was not a clear repudiation of its fiduciary duty; rather, it was a series of transactions that only became actionable upon the final accounting. *Pepper v. Litton*, 308 U.S. 295, 306 (1939) (fiduciary’s self-dealing is subject to close scrutiny and may be challenged after the relationship ends). Accordingly, the six-year statute of limitations under N.Y. C.P.L.R. § 213(7) did not begin to run until the receivership terminated. Appellant filed this action on October 31, 1995, one day before termination, which is timely. Even if some claims accrued earlier, the FDIC’s continuing duty to account tolled the statute until the receivership ended. *Matter of Estate of Loomis*, 273 N.Y. 76, 81 (1937) (statute of limitations does not run against a beneficiary while the fiduciary relationship continues and the fiduciary has not repudiated the trust). Therefore, the district court erred in granting summary judgment on statute of limitations grounds.

**Conclusion**

The judgment should be reversed.
