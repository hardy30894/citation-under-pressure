The federal courts possess exclusive jurisdiction over Vernon Minton's state-based legal malpractice claim because it necessarily involves a substantial question of federal patent law. The Supreme Court has long recognized that federal jurisdiction extends to cases where a well-pleaded complaint establishes that the plaintiff's right to relief depends on the resolution of a substantial federal question. As early as Osborn v. Bank of the United States, 22 U.S. 738 (1824), the Court held that federal jurisdiction is proper when a case involves a federal question, even if it is not the sole issue in the case. Id. at 822.

In this case, Minton's malpractice claim against his attorneys turns on the experimental use exception to the on-sale bar, a critical component of federal patent law. The validity of Minton's patent, and thus his malpractice claim, depends on the resolution of this federal issue. The Court has consistently held that cases "arising under" federal law involve a dispute or controversy respecting the validity, construction, or effect of a federal law. Shulthis v. McDougal, 225 U.S. 561, 569 (1912). Minton's claim meets this standard, as the outcome depends on the interpretation of federal patent law.

The Court has also emphasized the importance of uniformity in the application of federal patent law. In Wheaton v. Peters, 33 U.S. 591 (1834), the Court noted that the federal patent laws are intended to provide a uniform system of patent regulation. Id. at 658. Allowing state courts to adjudicate patent issues in the context of malpractice claims could undermine this uniformity, as it would permit multiple interpretations of federal patent law.

Furthermore, the Court has recognized that federal jurisdiction is exclusive in cases involving patent law. In Pratt v. Paris Gas Light & Coke Co., 168 U.S. 255 (1897), the Court held that the federal courts have exclusive jurisdiction over cases involving the validity and infringement of patents. Id. at 259. This principle is consistent with the idea that federal law is supreme in matters involving patent law, as seen in Bloomer v. McQuewan, 55 U.S. 539 (1852), and Wilson v. Sandford, 51 U.S. 99 (1850).

In Gully v. First Nat'l Bank in Meridian, 299 U.S. 109 (1936), the Court cautioned against defining "arising under" jurisdiction too broadly, instead adopting a "selective process which picks the substantial causes out of the web and lays the other ones aside." Id. at 117-18. Applying this principle, it is clear that Minton's claim involves a substantial question of federal patent law that is necessary to the resolution of his state-based malpractice claim.

In light of these precedents, it is evident that Minton's malpractice claim falls within the exclusive jurisdiction of the federal courts. The experimental use exception is a critical aspect of federal patent law, and its interpretation is essential to resolving Minton's claim. Therefore, the federal courts have exclusive jurisdiction over this case, and the state court lacks subject-matter jurisdiction to consider Minton's appeal.
