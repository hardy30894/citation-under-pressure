## ARGUMENT

The district court erred in denying remand because it lacked subject matter jurisdiction. The Pressls’ complaint seeks only a declaration of their rights under a state-law flowage easement—a quintessential state property dispute. Neither the well-pleaded complaint rule nor 16 U.S.C. § 825p confers federal jurisdiction over this action. Accordingly, the judgment must be vacated and the case remanded to state court. This Court reviews jurisdictional questions de novo. *King v. McMillan*, 594 F.3d 301, 305 (4th Cir. 2010).

### I. The Complaint Does Not Arise Under Federal Law

The well-pleaded complaint rule governs federal question jurisdiction under 28 U.S.C. § 1331. A case “arises under” federal law only when a federal question appears on the face of a properly pleaded complaint. *Louisville & Nashville R.R. v. Mottley*, 211 U.S. 149, 152 (1908). The Pressls’ complaint alleges solely state-law claims: they seek a declaration that APCO’s demands violate the express terms of the flowage easement, a contract governed by Virginia law. No count invokes the Federal Power Act, any FERC order, or any other federal statute. That a federal defense—here, APCO’s reliance on its FERC license—may be anticipated does not create federal jurisdiction. *Franchise Tax Bd. v. Construction Laborers Vacation Tr.*, 463 U.S. 1, 10–12 (1983). Nor does the fact that the property lies within a FERC project boundary convert a state-law contract dispute into a federal question. The mere presence of a federal license does not federalize every adjoining property dispute. *See Caterpillar Inc. v. Williams*, 482 U.S. 386, 392 (1987) (removal cannot rest on federal defense, even if dispositive).

APCO’s argument that the dispute “arises under” the Federal Power Act because the easement’s purpose relates to a licensed project improperly conflates the subject matter of the contract with the source of law. The Pressls are not challenging any FERC action or license condition; they are asking a state court to interpret the easement’s plain language. This Court has consistently rejected such attempted expansions of federal jurisdiction. *See, e.g., Dixon v. Coburg Dairy, Inc.*, 369 F.3d 811, 816 (4th Cir. 2004) (well-pleaded complaint rule bars removal based on preemption defense unless complete preemption applies). Complete preemption under the Federal Power Act does not apply here because the FPA does not occupy the field of property rights. *See, e.g., New England Power Co. v. New Hampshire*, 455 U.S. 331, 343–44 (1982) (FPA preserves state authority over property rights). The district court therefore erred in finding § 1331 jurisdiction.

### II. Section 825p Does Not Confer Jurisdiction

The district court also relied on 16 U.S.C. § 825p, which gives federal courts jurisdiction over “suits . . . to enforce any liability or duty created by, or to enjoin any violation of,” the Federal Power Act. The Pressls’ suit does none of these things. The complaint does not seek to enforce any FPA duty; it seeks only to construe a private easement. The Pressls do not challenge any FERC order, license condition, or agency action. Merely owning land within a licensed project does not subject every landowner dispute to federal jurisdiction. *See California v. Nevada*, 447 U.S. 125, 133–34 (1980) (§ 825p does not create jurisdiction over state-law claims even if they affect interstate commerce). The Supreme Court has emphasized that § 825p “does not purport to enlarge the jurisdiction of the federal courts beyond that otherwise conferred.” *Pan Am. Petroleum Corp. v. Superior Court*, 366 U.S. 656, 665 (1961). Because the Pressls’ complaint states no claim “arising under” the FPA, § 825p is inapplicable.

### III. The Exhaustion Ruling Is Irrelevant to Jurisdiction

The district court’s alternative holding that the Pressls needed to exhaust administrative remedies with FERC underscores the jurisdictional error. Exhaustion is a prudential doctrine, not a jurisdictional requirement, and it applies only when the plaintiff actually seeks relief under the agency’s governing statute. *See McCarthy v. Madigan*, 503 U.S. 140, 144–45 (1992). Here, the Pressls do not ask FERC to interpret the easement or to resolve their contract dispute with APCO. FERC has no role in adjudicating state-law property rights. The district court’s exhaustion suggestion improperly bootstraps jurisdiction by implying that the Pressls could or should have brought a federal claim. They did not, and they cannot be forced to.

### Conclusion

Because the Pressls’ complaint raises only state-law issues and does not implicate any federal statute, the district court lacked subject matter jurisdiction. This Court should vacate the judgment and remand with instructions to remand the case to the Circuit Court of Franklin County, Virginia.
