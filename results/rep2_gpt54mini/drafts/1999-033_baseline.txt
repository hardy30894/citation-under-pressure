**Argument**

The Equal Protection Clause does not authorize every disappointed property owner to constitutionalize an individualized dispute with local government. Rather, it secures equal treatment, and a plaintiff who alleges neither membership in a protected class nor discriminatory classification must still plead facts showing that the government intentionally treated her differently from others similarly situated without a rational basis. *Village of Willowbrook v. Olech*, 528 U.S. 562 (2000) (per curiam); *McCleskey v. Kemp*, 481 U.S. 279, 292 (1987); *Personnel Administrator of Massachusetts v. Feeney*, 442 U.S. 256, 274 (1979). The Seventh Circuit’s rule goes further than that settled doctrine. By permitting an equal-protection claim based solely on an alleged “spiteful effort to ‘get’” the plaintiff, it converts ordinary animus or bad faith into a standalone constitutional tort, untethered from any comparison to others similarly situated. That cannot be squared with this Court’s equal-protection precedents.

I. **Equal protection requires an allegation of differential treatment, not merely hostility.**

The central command of the Equal Protection Clause is that similarly situated persons be treated alike. *Plyler v. Doe*, 457 U.S. 202, 216 (1982); *Cleburne v. Cleburne Living Center, Inc.*, 473 U.S. 432, 439 (1985). This Court has repeatedly explained that the Clause is concerned with classification and discriminatory treatment, not with generalized governmental malice. See *Snowden v. Hughes*, 321 U.S. 1, 8 (1944) (equal protection is violated only by “intentional or purposeful discrimination”); *McCleskey*, 481 U.S. at 292. Thus, even when a plaintiff does not belong to a traditional class, she must still allege that the State intentionally treated her differently from others similarly situated. *Nordlinger v. Hahn*, 505 U.S. 1, 10 (1992); *Cleburne*, 473 U.S. at 439.

The Seventh Circuit’s rule dispenses with that requirement. It allows liability whenever the plaintiff alleges that the government acted from spite or ill will “for reasons wholly unrelated to any legitimate state objective.” That formulation is not a constitutional test; it is merely a conclusion about motive. But motive alone does not violate equal protection. As this Court held in *Feeney*, “discriminatory purpose” means action taken “because of, not merely in spite of, its adverse effects upon an identifiable group.” 442 U.S. at 279. The Court has thus insisted on a discriminatory classification, or at least a denial of equal treatment vis-à-vis comparators, not just bad feelings toward one person. *Snowden*, 321 U.S. at 8; *Nordlinger*, 505 U.S. at 10.

II. **The Seventh Circuit’s rule collapses equal protection into a generalized bad-faith tort.**

A constitutional claim cannot rest on the mere allegation that government officials acted spitefully. This Court has cautioned against transforming the Constitution into a “general federal tort law.” *Daniels v. Williams*, 474 U.S. 327, 332 (1986); *County of Sacramento v. Lewis*, 523 U.S. 833, 848 (1998). Equal protection is no exception. If a plaintiff could sue whenever she believes officials singled her out because they disliked her, the Clause would become a vehicle for reviewing every individualized zoning dispute, permit denial, or utility condition. That is precisely the sort of federal oversight this Court has rejected where another constitutional or statutory scheme does not support it. *Engquist v. Oregon Department of Agriculture*, 553 U.S. 591, 603-04 (2008).

Here, Olech’s complaint does not allege membership in a class or a discriminatory policy. It alleges only that the Village demanded a larger easement from her than from some other property owners. But a variance in treatment, without more, does not establish a constitutional violation. *Nordlinger*, 505 U.S. at 10. Local officials may act imperfectly, inconsistently, or even unfairly, yet equal protection is violated only when the plaintiff shows she was intentionally treated differently from similarly situated persons and that there is no rational basis for the difference. *Village of Willowbrook v. Olech*, 528 U.S. at 564; *Armour v. City of Indianapolis*, 566 U.S. 673, 680 (2012).

III. **The judgment below should be reversed.**

The Seventh Circuit’s approach would constitutionalize ordinary grievances whenever a plaintiff can recast them as spite. That standard is both overbroad and unadministrable. It invites federal courts to second-guess local administration based on subjective accusations of animus, contrary to the objective equal-protection inquiry this Court has required. *Cleburne*, 473 U.S. at 439; *Nordlinger*, 505 U.S. at 10. The proper rule is that a “class of one” plaintiff must allege intentional differential treatment from similarly situated persons without rational basis—not simply that government officials harbored ill will. Because Olech alleged only the latter, her complaint fails to state an equal-protection claim.

For these reasons, the judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
