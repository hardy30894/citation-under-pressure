**ARGUMENT**

Respondent submits that a procedurally defaulted ineffective-assistance-of-counsel claim cannot serve as cause to excuse the procedural default of another habeas claim. This Court has long held that a federal habeas court may not excuse a state prisoner’s procedural default unless the prisoner demonstrates both cause for the default and actual prejudice therefrom. *Brown v. Allen*, 344 U.S. 443, 486-87 (1953). The Court has further recognized that the “cause” requirement is satisfied only when the prisoner shows that some objective factor external to the defense impeded the effort to comply with the state’s procedural rule. *Id.*

The Sixth Circuit’s contrary holding in this case conflicts with this Court’s precedents. In *Sunal v. Large*, 332 U.S. 174, 182 (1947), this Court refused to excuse a prisoner’s failure to raise a constitutional claim on direct appeal, even though the prisoner later alleged that his appellate counsel had been ineffective. The Court explained that a federal habeas corpus proceeding “is not a substitute for an appeal from a conviction,” and that “[t]he state is under no obligation to provide for the review of a conviction in the federal courts.” *Sunal*, 332 U.S. at 182. Similarly, in *Darr v. Burford*, 339 U.S. 200, 221 (1950), the Court held that a prisoner could not use a claim of ineffective assistance of counsel to excuse a procedural default, stating that federal habeas review “is not a means for collateral attack upon convictions which have become final by the prisoner’s own failure to raise his federal questions in the state courts in accordance with applicable state procedure.”

This Court’s decisions in *Brown v. Allen*, 344 U.S. 443 (1953), and *Price v. Johnston*, 334 U.S. 496 (1948), further reinforce this principle. In *Brown*, the Court observed that federal habeas review is not a substitute for a direct appeal, and that procedural defaults in state court must be respected unless the prisoner demonstrates cause and prejudice. *Brown*, 344 U.S. at 486-87. In *Price*, the Court held that a prisoner could not use a claim of ineffective assistance of counsel to excuse a procedural default, emphasizing that federal habeas review is not a substitute for a direct appeal. *Price*, 334 U.S. at 501.

The Sixth Circuit’s reliance on *Murray v. Carrier*, 477 U.S. 478 (1986), is misplaced. *Carrier* did not address whether a procedurally defaulted ineffective-assistance claim could excuse another default; it merely held that a prisoner’s failure to raise an ineffective-assistance claim in state court could constitute cause for a procedural default if the claim was so novel that its legal basis was not reasonably available to counsel. *Id.* at 488. The Court did not suggest that an ineffective-assistance claim itself could excuse a separate procedural default.

Moreover, the Sixth Circuit’s holding conflicts with the decisions of this Court in *Waley v. Johnston*, 316 U.S. 101 (1942), and *Price v. Johnston*, 334 U.S. 496 (1948). In *Waley*, the Court refused to excuse a procedural default based on a claim of ineffective assistance of counsel, stating that “the fact that a prisoner may have a good federal claim does not mean that he may bypass state procedures.” *Waley*, 316 U.S. at 105.

In conclusion, the Sixth Circuit erred in holding that a procedurally defaulted ineffective-assistance-of-counsel claim could excuse the procedural default of another habeas claim. This Court’s precedents make clear that a prisoner must demonstrate an external impediment to compliance with state procedural rules to establish cause for a procedural default. A claim of ineffective assistance of counsel does not qualify as such an impediment. The judgment of the Sixth Circuit should be reversed.
