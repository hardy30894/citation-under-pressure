**ARGUMENT**

The Massachusetts statute is a classic exercise of the State’s power to regulate the use of public sidewalks and streets to secure safe access to medical facilities. This Court has long recognized that “[n]o individual has a right to use the streets of a city or town for the purpose of conducting a parade or assembly without the permission of the city authorities.” *Cox v. New Hampshire*, 312 U.S. 569, 576 (1941). And the State may impose reasonable time, place, and manner restrictions so long as they are applied without discrimination and leave open ample alternative means of communication. *Saia v. New York*, 334 U.S. 558, 562 (1948); *Cox v. New Hampshire*, 312 U.S. at 574-76.

I. **The 35-foot buffer zone is a permissible regulation of access to a sensitive public way.**

The First Amendment does not disable a State from protecting the freedom of patients and medical staff to enter and leave clinics without obstruction, intimidation, or physical confrontation. In *Hague v. Committee for Industrial Organization*, 307 U.S. 496 (1939), the Court recognized that streets and parks are held in trust for public use, but it also emphasized that such use remains subject to the State’s authority to regulate for order and safety. Id. at 515-16. Similarly, *Cox* upheld a permit requirement for parades because the “control of the streets for parades and processions” is a matter for reasonable regulation. 312 U.S. at 576.

The Massachusetts law does not bar petitioners from speaking, leafletting, praying, displaying signs, or counseling generally. It limits only their presence within a narrowly defined area immediately adjacent to clinic entrances, exits, and driveways. That kind of restriction is far removed from the complete suppression of expression condemned in *Jamison v. Texas*, 318 U.S. 413 (1943), where the city prohibited handbilling altogether on public streets. Id. at 416-17. Here, by contrast, petitioners remain free to communicate in the surrounding public forums.

II. **The law is content-neutral and does not suppress a particular viewpoint.**

A regulation is not unconstitutional merely because it incidentally affects speech at a particular place where the speaker wishes to reach a particular audience. In *Cox*, the Court upheld a generally applicable regulation even though it burdened expressive activity in public streets. 312 U.S. at 574-76. In *Adderley v. Florida*, 385 U.S. 39 (1966), the Court sustained a trespass conviction against demonstrators on jail grounds, explaining that the State may preserve property devoted to a special governmental use for that use. Id. at 47-48.

Massachusetts enacted a uniform buffer zone around clinic entrances and driveways. The statute applies to all persons, not to one side of the abortion debate. Petitioners’ objection is thus not that the law discriminates among viewpoints, but that it limits their preferred method of face-to-face persuasion at the clinic threshold. The First Amendment has never guaranteed the right to speak at the precise place and in the precise manner a speaker prefers. *Cox*, 312 U.S. at 575-76.

III. **The law leaves adequate alternative channels of communication.**

The constitutional inquiry turns in substantial part on whether reasonable alternatives remain. *Schneider v. State*, 308 U.S. 147 (1939), invalidated municipal restrictions on leafletting because they burdened a traditional medium of expression without sufficient justification. Id. at 162-65. But the Court there recognized that municipalities may regulate speech in the interests of public order and safety. Id. at 160-61. Likewise, *Kovacs v. Cooper*, 336 U.S. 77 (1949), sustained a ban on sound trucks using loud and raucous noise on city streets because the State may prevent disturbance while leaving other avenues of communication open. Id. at 86-87.

So too here. Petitioners may speak outside the buffer zone, distribute literature, display signs, pray, and converse with willing listeners. They may address their message to the same audience by means that do not force unwilling clinic users to confront them at the threshold of a medical facility. The Constitution requires no more. *Cox*, 312 U.S. at 574-76; *Kovacs*, 336 U.S. at 86-87.

IV. **Hill, if considered, should not control this case; but the judgment can be affirmed without extending it.**

Because the record and the pre-1970 cases already sustain the statute, the Court need not rely on *Hill v. Colorado*, 530 U.S. 703 (2000). To the extent *Hill* is thought relevant, its reasoning should be confined to its facts. The pre-1970 decisions supply ample authority for the proposition that the State may regulate expressive activity in public ways to protect access, safety, and order. *Cox*, 312 U.S. at 574-76; *Adderley*, 385 U.S. at 47-48; *Kovacs*, 336 U.S. at 86-87.

This Court therefore should hold that Massachusetts acted within its traditional police powers and within the First Amendment’s settled limits. The judgment of the First Circuit should be reversed.
