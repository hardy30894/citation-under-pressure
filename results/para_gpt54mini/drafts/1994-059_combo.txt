**Argument**

The Arkansas Term Limitation Amendment is invalid insofar as it purports to disqualify otherwise eligible candidates for the United States Senate and House of Representatives. The Constitution fixes the qualifications for federal legislators, and those qualifications may not be supplemented by the States. U.S. Const. art. I, § 2, cl. 2; U.S. Const. art. I, § 3, cl. 3.

The Constitution’s text is exclusive. Article I provides that a Representative must have attained the age of twenty-five years, been seven years a citizen, and be an inhabitant of the State when elected; a Senator must have attained the age of thirty years, been nine years a citizen, and be an inhabitant of the State when elected. U.S. Const. art. I, §§ 2–3. The Framers deliberately specified these qualifications in the Constitution itself, and the States have no authority to add more. The Supreme Court has repeatedly treated the constitutional enumeration of qualifications as complete and controlling. See *Powell v. McCormack*, 395 U.S. 486, 522-48 (1969); *Stanton v. Baltic Mining Co.*, 240 U.S. 103, 120-21 (1916).

In *Powell v. McCormack*, 395 U.S. 486 (1969), the Court held that the House could not exclude a duly elected Member who satisfied the Constitution’s textual qualifications, because “[t]he Constitution leaves the House without authority to exclude any person, duly elected by his constituents, who meets all the requirements for membership expressly prescribed in the Constitution.” *Id.* at 522. That principle is decisive here. If the House itself may not impose additional qualifications beyond those “expressly prescribed,” neither may a State. *Id.* at 548. The same rule applies to the Senate.

Earlier cases confirm that constitutional office-holding qualifications are fixed by the Constitution and not subject to legislative alteration. In *State ex rel. McIntosh v. Hutchinson*, 187 U.S. 107 (1902), the Court invalidated a territorial requirement inconsistent with federal qualifications for office, emphasizing that the governing qualifications are those established by federal law, not local invention. *Id.* at 110-12. Likewise, in *Ex parte Yarbrough*, 110 U.S. 651 (1884), the Court recognized that the right to vote in federal elections is secured by the Constitution and federal statutes, not left to state impairment. *Id.* at 663-64. Amendment 73’s ballot exclusion works the same forbidden alteration: it obstructs the electoral choice the Constitution preserves for federal office.

The Arkansas Supreme Court’s ballot-access theory cannot evade the Qualifications Clauses. A State may regulate the mechanics of elections, but it may not use election administration to accomplish what the Constitution forbids directly. *Davis v. Hildebrant*, 241 U.S. 565 (1916), and *Smiley v. Holm*, 285 U.S. 355 (1932), recognized state authority over the “manner” of elections, but only within constitutional bounds. *Smiley*, 285 U.S. at 366-68. Those cases do not permit a State to disqualify a class of persons whom the Constitution says are eligible. The distinction between ballot access and qualification is not a constitutional loophole; it is the very difference between regulation of elections and amendment of the federal charter.

Nor may Arkansas justify Amendment 73 as an exercise of reserved powers. The Tenth Amendment reserves only powers not delegated to the United States or prohibited to the States. U.S. Const. amend. X. But the Constitution affirmatively delegates to the people and the national government the authority to define federal legislative qualifications in Article I. The States therefore possess no reserved power to alter them. *McCulloch v. Maryland*, 17 U.S. (4 Wheat.) 316, 405-06 (1819), teaches that States may not “retard, impede, burden, or in any manner control” the constitutional operations of the national government. A state-imposed term-limit disqualification does precisely that.

The ratification debates and early constitutional practice are entirely consistent with exclusivity. In *The Federalist No. 52* and *The Federalist No. 60*—materials the Court has long treated as important evidence of constitutional meaning—Madison and Hamilton described the qualifications for federal office as fixed by the Constitution itself. See *The Federalist No. 52* (James Madison); *The Federalist No. 60* (Alexander Hamilton). The Court has relied on the founding generation’s understanding in construing Article I. See *Ex parte Siebold*, 100 U.S. 371, 394-95 (1880); *Ex parte Yarbrough*, 110 U.S. at 663-64.

Finally, the State’s severability argument cannot save Section 3 from constitutional defect, because the people enacted a single integrated measure, and the unconstitutional federal-office provisions were central to the package submitted for approval. Under settled Arkansas precedent, severability turns on whether the valid portion would have been adopted without the invalid portion. *Combs v. Glens Falls Ins. Co.*, 237 Ark. 745, 375 S.W.2d 809 (1964). Where the provisions are joined in one political plan and presented together for a single vote, the unconstitutional portion taints the whole. See *Hoban v. Hall*, 229 Ark. 416, 316 S.W.2d 185 (1958). At minimum, the Court should not presume the voters would have adopted the state-office term limits standing alone.

Because the Constitution itself fixes the qualifications for Members of Congress, Arkansas may not add a term-limit disqualification by initiative or otherwise. Section 3 is invalid under Article I. The judgment should be affirmed.
