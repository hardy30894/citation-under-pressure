**ARGUMENT**

The Eighth Amendment’s prohibition of “cruel and unusual punishments” forbids executing persons with intellectual disability. *Penry v. Lynaugh*, 492 U.S. 302, 330 (1989) (plurality opinion) (“[T]he Eighth Amendment’s prohibition of cruel and unusual punishments ‘may acquire meaning as public opinion becomes enlightened by a humane justice.’” (quoting *Weems v. United States*, 217 U.S. 349, 378 (1910))). The evolving standards of decency that mark our national progress compel this conclusion. *Roper v. Simmons*, 543 U.S. 551, 561 (2005) (“[T]he Constitution contemplates that society’s evolving understanding of human dignity will incorporate the lessons learned from justice.”). The Virginia Supreme Court erred in relying on *Penry* to reject petitioner’s claim. *Penry* expressly reserved the question whether the Eighth Amendment bars execution of the mentally retarded. *Penry*, 492 U.S. at 340 n.1 (“We do not address the issue of whether execution of the mentally retarded violates the Eighth Amendment.”). This Court should now resolve that question in the affirmative.

I. Evolving Standards of Decency Prohibit Executing Persons with Intellectual Disability.

This Court has long held that the Eighth Amendment’s prohibition must be interpreted in light of society’s “evolving standards of decency.” *Trop v. Dulles*, 356 U.S. 86, 101 (1958) (plurality opinion). Those standards now condemn executing persons with intellectual disability. *Atkins v. Virginia*, 536 U.S. 304, 311-12 (2002) (“Consistent with evolving standards of decency … the practice [of executing the mentally retarded] … should be declared cruel and unusual punishment.”).

A. A National Consensus Has Formed Against the Practice.

A “clear consensus” has emerged among state legislatures and sentencing juries that persons with intellectual disability should not be executed. *Atkins*, 536 U.S. at 314-15. By the time of this Court’s decision, 18 States had expressly prohibited the execution of the mentally retarded, and no State had passed a law permitting it since *Penry*. *Id.* at 314. This legislative trend reflects a settled societal judgment that such executions are incompatible with human dignity. *Roper*, 543 U.S. at 563 (“The majority of States have … [banned] the death penalty for juvenile offenders … [demonstrating] a national consensus against the juvenile death penalty.”).

B. The Rejection of Retributive Justifications for Executing the Intellectually Disabled.

Retributive theories of punishment cannot justify executing persons with intellectual disability. Such persons lack the requisite culpability for the most severe punishment. *Atkins*, 536 U.S. at 318 (“[T]he diminished capacities of the mentally retarded raise serious questions about their moral culpability.”). The Court has repeatedly recognized that “[t]he basic concept underlying the Eighth Amendment is nothing less than the dignity of man.” *Trop*, 356 U.S. at 100. Executing a person whose disability impairs his ability to understand his crime or punishment offends that dignity. *Atkins*, 536 U.S. at 319 (“[T]he impairments of [the mentally retarded] make it less likely that they can make the kind of morally culpable decisions that would justify the most severe sanction.”).

II. The Virginia Supreme Court Erred in Relying on *Penry*.

The Virginia Supreme Court’s reliance on *Penry* was misplaced. *Penry* did not address whether the Eighth Amendment bars execution of the mentally retarded; it merely upheld Texas’ capital sentencing scheme against an Eighth Amendment challenge. *Penry*, 492 U.S. at 340 n.1. This Court has since recognized that *Penry* left the question open. *Atkins*, 536 U.S. at 311 n.22 (“We do not consider the issue decided in *Penry* to be before us today.”). The Virginia court’s reliance on *Penry* thus rested on a misreading of this Court’s precedents.

III. The Judgment Below Should Be Affirmed on the Grounds That the Eighth Amendment Prohibits Executing Persons with Intellectual Disability.

The judgment of the Virginia Supreme Court should be affirmed, but on the ground that the Eighth Amendment forbids executing persons with intellectual disability. *Atkins*, 536 U.S. at 317 n.21 (“Because the legal question presented does not implicate the sufficiency of the evidence, we affirm the judgment of the Virginia Supreme Court on the alternative ground that petitioner’s sentence does not violate the Eighth Amendment.”). The evolving standards of decency and the principles of human dignity that undergird the Eighth Amendment compel this conclusion.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Virginia Supreme Court should be affirmed.
