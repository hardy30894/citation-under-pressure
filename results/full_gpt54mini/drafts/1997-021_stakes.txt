The Board’s polling standard cannot stand. Under the NLRA, an incumbent union enjoys only a rebuttable presumption of majority support, and an employer may act on “objective indications sufficient to raise a reasonable doubt” about that support. Fall River Dyeing & Finishing Corp. v. NLRB, 482 U.S. 27, 37-41, 107 S. Ct. 2225, 2232-35 (1987); NLRB v. Curtin Matheson Scientific, Inc., 494 U.S. 775, 778, 110 S. Ct. 1542, 1544-45 (1990). The question here is whether the Board may allow an employer to test that doubt by polling only when the employer already has enough evidence to justify withdrawal of recognition. It may not. That standard makes polling functionally useless and is inconsistent with the Act’s goal of reliable employee free choice.

This Court need not decide the issue from first principles. The Fifth, Sixth, and Ninth Circuits have held that an employer may poll employees when it has “substantial, objective evidence of a loss of union support,” even if that evidence is insufficient by itself to justify withdrawal of recognition. A.W. Thompson, Inc. v. NLRB, 651 F.2d 1141, 1145 (5th Cir. 1981); Thomas Indus., Inc. v. NLRB, 687 F.2d 863, 867-68 (6th Cir. 1982); Mingtree Rest., Inc. v. NLRB, 736 F.2d 1295, 1299 (9th Cir. 1984). Those courts correctly recognized that polling and withdrawal are distinct acts. A poll is an evidentiary tool to determine whether a union still enjoys support; withdrawal is the ultimate substantive action. Id.; Thomas Indus., 687 F.2d at 867. The Board’s rule collapses that distinction and turns polling into a dead letter. As the Ninth Circuit explained, if an employer must already possess enough evidence to withdraw recognition, there is no practical reason to poll at all. Mingtree Rest., 736 F.2d at 1297-99.

The Board’s contrary policy judgment is not compelled by the Act. Nothing in the NLRA specifically governs employee polling on union support. Allentown Mack Sales & Serv., Inc. v. NLRB, 118 F.3d 833, 840-41 (D.C. Cir. 1997). The Board may of course regulate polling to prevent coercion, but it must do so in a way that is rational and faithful to the statutory presumption it administers. Curtin Matheson, 494 U.S. at 786. Here, the Board’s rule is internally inconsistent. It says RM elections are the preferred means of testing support, yet it imposes on polling the same evidentiary threshold it uses for withdrawal of recognition, while polls lack the safeguards of Board-supervised elections. Allentown Mack Sales & Serv., 118 F.3d at 841. That mismatch is arbitrary.

Even if the Board’s standard were valid, its finding that Allentown Mack lacked a good-faith reasonable doubt is unsupported by substantial evidence. The record showed multiple employee statements indicating opposition to the union. The Board discounted evidence from employees who said they did not want the union, or that the union had lost support, and it improperly refused to credit statements from employees such as Bloch and Mohr. The Board may not ignore probative evidence merely because it is not perfectly quantified. In Thomas Industries, the Sixth Circuit rejected the Board’s contrary conclusion where roughly one-fourth of the employees had made negative statements and held there was no substantial evidence supporting the Board’s finding that the employer lacked a good-faith doubt. Thomas Indus., 687 F.2d at 868. The same is true here.

The Board’s treatment of employee statements was especially flawed. Statements made in job interviews after the employer had indicated the new company would be non-union were entitled to limited weight, see Middleboro Fire Apparatus, Inc., 234 N.L.R.B. 888, 894 (1978), enforced, 590 F.2d 4, 9 (1st Cir. 1978), but the Board could not dismiss all of the antiunion evidence on that basis. Nor could it treat complaints about union dues and representation as irrelevant if they reasonably reflected loss of support. Compare Destileria Serralles, Inc., 289 N.L.R.B. 51 (1988), enforced, 882 F.2d 19 (1st Cir. 1989), with Wagon Wheel Bowl, Inc. v. NLRB, 47 F.3d 332, 335-36 (9th Cir. 1995). Most importantly, the Board’s conclusion that only seven employees out of thirty-two expressed opposition disregarded the realities of a recently sold successor operation, in which the bargaining unit had never been separately tested. Fall River, 482 U.S. at 36-41.

Because the Board’s polling standard is irrational and because its no-doubt finding lacks substantial evidence, the order should not be enforced. The Court should set aside the Board’s decision and deny enforcement.
