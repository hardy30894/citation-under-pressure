**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE CONTROLLED SUBSTANCES ACT’S TEMPORARY SCHEDULING PROVISION DOES NOT UNCONSTITUTIONALLY DELEGATE LEGISLATIVE POWER TO THE ATTORNEY GENERAL.**

The nondelegation doctrine bars only those delegations that lack an “intelligible principle” to guide the delegate’s exercise of authority. *Mistretta v. United States*, 488 U.S. 361, 372 (1989) (quoting *J.W. Hampton, Jr., & Co. v. United States*, 276 U.S. 394, 409 (1928)). The temporary scheduling provision of 21 U.S.C. § 811(h) satisfies this standard.

Congress specified the Attorney General’s task: to temporarily schedule a substance that poses “an imminent hazard to the public safety.” 21 U.S.C. § 811(h)(1). It further directed that the Attorney General consider three factors—(1) the substance’s history and current pattern of abuse, (2) the scope, duration, and significance of such abuse, and (3) the risk to public health—before acting. *Id.* § 811(h)(3). These factors are “sufficiently definite” to channel the Attorney General’s discretion. *Mistretta*, 488 U.S. at 373.

The Court has repeatedly upheld delegations where Congress provided similarly broad but discernible standards. *See, e.g., Yakus v. United States*, 321 U.S. 414, 424-26 (1944) (upholding delegation to fix prices based on “fair and equitable” standards); *FPC v. Hope Natural Gas Co.*, 320 U.S. 591, 602 (1944) (upholding delegation to set “just and reasonable” rates). The temporary scheduling provision is no different. It “lay[s] down an intelligible principle” to guide the Attorney General’s discretion, *Mistretta*, 488 U.S. at 372, and thus does not violate the nondelegation doctrine.

The Toubys’ reliance on *Panama Refining Co. v. Ryan*, 293 U.S. 388 (1935), and *A.L.A. Schechter Poultry Corp. v. United States*, 295 U.S. 495 (1935), is misplaced. Those cases involved delegations that lacked any standards whatsoever. *Panama Refining*, 293 U.S. at 415 (finding no “criterion to govern the President’s course”); *Schechter Poultry*, 295 U.S. at 542-43 (finding no “standards prescribed by the statute”). Here, by contrast, Congress provided explicit criteria to govern the Attorney General’s decision. The temporary scheduling provision is therefore constitutional.

**II. THE ATTORNEY GENERAL LAWFULLY DELEGATED HIS TEMPORARY SCHEDULING AUTHORITY TO THE DEA ADMINISTRATOR.**

The Attorney General’s subdelegation of authority to the DEA Administrator is authorized by 21 U.S.C. § 871(a), which permits the Attorney General to “delegate any of his functions under [the CSA] to any officer or employee of the Department of Justice.” The DEA Administrator is such an officer. *See* 28 C.F.R. § 0.100(b) (1989) (subdelegating CSA functions to the DEA Administrator).

The Supreme Court has long recognized that executive officials may subdelegate authority where the original delegation is valid. *See United States v. Grimaud*, 220 U.S. 506, 517 (1911) (upholding subdelegation of rulemaking authority under the Forest Reserve Act). The temporary scheduling provision is no exception. The Attorney General’s subdelegation to the DEA Administrator is lawful.

**III. THE STATUTORY BAR ON JUDICIAL REVIEW OF TEMPORARY SCHEDULING ORDERS DOES NOT PRECLUDE CONSTITUTIONAL REVIEW.**

Section 811(h)(6) provides that a temporary scheduling order “is not subject to judicial review.” 21 U.S.C. § 811(h)(6). This provision does not bar constitutional challenges to the delegation itself. *See Califano v. Sanders*, 430 U.S. 99, 109 (1977) (holding that statutory bars to review do not apply to constitutional claims unless Congress’s intent is “clear and convincing”).

Every court to consider the issue has concluded that § 811(h)(6) does not foreclose constitutional review. *See United States v. Emerson*, 846 F.2d 541, 544 (9th Cir. 1988); *United States v. Spain*, 825 F.2d 1426, 1429 (10th Cir. 1987); *United States v. Hovey*, 674 F. Supp. 161, 164 (D. Del. 1987). The Third Circuit correctly followed this consensus. *Touby v. United States*, 909 F.2d 717, 723 (3d Cir. 1990). The statute’s bar on review does not apply to the Toubys’ nondelegation challenge.

**CONCLUSION**

The Controlled Substances Act’s temporary scheduling provision does not unconstitutionally delegate legislative power to the Attorney General, and the Attorney General lawfully delegated his authority to the DEA Administrator. The judgment below should be affirmed.
