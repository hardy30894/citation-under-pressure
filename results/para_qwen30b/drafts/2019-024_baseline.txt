**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether the three-year statute of limitations in ERISA bars a suit where defendants disclosed all relevant information but the plaintiff chose not to read or could not recall having read it—requires this Court to interpret the meaning of “actual knowledge” under 29 U.S.C. § 1113(2), which provides that an action under § 1104 may not be commenced more than “three years after the earliest date on which the plaintiff had actual knowledge of the breach or violation.” The Ninth Circuit’s decision, which holds that a plaintiff may avoid the statute of limitations by claiming ignorance of information he had access to, misreads the plain language of the statute and contradicts binding precedent. We respectfully urge this Court to reverse.

### I. The Ninth Circuit Misapplies the “Actual Knowledge” Standard by Requiring Knowledge of Imprudence, Not Just the Transaction

The Ninth Circuit held that “actual knowledge” under § 1113(2) requires knowledge not only of the transaction but also of its imprudence. This interpretation is contrary to the Supreme Court’s clear directive in *Tibble v. Edison Int’l*, 135 S. Ct. 1823, 1832 (2015), that the statute of limitations “begins to run when the plaintiff has actual knowledge of the breach or violation,” and that “knowledge of the breach” is not synonymous with “knowledge of the law.” The Court emphasized that “actual knowledge” is a factual inquiry, not a legal one, and that “a plaintiff need not know that a particular action violates ERISA to trigger the limitations period.” Id. at 1833.

The Ninth Circuit’s requirement that Sulyma must have known the investments were “imprudent” to trigger the statute of limitations effectively imports a legal conclusion—imprudence—into the factual inquiry of “actual knowledge.” This is impermissible. As the Supreme Court made clear in *Meagher v. Int’l Ass’n of Machinists & Aerospace Workers Pension Plan*, 856 F.2d 1418, 1423 (9th Cir. 1988), “the statute of limitations is triggered by [a claimant’s] knowledge of the transaction that constituted the alleged violation, not by [his] knowledge of the law.” The same principle applies here: if Sulyma had actual knowledge that Intel had invested in hedge funds and private equity—regardless of whether he understood the legal standard of prudence—the limitations period began to run.

The Ninth Circuit’s contrary holding creates a dangerous precedent: a plaintiff may delay filing suit indefinitely by simply refusing to read or recall disclosures, even when those disclosures are readily available and contain all material facts. This undermines the purpose of the statute of limitations, which is to “encourage prompt litigation and prevent stale claims.” *Tibble*, 135 S. Ct. at 1831. It also contradicts the well-established principle that “a party cannot sit on his rights and then complain that he did not know of them.” *Blanton v. Anzalone*, 760 F.2d 989, 991 (9th Cir. 1985). In *Blanton*, we held that trustees had actual knowledge of a transaction when they were parties to it, even if they did not know it violated ERISA. The same logic applies here: Sulyma had actual knowledge of the investment decisions when he had access to the Fund Fact Sheets, Summary Plan Descriptions, and other disclosures that detailed the allocation of assets to hedge funds and private equity.

### II. The Ninth Circuit’s “Knowledge of Imprudence” Standard Is Unsupported by ERISA’s Text and Structure

ERISA does not define “actual knowledge,” but it does define “breach or violation” in § 1104(a)(1) as a failure to act “solely in the interest of the participants and beneficiaries” and “with the care, skill, prudence, and diligence ... that a prudent man acting in a like capacity and familiar with such matters would use.” The statute of limitations, however, does not require knowledge of the legal standard of prudence. It requires only knowledge of the *breach*—that is, the act or omission that constitutes the violation.

The Ninth Circuit’s requirement that a plaintiff must know the investment was “imprudent” to trigger the limitations period effectively transforms the statute into a “knowledge of the law” standard, which Congress eliminated in 1987 when it repealed the constructive knowledge provision. See Omnibus Budget Reconciliation Act of 1987, Pub. L. No. 100-203, § 9342(b), 101 Stat. 1330. The removal of constructive knowledge was intended to prevent plaintiffs from relying on “reasonably expected” knowledge and to focus instead on actual, personal awareness. *Tibble*, 135 S. Ct. at 1832. The Ninth Circuit’s approach, by requiring knowledge of the legal standard of prudence, reintroduces a form of constructive knowledge—namely, that a plaintiff must have known the investment was legally impermissible—thereby undermining Congress’s intent.

### III. The Ninth Circuit’s Decision Creates a Dangerous Loophole That Encourages Forum Shopping and Abuse

The Ninth Circuit’s holding allows plaintiffs to avoid the statute of limitations by simply claiming they did not read or recall disclosures, even when those disclosures were provided in a clear, accessible format. This invites abuse: a plaintiff could wait years after receiving detailed information about investment allocations, then file suit on the grounds that he “did not know” the investments were imprudent. Such a rule would render the three-year limitations period meaningless, as plaintiffs could delay litigation indefinitely by refusing to engage with available information.

This Court has repeatedly rejected such “willful blindness” as a basis for tolling limitations periods. In *Tibble*, the Court emphasized that “a plaintiff cannot avoid the statute of limitations by failing to investigate.” 135 S. Ct. at 1833. The same principle applies here. If Sulyma had access to the Fund Fact Sheets and other disclosures that detailed the allocation of assets to alternative investments, and if he had the opportunity to review them, then he had actual knowledge of the transaction. Whether he chose not to read them—or could not recall having read them—does not negate that knowledge.
