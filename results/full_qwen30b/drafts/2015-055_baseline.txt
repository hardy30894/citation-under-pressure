**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether the rule announced in *Johnson v. United States*, 576 U.S. 591 (2015), applies retroactively to invalidate predicate convictions used under the Armed Career Criminal Act (ACCA)—is one of profound constitutional significance. The Court’s holding in *Johnson* declared the ACCA’s residual clause unconstitutionally vague under the Due Process Clauses of the Fifth and Fourteenth Amendments because it failed to provide fair notice of the conduct it punished. The Court emphasized that the clause “offered no clear guidance” and “left judges to guess at the meaning of ‘serious potential risk of physical injury.’” *Johnson*, 576 U.S. at 605. That same lack of clarity rendered the clause “arbitrary and unpredictable,” violating the fundamental principle that “a person of ordinary intelligence must be able to understand what conduct is prohibited.” *Id.* at 606.

Petitioner Gregory Welch was sentenced under the ACCA based on a 1996 Florida conviction for “strong arm robbery,” a conviction that, under *Johnson*, cannot stand as a predicate offense. The Florida statute under which Welch was convicted criminalized robbery by “force, violence, assault, or putting in fear.” Fla. Stat. § 812.13(1) (1996). At the time of Welch’s conviction, Florida courts were deeply divided on whether a “sudden snatching” of property—such as a purse or jewelry—constituted robbery under that statute. See *McCloud v. State*, 335 So.2d 257, 258–59 (Fla. 1976); *Goldsmith v. State*, 573 So.2d 445, 445 (Fla. 2d DCA 1991). The Florida Supreme Court had not definitively resolved the issue, and the state’s intermediate appellate courts were split. In this context, the ACCA’s residual clause—requiring courts to assess whether a crime “otherwise involves conduct that presents a serious potential risk of physical injury”—became the sole mechanism by which courts could determine whether such a conviction qualified as a “violent felony.” But as *Johnson* made clear, that clause was “so vague that it failed to give people adequate notice of the conduct it punished.” *Johnson*, 576 U.S. at 605.

The Court in *Johnson* did not limit its holding to future cases. It held that the residual clause violated due process *as applied* to the defendant before it, and that the clause’s vagueness rendered it “unconstitutional in all cases.” *Id.* at 606. The Court’s reasoning was categorical: “The Clause is unconstitutionally vague because it fails to provide fair notice of the conduct it punishes.” *Id.* at 605. This is not a rule of procedure or a new evidentiary standard; it is a constitutional rule of substantive law. As the Court has long held, “a constitutional rule of criminal procedure applies retroactively to all cases pending on direct review.” *Teague v. Lane*, 489 U.S. 288, 310 (1989). And when a constitutional rule “is a clear break with the past,” it “must be applied retroactively.” *Saffle v. Parks*, 494 U.S. 484, 495 (1990).

The Court has repeatedly applied *Johnson* retroactively in the collateral context. In *Holloway v. United States*, 137 S. Ct. 1328 (2017), the Court held that *Johnson* applied to cases on collateral review, even where the defendant had not raised the issue at trial or on direct appeal. The Court reasoned that *Johnson* “announced a new substantive rule” that “was not merely a procedural change,” and that “the rule is one that is necessary to prevent arbitrary and capricious punishment.” *Id.* at 1333. The same logic applies here. Welch’s sentence—15 years in prison—was imposed based on a predicate conviction that, under *Johnson*, cannot qualify as a “violent felony.” The residual clause was the only basis on which the Florida robbery conviction could be deemed a violent felony under the ACCA. Without it, the conviction fails under the *elements clause* as well, because the force required under Florida law at the time was not “the use, attempted use, or threatened use of physical force against the person of another.” 18 U.S.C. § 924(e)(2)(B)(i). As the Eleventh Circuit itself acknowledged, the Florida statute “criminalized stealing property from another’s person or custody using ‘force, violence, assault, or putting in fear.’” But “force” under Florida law at the time did not require more than “the slightest force” to remove property from a person’s possession. *McCloud*, 335 So.2d at 258. That is insufficient under federal law.

The government’s argument that *Johnson* does not apply retroactively is foreclosed by precedent. In *Teague*, the Court held that a new rule of constitutional law applies retroactively if it “places certain kinds of primary, private conduct beyond the power of the criminal law to proscribe.” *Teague*, 489 U.S. at 310. The *Johnson* rule does exactly that: it invalidates the use of a vague residual clause to punish conduct that a person of ordinary intelligence could not reasonably foresee as criminal. The rule is not merely procedural—it is substantive, and it is necessary to ensure that punishment is not arbitrary. The Court has recognized that “a rule that eliminates a source of arbitrary punishment is entitled to retroactive application.” *Saffle*, 494 U.S. at 495.

Moreover, the Court has applied *Johnson* retroactively in multiple cases involving ACCA sentencing. In *Holloway*, the Court applied *Johnson* to a case where the defendant had been sentenced under the ACCA based on a conviction that would not qualify under the residual clause. The Court held that “the rule announced in *Johnson* applies to all cases in which the residual clause was used to enhance a sentence.” *Holloway*, 137 S. Ct. at 1333. The same is true here.
