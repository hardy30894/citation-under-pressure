The Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment requires a trial court to apply a heightened standard to determine the competency of a defendant to enter a plea of guilty or waive counsel. The Ninth Circuit correctly held that the trial court erred in failing to hold a hearing to evaluate Moran's competency to waive his constitutional rights.

The competency standard for waiving constitutional rights is distinct from the standard for standing trial. As established in Dusky v. United States, 362 U.S. 402 (1960), a defendant is competent to stand trial if they have a rational and factual understanding of the proceedings and can assist their counsel. However, waiving constitutional rights demands a higher level of mental functioning, as noted in Johnson v. Zerbst, 304 U.S. 458 (1938), where the Court emphasized the importance of a defendant's "intelligent and competent waiver" of their rights.

The Supreme Court has long recognized that a trial court must have a "good faith doubt" about a defendant's competency before it is required to hold a hearing, as stated in Pate v. Robinson, 383 U.S. 375 (1966). In Moran's case, the trial court was presented with substantial evidence that should have raised doubts about his competency, including his recent suicide attempt, his ingestion of multiple medications, and his monosyllabic responses to the court's questions.

The trial court's failure to inquire further into Moran's competency was a clear error, as it had a duty to "indulge every reasonable presumption against waiver" of Moran's constitutional rights, as stated in Glasser v. United States, 315 U.S. 60 (1942). The Court in Von Moltke v. Gillies, 332 U.S. 708 (1948), emphasized that a trial court must ensure that a defendant's waiver is "intelligent and competent."

The post-conviction hearing held three years after Moran's plea did not cure the trial court's error, as it applied the wrong legal standard for determining competency. The state court's reliance on psychiatric evaluations that assessed Moran's competency to stand trial, rather than his competency to waive constitutional rights, was misplaced, as seen in Massey v. Moore, 348 U.S. 105 (1954), where the Court held that a defendant's competency to stand trial does not necessarily establish their competency to waive their rights.

Furthermore, the psychiatric evaluations themselves were limited in their scope and did not address the effects of Moran's medication on his competency. As the Court noted in Powell v. Alabama, 287 U.S. 45 (1932), a defendant's waiver of their rights must be made with a full understanding of the consequences, and the trial court had a duty to ensure that Moran's waiver was informed.

In conclusion, the Due Process Clause requires a trial court to apply a heightened standard to determine a defendant's competency to waive constitutional rights. The Ninth Circuit correctly held that the trial court erred in failing to hold a hearing to evaluate Moran's competency, and the post-conviction hearing did not cure this error. The judgment of the Ninth Circuit should be affirmed.
