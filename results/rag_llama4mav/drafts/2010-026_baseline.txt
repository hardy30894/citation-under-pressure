The Ninth Circuit erred in exempting documents relating to the effects of explosions from disclosure under the FOIA because they are "predominantly" for internal use and present a risk of circumventing agency regulation.

The Freedom of Information Act ("FOIA") reflects "a general philosophy of full agency disclosure unless information is exempted under clearly delineated statutory language." Department of the Air Force v. Rose, 425 U.S. 352, 360-61 (1976). The nine exemptions under FOIA are "explicitly exclusive" and must be "narrowly construed." United States Department of Justice v. Tax Analysts, 492 U.S. 136, 151 (1989); Lahr v. NTSB, 569 F.3d 964, 973 (9th Cir.2009).

The Ninth Circuit's decision to exempt the documents under Exemption 2 was incorrect. Exemption 2 shields matters that are "related solely to the internal personnel rules and practices of an agency." 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(2). The court adopted the "predominantly internal" standard, which is not supported by the statutory language or Supreme Court precedent. The phrase "law enforcement" is not in the text of § 552(b)(2), and the Supreme Court in Rose does not use it as a standard for Exemption 2.

Moreover, the Ninth Circuit's application of the "predominantly internal" standard is overly broad. The documents at issue, Explosive Safety Quantity Distance ("ESQD") data, are not "predominantly internal" in the sense that they are used for a significant public purpose - ensuring the safety of the surrounding community. The Navy's own manual, the OP-5 manual, states that the ESQD calculations are used to "prescribe standardized safety regulations for the production, renovation, care, handling, storage, preparation for shipment, and disposal of" ammunition and explosives.

Furthermore, the Navy has not demonstrated that disclosure of the ESQD data would present a risk of circumvention of agency regulation. The Navy's concern that the information could be used to plan an attack or disrupt operations on NMII is not sufficient to justify exemption under Exemption 2. The Navy has not classified the ESQD arc maps, and there is reason to suspect that the Navy's reluctance to release the documents is based on political sensitivity rather than a genuine national security concern.

The Ninth Circuit should have considered alternative exemptions, such as Exemption 1, which exempts classified matters "kept secret in the interest of national defense or foreign policy." 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(1). If the Navy truly believes that disclosure of the ESQD arc maps would pose a threat to national security, it should classify them. The Navy's failure to do so suggests that the documents are not exempt under Exemption 1.

In conclusion, the Ninth Circuit erred in exempting the documents relating to the effects of explosions from disclosure under FOIA. The "predominantly internal" standard is not supported by the statutory language or Supreme Court precedent, and the Navy has not demonstrated a sufficient risk of circumvention to justify exemption under Exemption 2. The judgment of the Ninth Circuit should be reversed.
