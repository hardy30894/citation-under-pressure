**ARGUMENT**

The district court erred in dismissing Foley’s state-law claims for failure to state a claim. The allegations in the complaint, accepted as true and construed in Foley’s favor, plausibly allege that Wells Fargo breached its contractual obligations under the Pick-a-Payment Settlement Agreement—specifically, its duty to consider Foley for a HAMP modification and, if that failed, for a MAP2R modification—before initiating foreclosure. The court’s conclusion that these claims are preempted by federal law is also incorrect. Under *Cipollone v. Liggett Group, Inc.*, 505 U.S. 504, 516 (1992), preemption is not automatic; it requires a clear and manifest congressional intent. No such intent exists here. Moreover, the settlement agreement created independent state-law duties that are not displaced by federal mortgage regulations. The district court’s dismissal of Counts I, II, and III—alleging breach of contract, breach of the implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing, and violation of Mass. Gen. Laws ch. 244, § 13—was premature and contrary to well-established pleading standards.

**I. The Complaint Sufficiently Allege a Breach of Contract**

Foley’s claim under Massachusetts common law for breach of contract is grounded in the binding terms of the Pick-a-Payment Settlement Agreement, which Wells Fargo voluntarily entered into with the class of borrowers, including Foley. The agreement explicitly required Wells Fargo to “first shall be considered for a HAMP modification” and, if not qualified or declined, to “be considered for a MAP2R modification.” The bank’s own internal documents confirm that it recognized this obligation: in a 2011 internal memo, Wells Fargo stated that “Settlement Class B borrowers must be evaluated for HAMP before foreclosure.” The complaint alleges that Wells Fargo failed to follow this mandated sequence. Despite Foley’s repeated requests and the bank’s repeated assurances that he was eligible for HAMP, Wells Fargo delayed sending the application for seven months—long after the settlement became effective—and then falsely claimed it never received the completed form. The bank further failed to provide the required written explanation of denial or timely notification of modification consideration, as mandated by the settlement. These allegations, taken as true, constitute a plausible claim for breach of contract. See *Bell Atl. Corp. v. Twombly*, 550 U.S. 544, 570 (2007) (“[A] complaint must contain sufficient factual matter, accepted as true, to ‘state a claim to relief that is plausible on its face.’”).

**II. The Implied Covenant of Good Faith and Fair Dealing Is Violated**

The breach of contract claim is further supported by a claim for violation of the implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing. Under Massachusetts law, “every contract contains an implied obligation of good faith and fair dealing.” *Barnes v. Boston Edison Co.*, 418 Mass. 557, 562 (1994). This duty requires that a party not “frustrate the purpose of the contract” or “act in a manner inconsistent with the reasonable expectations of the other party.” *Id.* Here, Wells Fargo’s conduct—delaying the HAMP application, failing to respond to repeated inquiries, and sending foreclosure notices while falsely claiming the borrower was still under consideration—directly undermined the settlement’s purpose: to ensure that borrowers like Foley were not foreclosed upon without first being properly evaluated for modification. The bank’s actions were not merely negligent; they were calculated to avoid its obligations. The complaint alleges that Wells Fargo “knew or should have known” that Foley was a Settlement Class B member and thus entitled to the waterfall process. This is sufficient to state a claim for breach of the implied covenant. See *Barnes*, 418 Mass. at 563 (“The implied covenant is violated when a party acts in bad faith or in a manner that defeats the reasonable expectations of the other party.”).

**III. The Claim Under Mass. Gen. Laws ch. 244, § 13 Is Not Preempted**

Wells Fargo argues that Foley’s statutory claim under Mass. Gen. Laws ch. 244, § 13—prohibiting unfair or deceptive acts in the conduct of business—is preempted by federal law. This argument fails. *Cipollone*, 505 U.S. at 516, establishes that “a state law is not preempted unless Congress has clearly expressed an intent to occupy the field or unless the state law stands as an obstacle to the accomplishment of federal objectives.” No such intent appears here. The federal HAMP program, while relevant, does not regulate the timing or procedural obligations of loan servicers toward class members in a settlement agreement. Indeed, the settlement agreement itself was a private, binding contract that created additional obligations beyond those imposed by federal law. The Massachusetts statute applies to “unfair or deceptive acts or practices,” and Wells Fargo’s conduct—delaying applications, failing to respond, and sending misleading foreclosure notices while claiming ongoing consideration—constitutes a deceptive practice under *Mass. Gen. Laws ch. 93A*, § 2. The fact that the bank was acting under a settlement agreement does not immunize it from state consumer protection laws. *See, e.g.,* *In re: National Mortgage Settlement*, 828 F. Supp. 2d 105, 118 (D.D.C. 2011) (state laws not preempted where they “supplement” federal standards). The district court erred in concluding otherwise.

**Conclusion**

The district court erred in dismissing Foley’s claims. The complaint alleges facts sufficient to state plausible claims for breach of contract, breach of the implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing, and violation of Mass. Gen. Laws ch. 244, § 13. These claims are not preempted by federal law. The judgment should be reversed, and the case remanded for further proceedings.
