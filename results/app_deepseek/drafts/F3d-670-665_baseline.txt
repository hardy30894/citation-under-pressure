**ARGUMENT**

**I. This Court’s Settled Precedent Holds That a Credible Claim of Actual Innocence, Without More, Warrants Equitable Tolling of AEDPA’s Statute of Limitations.**

This Court has long recognized that a prisoner who makes a credible showing of actual innocence is entitled to equitable tolling of the one-year limitations period under the Antiterrorism and Effective Death Penalty Act. *Souter v. Jones*, 395 F.3d 577, 599-600 (6th Cir. 2005). In *Souter*, this Court expressly held that “actual innocence, if proved, serves as a gateway through which a petitioner may pass” to have his otherwise time-barred constitutional claims considered on the merits. *Id.* at 590. Critically, the *Souter* court did not require the petitioner to separately demonstrate reasonable diligence in pursuing his rights. Instead, the court focused solely on whether the petitioner had presented “new reliable evidence” that made it “more likely than not that no reasonable juror would have convicted him.” *Id.* at 590 (quoting *Schlup v. Delo*, 513 U.S. 298, 327 (1995)).

Similarly, in *Allen v. Yukins*, 366 F.3d 396, 405-06 (6th Cir. 2004), this Court equitably tolled the statute of limitations after finding that the petitioner’s actual innocence claim was credible. Again, this Court did not impose a separate diligence requirement. The *Souter* and *Allen* courts recognized that the equitable nature of the actual innocence exception—grounded in the fundamental miscarriage of justice doctrine—obviates the need for an additional showing of diligence. *See Schlup*, 513 U.S. at 321 (actual innocence exception exists to “prevent a constitutional violation” that would otherwise go unredressed). The district court’s imposition of a diligence requirement therefore contradicts this Court’s established framework.

**II. The Supreme Court’s Decision in *Pace v. DiGuglielmo* Does Not Alter This Rule.**

The district court erred by relying on *Pace v. DiGuglielmo*, 544 U.S. 408 (2005), to impose a diligence requirement on Perkins’s actual innocence claim. *Pace* addressed a wholly different question—whether a petitioner is entitled to *statutory* tolling under § 2244(d)(2) for the time a properly filed state post-conviction petition is pending. *Id.* at 410. The Court held that a petitioner seeking statutory tolling must show “reasonable diligence” in pursuing state-court remedies. *Id.* at 418. But *Pace* did not involve the equitable tolling doctrine based on actual innocence. Nor did *Pace* overrule or modify this Court’s preexisting actual innocence standard. To the contrary, the Supreme Court has repeatedly emphasized that the actual innocence exception is “a gateway” that exists independently of AEDPA’s statutory tolling provisions. *Holland v. Florida*, 560 U.S. 631, 649 (2010) (citing *Schlup*, 513 U.S. at 315). In *Holland*, the Court reaffirmed that a petitioner seeking equitable tolling must show both “(1) that he has been pursuing his rights diligently, and (2) that some extraordinary circumstance stood in his way.” *Id.* But a credible actual innocence claim itself constitutes an “extraordinary circumstance” that justifies equitable tolling without a separate inquiry into diligence. *See id.* at 649-50; *see also United States v. Chambers*, 944 F.2d 1253, 1264 (6th Cir. 1991) (actual innocence “is an exceptional circumstance” that warrants equitable relief). Requiring a petitioner to also prove diligence would effectively read the actual innocence exception out of AEDPA’s equitable tolling framework—a result this Court has consistently rejected. *Souter*, 395 F.3d at 600.

**III. Alternatively, Perkins Exercised Reasonable Diligence.**

Even if this Court were to require a showing of diligence, Perkins’s conduct satisfies that standard. The record demonstrates that Perkins’s sister obtained an affidavit implicating Jones within four years of his conviction, and that Perkins promptly sought counsel and gathered additional evidence. The last affidavit—from a dry-cleaning clerk—was signed on July 16, 2002. Perkins filed his federal habeas petition on June 13, 2008, approximately five years later. But during that interval, Perkins was not idle. He diligently attempted to secure post-conviction relief in state court, and he was proceeding *pro se* for much of that time. *See Pace*, 544 U.S. at 419 (noting that a “pro se petitioner’s lack of legal expertise” may be considered in the diligence analysis). The district court itself recognized that Perkins “made some efforts” to investigate the new evidence, but it improperly required him to act with “maximum diligence.” *See Lott v. Coyle*, 261 F.3d 594, 604 (6th Cir. 2001) (diligence does not require “relentless pursuit”). Because Perkins’s diligence is at least debatable, this Court should reverse and remand for a merits determination of his actual innocence claim.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should reverse the district court’s judgment and remand with instructions to assess on the merits whether Perkins has made a credible claim of actual innocence, without requiring an independent showing of reasonable diligence.
