# ARGUMENT

## I. The Text and Structure of OCSLA Confine State Law to a Gap-Filling Role.

Section 1333(a)(2)(A) of the Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act (OCSLA) declares state law to be federal law only "[t]o the extent that [it is] applicable and not inconsistent with … other Federal laws." 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(2)(A). This Court has already construed that provision once, in *Rodrigue v. Aetna Casualty & Surety Co.*, 395 U.S. 352 (1969), and its analysis confirms that OCSLA borrows state law only where federal law itself supplies none. The Court explained that "the purpose of the Act was to define a body of law applicable to the seabed, the subsoil, and the fixed structures … on the outer Continental Shelf," *id.* at 355, treating those structures "as federal enclaves within a landlocked State," *id.* Congress's chosen model—federal enclave law—necessarily implies that state law operates interstitially, filling voids that Congress's own body of law leaves open, rather than running concurrently with a complete federal scheme.

That is precisely how *Rodrigue* applied the statute. The Court permitted resort to state wrongful-death law only after concluding that no federal admiralty remedy existed for deaths on fixed platforms, because the accidents did not "involve[] no collision with a vessel" and the platforms "were not navigational aids." *Id.* at 360. Having found federal maritime law inapplicable, the Court held that this absence of a competing federal remedy "removes any obstacle to the application of state law by incorporation as federal law through" § 1333(a)(2). *Id.* at 366. The necessary premise of that holding is that a *present* federal remedy would have been such an obstacle. *Rodrigue* thus stands for the proposition that state law is adopted as surrogate federal law only when federal law does not already occupy the relevant ground—precisely the "gap" inquiry the Fifth Circuit later crystallized in *Continental Oil Co. v. London S.S. Owners' Mut. Ins. Ass'n*, 417 F.2d 1030 (5th Cir. 1969), which required "necessity to fill a significant void or gap" in federal law before assimilating state law under § 1333(a)(2). *Id.* at 1036.

## II. OCSLA's Enclave Model Compels a Gap-Filling Construction.

Congress modeled OCSLA's choice-of-law provision on the law governing traditional federal enclaves, and that body of law has long treated state law as merely filling interstices in an otherwise exclusive federal domain. In *Chicago, Rock Island & Pacific Railway Co. v. McGlinn*, 114 U.S. 542 (1885), this Court held that when territory becomes a federal enclave, the state law in force at the time of cession continues only "as if the territory had remained under the exclusive jurisdiction of the State," and does not evolve automatically with subsequent state legislation absent congressional adoption. *Id.* at 546. That enclave framework reflects the understanding that federal, not state, law is the governing sovereign authority; state law persists only where the federal sovereign has not spoken.

This Court in *United States v. Sharpnack*, 355 U.S. 286 (1958), upheld Congress's power to adopt *future* state criminal law for federal enclaves under the Assimilative Crimes Act precisely because doing so filled otherwise-open gaps in federal criminal coverage on the enclave—not because state law was meant to operate alongside a complete federal code. *Id.* at 293–94. OCSLA's 1975 amendment, which likewise updated "applicable" state law to include after-enacted state statutes, imported the same enclave logic that *Sharpnack* approved: state law supplies rules of decision only where none yet exist in federal law, not a freestanding overlay on subjects Congress has already regulated by federal statute.

## III. The FLSA Is a Comprehensive Scheme That Leaves No Room for California's Wage-and-Hour Law.

The gap-filling inquiry required by OCSLA forecloses respondent's reliance on California law here because the Fair Labor Standards Act (FLSA), 29 U.S.C. § 201 *et seq.*, comprehensively regulates the subject of minimum wage, overtime, and related wage-hour protections for platform workers. This Court has recognized that a federal regulatory scheme may be "so pervasive as to make reasonable the inference that Congress left no room for the States to supplement it." *Rice v. Santa Fe Elevator Corp.*, 331 U.S. 218, 230 (1947). The FLSA's detailed provisions governing minimum wage, overtime compensation, and recordkeeping—together with the Department of Labor's implementing regulations—constitute exactly such a pervasive scheme. There is no "void or gap," *Continental Oil*, 417 F.2d at 1036, in federal law respecting the wages an outer-Continental-Shelf worker like respondent must be paid; the FLSA speaks directly and completely to that subject.

Respondent's suggestion that state law may apply concurrently whenever it is not affirmatively inconsistent with federal law—rather than only when federal law is silent—cannot be squared with *Rodrigue*'s enclave-based analysis or with the gap-filling logic that necessarily follows from Congress's decision to treat the Shelf as if it were federal territory. *See Rodrigue*, 395 U.S. at 355, 366. Because the FLSA fully occupies the field of wage-and-hour regulation for OCS platform workers, California's meal-break and overtime requirements are not "applicable" state law under § 1333(a)(2)(A), and the district court correctly held that Newton's state-law claims could not proceed.

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should reverse the judgment of the court of appeals and hold that OCSLA adopts state law only to fill gaps left by federal law.
