The question presented should be answered no. Withdrawal before the limitations period does not shift to the Government a burden to prove beyond a reasonable doubt that the accused remained a conspirator during the relevant period. Rather, once the Government proves the defendant knowingly joined the conspiracy, the burden rests on the defendant to prove withdrawal as an affirmative defense or, at minimum, to raise a factual issue on withdrawal that defeats liability for later acts. See Hyde v. United States, 225 U.S. 347, 369-70 (1912); Brown v. United States, 276 U.S. 134, 141-42 (1928).

This Court has long treated conspiracy as a continuing offense. Hyde held that “the conspiracy continues until consummated or abandoned,” and that a defendant who has joined it remains liable until he shows that he “did all that he could to disassociate himself.” 225 U.S. at 369-70. Brown reiterated that “membership in a conspiracy is presumed to continue” until the defendant withdraws, and that withdrawal is something the conspirator must establish. 276 U.S. at 141-42. Those principles foreclose petitioner’s proposed rule. If membership presumptively continues, the Government does not carry an additional burden to prove, beyond a reasonable doubt, that the defendant was still a member during the limitations period unless and until the defendant produces evidence of withdrawal.

The Court’s earlier conspiracy decisions point the same way. In United States v. Britton, 108 U.S. 199, 205-06 (1883), the Court recognized that conspiracy is not a series of isolated acts but an agreement that endures until its objective is accomplished or abandoned. In United States v. Kissel, 218 U.S. 601, 608-09 (1910), the Court explained that a conspiracy may be “a partnership in criminal purposes” that persists over time, so that participation is not confined to a single day or overt act. And in Clune v. United States, 159 U.S. 590, 595 (1895), the Court emphasized that the offense lies in the agreement itself, not merely in contemporaneous acts, making continued membership the default once entry is shown.

That framework is inconsistent with treating withdrawal as part of the Government’s case-in-chief. This Court has instead placed the burden of proving a defense on the party asserting it when the defense concerns a matter peculiarly within the defendant’s knowledge. See Morrison v. California, 291 U.S. 82, 88-89 (1934) (allocation of burden may constitutionally rest on defendant where fact is especially within his knowledge); Casey v. United States, 276 U.S. 413, 418-19 (1928) (recognizing that Congress may place on the defendant the burden of going forward on a matter in the nature of a defense). Withdrawal fits that description. It is not an element of conspiracy; it is a later event that excuses future liability. The Government must prove the agreement and the defendant’s knowing participation. Once that is done, the defendant who claims he later withdrew must prove the fact of withdrawal.

Petitioner’s rule would also create an anomaly in limitations law. A conspirator could admit knowing participation, remain silent about withdrawal, and yet force the Government to disprove a negative—his continued membership—beyond a reasonable doubt. This Court has rejected similar efforts to recast affirmative defenses as elements of the offense. In Davis v. United States, 160 U.S. 469, 488-89 (1895), the Court held that where a defendant relies on a matter of excuse or exception peculiarly within his knowledge, he may be required to produce evidence of it. The same reasoning applies here: withdrawal is an excuse for later liability, not a fact that transforms the conspiracy count into a new offense every day.

Nor does the statute of limitations change that allocation. The limitations bar prevents prosecution of stale crimes; it does not redefine the elements of conspiracy. Once the Government proves the defendant joined the conspiracy within the limitations period or remained in it absent withdrawal, the prosecution is timely. See United States v. Kissel, 218 U.S. at 608-09; Brown v. United States, 276 U.S. at 141-42. If petitioner’s rule were adopted, every limitations defense in a conspiracy case would require the Government to negate withdrawal affirmatively, even where the defendant alone knows whether he renounced the enterprise. Nothing in this Court’s pre-1970 precedents supports that result.

The judgment should therefore be affirmed. The Government need not prove beyond a reasonable doubt that a defendant remained a member of the conspiracy during the limitations period where the defendant claims to have withdrawn earlier. Withdrawal is the defendant’s defense, not the Government’s burden.
