**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Eleventh Circuit Properly Reviewed the Record as a Whole to Determine Prejudice.**

Under Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 52(b), an appellate court may correct an error not preserved for appeal only if the error "affects substantial rights." United States v. Olano, 507 U.S. 725, 732 (1993). To determine if an error affects substantial rights, the court must consider the record as a whole, including the plea agreement and sentencing hearing, not merely the evidence presented to the jury. United States v. Dominguez Benitez, 542 U.S. 74, 84 (2004). The Eleventh Circuit correctly looked at the entire record, including the plea agreement where Greer admitted his status as a felon, to assess prejudice. The court’s review was consistent with Eleventh Circuit precedent, which requires a "thorough examination of the record" to determine whether an error affected substantial rights. United States v. Pugh, 915 F.3d 1298, 1302 (11th Cir. 2019).

**II. The Error Did Not Affect Greer’s Substantial Rights.**

Here, Greer’s substantial rights were not affected because he admitted his status as a felon in his plea agreement. In *United States v. Broce*, the Supreme Court held that a defendant's plea agreement is a binding admission of the facts constituting the offense. 488 U.S. 563, 575 (1989). Furthermore, a guilty plea waives the right to challenge the sufficiency of the evidence. United States v. Ruiz, 536 U.S. 622, 627 (2002). The record shows Greer admitted his status during the sentencing hearing, which is part of the record on appeal. Therefore, the error was harmless. The Supreme Court has held that an error is harmless if the record demonstrates that the error did not affect the outcome. United States v. Cotton, 535 U.S. 625, 635 (2002).

**III. The Error Did Not Affect the Fairness, Integrity, or Public Reputation of the Trial.**

Additionally, the error did not affect the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of the trial. The Supreme Court has emphasized that the "fairness" prong of the plain error test requires a showing that the error "seriously affect[s] the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of judicial proceedings." United States v. Strickler, 527 U.S. 263, 280 (1999). Because the jury never needed to determine Greer’s status, the lack of a specific instruction on that element did not undermine the trial's integrity. The error was harmless because the prosecution did not need to prove the knowledge element to a jury; the defendant admitted it. *Rehaif v. United States*, 139 S. Ct. 2199, 2203 (2019). The Eleventh Circuit properly concluded that the error was harmless.
