**ARGUMENT**

**I. Federal Jurisdiction Over Patent-Related Claims Is Exclusive Under § 1338(a) Where Patent Law Is a Necessary Element of the Claim**

The Supreme Court has long held that federal courts possess exclusive jurisdiction over cases "arising under" federal patent law. *St. Paul Plough Works v. Starling*, 127 U.S. 376, 379 (1888) ("[I]n any case touching patent rights" federal courts have jurisdiction). This principle applies with particular force where, as here, the plaintiff’s state-law malpractice claim *necessarily* requires resolution of a disputed and substantial question of federal patent law. *Christianson v. Colt Indus. Operating Corp.*, 486 U.S. 800, 808-09 (1988) (holding that § 1338(a) extends to cases where "the plaintiff’s right to relief necessarily depends on resolution of a substantial question of federal patent law").

Minton’s malpractice claim is inextricably intertwined with federal patent law. To prevail, he must prove that his attorneys’ failure to raise the experimental use exception proximately caused the loss of his patent infringement claim. *Alexander v. Turtur & Assocs.*, 146 S.W.3d 113, 117 (Tex. 2004). The experimental use exception, in turn, is a creature of federal patent law. *Electromotive Div. of Gen. Motors Corp. v. Transp. Sys. Div. of Gen. Elec. Co.*, 417 F.3d 1203, 1210 (Fed. Cir. 2005). Without resolving whether Minton’s earlier sale of TEXCEN qualified for the exception, the malpractice claim cannot succeed. Thus, the federal patent issue is not merely "incidental" but *necessary* to the malpractice claim. *Marsh v. Nichols, Shepard & Co.*, 140 U.S. 344, 350 (1891) ("[T]he subject matter of this suit differed from the subject matter of [the prior patent suit], and nothing in this suit prevented plaintiffs in error from prosecuting their appeal").

**II. The Federal Patent Issue Is Substantial and Disputed**

The Supreme Court has emphasized that federal jurisdiction exists only where the federal issue is both "substantial" and "actually disputed." *Grable & Sons Metal Prods., Inc. v. Darue Eng’g & Mfg.*, 545 U.S. 308, 313 (2005). Here, the experimental use exception is a well-recognized defense under federal patent law, and its applicability turns on contested factual questions—e.g., whether Minton’s lease of TEXCEN was primarily experimental. *Root v. Third Avenue Railroad*, 146 U.S. 210, 216 (1892) (distinguishing between experimental and commercial use under patent law). The Federal Circuit has repeatedly treated the experimental use exception as a substantial issue in patent litigation. *Electromotive*, 417 F.3d at 1210. Thus, the federal patent issue is not a "frivolous" or "insubstantial" pretext for jurisdiction but a core dispute in the malpractice claim.

**III. Exclusive Federal Jurisdiction Does Not Disturb the Federal-State Balance**

The Supreme Court has cautioned that federal jurisdiction should not be expanded in ways that disrupt the balance between state and federal courts. *Gully v. First Nat’l Bank in Meridian*, 299 U.S. 109, 117-18 (1936). However, where a state-law claim *necessarily* implicates federal patent law, federal jurisdiction is appropriate to ensure uniformity in patent enforcement. *St. Paul Plough Works*, 127 U.S. at 379. The experimental use exception is a quintessential example of a federal patent doctrine that requires uniform application. *Andrews v. Hovey*, 123 U.S. 267, 275 (1887) (federal patent law governs the validity of patents based on public use). Allowing state courts to adjudicate malpractice claims arising from patent litigation would risk inconsistent rulings on patent validity and defenses, undermining Congress’s intent to create a unified patent system. *Hoover Co. v. Coe*, 325 U.S. 79, 84 (1945) (federal courts have jurisdiction over patent-related disputes to ensure uniformity).

**IV. Prior Supreme Court Precedent Supports Exclusive Jurisdiction in This Case**

The Supreme Court has repeatedly recognized that federal jurisdiction over patent matters is broad and exclusive where patent law is a necessary element of the claim. *St. Paul Plough Works*, 127 U.S. at 379; *Ex Parte Frasch*, 192 U.S. 566, 570 (1904) (federal courts have jurisdiction over patent-related disputes to ensure consistent application of patent law). In *Andrews v. Hovey*, 124 U.S. 694 (1888), the Court held that federal patent law governs the validity of patents based on public use, even where the claim arises in state court. Similarly, in *Root v. Third Avenue Railroad*, 146 U.S. at 216, the Court distinguished between experimental and commercial use under federal patent law, reinforcing the need for federal resolution of such disputes.

**CONCLUSION**

The Supreme Court’s precedents compel the conclusion that federal courts have exclusive jurisdiction over Minton’s malpractice claim, as it necessarily requires resolution of a substantial and disputed question of federal patent law. The experimental use exception is a core patent doctrine, and its application cannot be left to state courts without risking inconsistent rulings that undermine the federal patent system. The judgment below should be reversed.
