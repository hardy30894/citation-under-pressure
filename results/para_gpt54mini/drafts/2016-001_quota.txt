**Argument**

Design-patent damages are governed by 35 U.S.C. § 289, which permits recovery of “the total profit” from the “article of manufacture” to which the patented design is applied. But that statute does not authorize profits untethered to the actual article that embodies the design. The Supreme Court has long recognized that patent remedies must be applied in a manner faithful to the patent’s scope, not the commercial value of unpatented aspects of a larger product. *Seymour v. McCormick*, 57 U.S. (16 How.) 480, 490 (1853); *Garretson v. Clark*, 111 U.S. 120, 121 (1884). Where a patent covers only a component or ornamental portion of a multi-component product, damages must be limited to the profit attributable to the relevant article of manufacture, not the infringer’s entire profits on the end product. *Id.*; *Elizabeth v. Pavement Co.*, 97 U.S. 126, 138 (1877).

The Court’s modern apportionment cases confirm that principle. In *Westinghouse Electric & Manufacturing Co. v. Wagner Electric & Manufacturing Co.*, 225 U.S. 604, 614-15 (1912), the Court held that when a patented feature is only part of a larger machine, the patentee may recover only the profits attributable to that feature unless the patentee proves the accused product’s profits are wholly due to the invention. *Dowagiac Manufacturing Co. v. Minnesota Moline Plow Co.*, 235 U.S. 641, 648-49 (1915), likewise rejected recovery of all profits from a multi-part product where the patent covered only one element. And *Aro Manufacturing Co. v. Convertible Top Replacement Co.*, 377 U.S. 476, 507 (1964), emphasized that patent damages do not permit recovery for value created by unpatented components or unpatented uses. Those decisions reflect a consistent rule: the patentee bears the burden of tying compensation to the patented contribution.

That rule is especially important here. Section 289 states that an infringer “shall be liable to the owner to the extent of his total profit,” 35 U.S.C. § 289, but the phrase “article of manufacture” is the limiting principle. A smartphone is not necessarily the relevant article merely because it is the commercial product sold to consumers. When the asserted design patent covers only a component or a portion of the product, the statute requires identifying the article of manufacture to which the design was applied and then awarding only the profit from that article. Reading § 289 to reach the entire end-product profit would collapse the article-of-manufacture limitation and revive the overbroad recovery rejected in *Garretson*, 111 U.S. at 121, and *Dowagiac*, 235 U.S. at 648-49.

The Federal Circuit’s contrary approach also conflicts with general principles of patent damages. In *Suffolk Co. v. Hayden*, 70 U.S. (3 Wall.) 315, 320 (1865), the Court explained that damages must be limited to the actual injury proved. Likewise, in *Sheldon v. Metro-Goldwyn Pictures Corp.*, 309 U.S. 390, 402-03 (1940), the Court rejected an award of profits not fairly attributable to the protected contribution. Although *Sheldon* arose under copyright, its core principle is the same: when protected and unprotected features are commingled, courts must apportion rather than award windfalls. Patent law has adopted that same logic repeatedly. *Westinghouse*, 225 U.S. at 614-15; *Dowagiac*, 235 U.S. at 648-49.

Samsung’s position is not that design patents are disfavored or that apportionment is always mathematically exact. It is that § 289 does not permit the patentee to capture profits from components the design patent did not cover. That position is consistent with the Court’s recognition that the scope of patent remedies tracks the scope of the invention. *Seymour*, 57 U.S. at 490; *Garretson*, 111 U.S. at 121; *Westinghouse*, 225 U.S. at 614-15. It also accords with the Court’s treatment of multicomponent products in utility-patent damages cases, where recovery must reflect the value of the patented feature, not the whole device. *Aro*, 377 U.S. at 507; *Dowagiac*, 235 U.S. at 648-49.

The judgment below should therefore be reversed. The proper rule is that when a design patent covers only a component of a product, damages under § 289 must be limited to the infringer’s profits attributable to that component, because only that component is the relevant “article of manufacture.” Anything broader ignores the statute’s text and the Court’s apportionment precedents. *Garretson*, 111 U.S. at 121; *Westinghouse*, 225 U.S. at 614-15; *Dowagiac*, 235 U.S. at 648-49.
