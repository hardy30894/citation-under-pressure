**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE DISTRICT COURT COMMITTED REVERSIBLE ERROR BY ADMITTING IMPERMISSIBLE OPINION TESTIMONY THAT DEPRIVED APPELLANTS OF A FAIR TRIAL.**

The admission of opinion testimony from the Assistant District Attorneys ("ADAs") and the police lieutenant violated Federal Rule of Evidence 701 and 704, as well as longstanding principles of due process. This Court has repeatedly held that lay witnesses may not offer opinions on the credibility of other witnesses or on ultimate issues of fact. *See United States v. Dukagjini*, 326 F.3d 45, 54 (2d Cir. 2003) ("A witness may not opine on the credibility of another witness."); *United States v. Garcia*, 993 F.2d 18, 21 (2d Cir. 1993) ("A witness may not give an opinion on the ultimate issue in the case."). Moreover, the Supreme Court has made clear that the admission of such testimony is not harmless when it "permeate[s] the entire record." *United States v. Hasting*, 461 U.S. 499, 510 (1983).

Here, the ADAs and the police lieutenant were permitted to testify that they believed Ramos and Rivera were credible, that probable cause existed for the arrests, and that the evidence supported the officers’ version of events. This testimony directly invaded the province of the jury and improperly bolstered the appellees’ case. *See United States v. Mejia*, 545 F.3d 179, 190 (2d Cir. 2008) ("A witness may not vouch for the credibility of another witness."). The District Court compounded this error by failing to give a limiting instruction, leaving the jury with the impression that these government agents’ opinions were entitled to special weight. *See United States v. Locascio*, 6 F.3d 924, 934 (2d Cir. 1993) (holding that the failure to give a limiting instruction exacerbated the prejudice of improper opinion testimony).

The prejudice was exacerbated by the fact that the improper testimony went to the two most critical issues in the case: the credibility of Ramos and Rivera and the existence of probable cause. *See Hynes v. Coughlin*, 79 F.3d 285, 291 (2d Cir. 1996) ("The admission of improper opinion testimony is particularly prejudicial when it concerns the central issues in the case."). The jury’s verdict cannot stand where it was unduly influenced by this impermissible testimony. *See Dukagjini*, 326 F.3d at 55 ("The admission of improper opinion testimony is reversible error when it affects the substantial rights of a party.").

**II. THE DISTRICT COURT PROPERLY DENIED APPELLANTS’ MOTION FOR JUDGMENT AS A MATTER OF LAW, AS THE SECURITY CAMERA PHOTOGRAPHS DO NOT DEFINITIVELY CONTRADICT APPELLEES’ ACCOUNT.**

Appellants argue that the security camera photographs "blatantly contradict" appellees’ version of events and thus entitle them to judgment as a matter of law. *See* Appellants’ Br. 31. This argument fails because the photographs are ambiguous and do not conclusively establish appellees’ liability. The Second Circuit has held that a motion for judgment as a matter of law should be granted only where "there is such a complete absence of evidence supporting the verdict that the jury’s findings could only have been the result of sheer surmise and conjecture." *Simpson v. Sanderson*, 903 F.2d 30, 33 (2d Cir. 1990). Here, the photographs do not definitively rule out appellees’ account, and thus the District Court correctly denied the motion. *See id.* ("Where the evidence is subject to conflicting interpretations, the jury’s verdict must stand.").

**III. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED BY FAILING TO INSTRUCT THE JURY ON THE ELEMENTS OF OBSTRUCTING GOVERNMENTAL ADMINISTRATION AND ON PUNITIVE DAMAGES.**

**A. The District Court Should Have Instructed the Jury That Obstructing Governmental Administration Requires a Lawful Arrest.**

Under New York law, obstructing governmental administration ("OGA") requires that the obstructed officers be engaged in a lawful arrest. *See People v. Case*, 42 N.Y.2d 98, 102 (1977) ("The offense of obstructing governmental administration requires proof that the defendant knew that the police were engaged in the lawful performance of their duties."). The District Court failed to instruct the jury on this essential element, depriving appellants of a fair trial. *See Estelle v. McGuire*, 502 U.S. 62, 67 (1991) ("A jury instruction is erroneous if it fails to instruct on an essential element of the offense.").

**B. The District Court Should Have Instructed the Jury on Punitive Damages Where Appellants’ Version of Events Would Support a Finding of Malice.**

Appellants’ version of events—if credited by the jury—would have supported a finding that appellees acted with malice, entitling them to a punitive damages instruction. *See Smith v. Wade*, 461 U.S. 30, 56 (1983) ("Punitive damages may be awarded where the defendant’s conduct is shown to be motivated by evil motive or intent."). The District Court’s failure to give this instruction was error, particularly given the jury’s role in assessing credibility and intent. *See id.* ("The jury’s role in assessing punitive damages requires clear instructions on the standard for their award.").

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the District Court should be reversed and the case remanded for a new trial.
