**ARGUMENT**

The National Park Service (“NPS”) permissibly applied its regulation prohibiting hovercraft operation to state-owned waters and submerged lands within the Yukon-Charley Rivers National Preserve. The panel correctly held that Section 103(c) of the Alaska National Interest Lands Conservation Act (“ANILCA”), 16 U.S.C. § 3103(c), does not strip NPS of regulatory authority over non-federal lands and waters within conservation system units in Alaska. This Court should affirm.

**I. The Plain Text of ANILCA § 103(c) Does Not Prohibit NPS from Regulating State-Owned Lands and Waters Within Park Boundaries.**

Section 103(c) provides that “[n]o conveyance of land or interest in land to the State . . . shall affect the authority of the Secretary over the remaining public lands within the boundaries of a conservation system unit.” 16 U.S.C. § 3103(c). By its express terms, this provision preserves the Secretary’s authority over “public lands”—lands owned by the United States—that lie within a conservation system unit. Sturgeon v. Masica, 133 F. Supp. 3d 1087, 1095 (D. Alaska 2013). It says nothing about limiting federal regulatory authority over state-owned lands or waters.

The statute Congress enacted contains no language prohibiting NPS from regulating activities on non-federal lands within park boundaries. See 16 U.S.C. § 3103(c). Where “the statute’s language is plain, the sole function of the courts—at least where the disposition required by the text is not absurd—is to enforce it according to its terms.” Hartford Underwriters Ins. Co. v. Union Planters Bank, N.A., 530 U.S. 1, 6 (2000). The Ninth Circuit thus correctly held that Sturgeon’s interpretation is “foreclosed by the plain text of the statute.” Pet. App. 5a.

**II. ANILCA’s Broader Structure Confirms that NPS Retains Regulatory Authority Over All Lands Within Park Boundaries.**

Other provisions of ANILCA confirm that Congress did not intend to create a checkerboard of federal authority within Alaska’s national parks. Section 101(b) declares that the purpose of the Act is to “preserve for the benefit, use, education, and inspiration of present and future generations” the “unusual and unique characteristics” of Alaska’s public lands. 16 U.S.C. § 3101(b). To achieve this purpose, Congress gave the Secretary of the Interior authority to “prescribe such regulations as are necessary and appropriate for the management of [national park] units.” 16 U.S.C. § 3124(a).

Sturgeon’s reading would render the Secretary powerless to protect park resources from harmful activities occurring on non-federal inholdings, even as those activities threaten the very values Congress sought to preserve. “It is a commonplace of statutory interpretation that the specific governs the general.” RadLAX Gateway Hotel, LLC v. Amalgamated Bank, 566 U.S. 639, 645 (2012). Section 103(c)’s specific preservation of federal authority over public lands does not impliedly repeal NPS’s general regulatory authority over state lands within parks. See 16 U.S.C. § 3103(c).

**III. Longstanding NPS Regulations Apply Uniformly Within Park Boundaries.**

For decades, NPS regulations have applied to “all lands and waters” within park boundaries. 36 C.F.R. § 1.2(a)(1). The hovercraft ban at issue is one such regulation, prohibiting the “[o]peration or use of hovercraft” in all areas administered by NPS. 36 C.F.R. § 2.17(e). This Court has long recognized that federal land management agencies may regulate activities on non-federal lands within park boundaries to protect federal resources. See, e.g., Kleppe v. New Mexico, 426 U.S. 529, 539 (1976) (upholding federal authority to regulate wild horses and burros on state and private lands within national forests under the Property Clause).

The Property Clause grants Congress “the power of a proprietor” over federal lands. Kleppe, 426 U.S. at 539. That power includes the authority to regulate conduct on non-federal lands that threatens federal property. Id. at 540-41. NPS’s regulation of hovercrafts on state-owned waters within national preserves—waters that flow through and adjacent to federal lands—is a permissible exercise of this proprietary authority.

**IV. ANILCA Itself Preserves Federal Regulatory Authority Over Navigable Waters.**

Even assuming Sturgeon’s reading of Section 103(c) were plausible, ANILCA Section 1319 provides that “[n]othing in [this Act] shall be construed as . . . superseding, modifying, or repealing, except as specifically set forth in this Act, existing laws applicable to the various Federal agencies which are authorized to . . . exercise licensing or regulatory functions in relation thereto.” 16 U.S.C. § 3207. This savings clause confirms that ANILCA did not displace NPS’s pre-existing authority to regulate navigable waters within park units.

Sturgeon’s proposed interpretation would produce absurd results. If accepted, it would mean that within Alaska’s national park boundaries, the federal government could regulate activities on adjacent federal lands but could not regulate the same activities occurring on state-owned riverbeds—even where those activities directly impair park values. Congress could not have intended such a fragmented regulatory regime when it created Alaska’s conservation system units. See FDA v. Brown & Williamson Tobacco Corp., 529 U.S. 120, 133 (2000) (courts must interpret statutes to avoid absurd results).

**CONCLUSION**

The judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
