**ARGUMENT**

The question presented should be answered **no**. A federal fee-shifting statute authorizes a “reasonable” fee, and reasonableness does not turn on a judge’s after-the-fact belief that counsel performed superbly or achieved unusually good results. Long before 1970, this Court made clear that fee awards must rest on objective measures of fair compensation, not subjective praise for counsel.

In **Woods v. City Nat’l Bank & Trust Co. of Chicago**, 312 U.S. 262 (1941), the Court explained that when Congress authorizes a “reasonable” fee, the allowance must be tied to the work done and the value of the services rendered, not to an open-ended judicial bonus. The Court emphasized that fee determinations must be controlled by standards capable of consistent application, not individualized admiration. See id. at 268-69. That principle is incompatible with an enhancement based solely on perceived quality of performance or results obtained.

Likewise, in **Brown v. Gerdes**, 321 U.S. 178 (1944), the Court held that the reasonableness of compensation for legal services must be grounded in “the nature, extent, and value of such services.” Id. at 188. Brown teaches that compensation is measured by the services actually performed, not by a premium for excellence untethered to a demonstrable market value. A district court may not convert a reasonable fee into a discretionary award of praise simply because it regards the representation as outstanding.

This Court’s pre-1970 decisions also reject the notion that fees may be inflated for results alone. In **Missouri v. Jenkins**, 93 U.S. 1 (1876), the Court treated fee entitlement as a matter of actual professional services, not a reward for litigation success as such. The Court’s approach was functional and compensatory: the fee follows the legal work, not a judicial assessment of the lawyer’s stature.

That understanding is reinforced by **Trustees v. Greenough**, 105 U.S. 527 (1882), where the Court approved reimbursement only for expenses and fees reasonably incurred in creating, preserving, or protecting a common fund. See id. at 532-37. Greenough did not authorize an additional premium for excellent advocacy; it grounded compensation in equitable reimbursement. So too in **Central Railroad & Banking Co. v. Pettus**, 113 U.S. 116 (1885), the Court upheld compensation for counsel from a fund because the services benefitted the class and warranted reimbursement, not because the lawyers’ performance deserved a separate enhancement. See id. at 122-23.

The same point appears in **Harrison v. Perea**, 168 U.S. 311 (1897), where the Court recognized that counsel fees awarded from a fund are limited to what is fair compensation for the services rendered. Id. at 323. And in **McGowan v. Parish**, 189 U.S. 287 (1903), the Court again treated fees as compensatory, not punitive or congratulatory. Id. at 294-95. These cases all reflect a consistent pre-1970 rule: the fee award must correspond to the value of the legal services, and must not become a judicial gratuity for good performance.

That rule is especially important under federal fee-shifting statutes. Congress used the word “reasonable” to confine, not expand, judicial discretion. See **Woods**, 312 U.S. at 268-69; **Brown**, 321 U.S. at 188. If quality and results alone could justify an enhancement, then the statutory word “reasonable” would do no work: the fee would rise whenever a judge admired counsel’s skill or approved the outcome. The pre-1970 cases reject that kind of standardless supplement.

Respondents are not arguing that quality and results are irrelevant to a fee award. They are relevant insofar as they help determine the reasonable value of the services performed. But an **enhancement solely on that basis** is something different: it is an extra award added on top of an already reasonable fee because the court believes counsel did an unusually good job. The pre-1970 authorities do not permit that. They require compensation for services, not a multiplier for excellence. See **Brown**, 321 U.S. at 188; **Harrison**, 168 U.S. at 323; **Pettus**, 113 U.S. at 122-23.

Because the federal fee-shifting statute authorizes only a “reasonable” fee, and because pre-1970 precedent limits fee awards to fair compensation for services actually rendered, the judgment below should be reversed insofar as it approved an enhancement based solely on quality of performance and results obtained.
