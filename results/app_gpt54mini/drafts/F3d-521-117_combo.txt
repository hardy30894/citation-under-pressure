**Argument**

The Fourth Amendment permits a limited pat-frisk only when an officer can point to “specific and articulable facts” creating a reasonable belief that the person is armed and dangerous. *Terry v. Ohio*, 392 U.S. 1, 21, 27 (1968). That standard is demanding. An officer’s inchoate “hunch” will not do. *Id.* at 27. On this record, the frisk of Michael Soares was unsupported by the individualized suspicion that *Terry* requires, and the handgun should have been suppressed.

First, the stop for defective headlights did not itself justify a frisk of a passenger. The Supreme Court has long distinguished a lawful stop from a search for weapons. In *Terry*, the Court emphasized that a protective frisk is a separate intrusion that must be independently justified. 392 U.S. at 16-17, 21-24. Soares was a passenger in a car stopped for a traffic violation. Nothing in the initial offense suggested that Soares was armed. The government therefore had to show something more than presence in a stopped car late at night in a high-crime neighborhood.

Second, the officers’ observations of “furtive movements” and agitation were insufficient as a matter of law. The Supreme Court has rejected generalized suspicion untethered to concrete facts. In *Sibron v. New York*, 392 U.S. 40, 62-64 (1968), the Court held that a frisk was invalid where the officer lacked a “reasonable belief” that the suspect was armed and dangerous. Likewise, in *Beck v. Ohio*, 379 U.S. 89, 97 (1964), the Court explained that probable cause cannot rest on “mere suspicion.” The same principle governs here. Soares’s movements and profanity, even if annoying or disrespectful, did not amount to a particularized indication that he posed an immediate threat of violence. See *Henry v. United States*, 361 U.S. 98, 103-04 (1959) (reversing where arrest rested on suspicion rather than facts showing unlawful conduct); *Wong Sun v. United States*, 371 U.S. 471, 479-80 (1963) (Fourth Amendment requires lawful police action at each step).

Third, the government cannot bootstrap a frisk from the officers’ subjective unease. The Court has repeatedly required objective facts. *Terry*, 392 U.S. at 21-22; *Sibron*, 392 U.S. at 62-64. Here, the record shows that Soares complied initially, and his later behavior consisted of rocking, hand motions, and verbal protest after he had already been confronted at gunpoint by officers in an escalating roadside encounter. Those facts do not establish that he was armed. If anything, the officers’ conduct created the tension they then used to justify the frisk.

Fourth, the frisk was especially unjustified because the officers escalated the encounter by ordering Soares out of the car and immediately attempting a pat-down. The Supreme Court has recognized that police may take precautions for their safety, but only within constitutional limits. *Terry*, 392 U.S. at 24-27. The Court in *Agnello v. United States*, 269 U.S. 20, 31 (1925), held that a search incident to arrest must be tied to a lawful arrest; absent such a foundation, the search is invalid. Likewise, *Carroll v. United States*, 267 U.S. 132, 161-62 (1925), permitted a search only upon a showing of probable cause. The frisk here rested on neither probable cause nor a properly established Terry justification. It was therefore unconstitutional.

Fifth, the district court’s ruling cannot be sustained by invoking the general dangers of roadside stops or the presence of multiple officers. The Supreme Court has warned against diluting Fourth Amendment protections by reference to broad police safety concerns alone. *Sibron*, 392 U.S. at 62-64; *Henry*, 361 U.S. at 103-04. Safety concerns must be linked to the particular suspect and supported by concrete observations. Here, the officers observed no bulge, no weapon, no threat, and no reaching toward a waistband after Soares stepped out of the car. The frisk followed from suspicion of attitude, not suspicion of a weapon.

Finally, suppression is required because the gun was the direct fruit of the unconstitutional frisk. *Wong Sun*, 371 U.S. at 484-88. The government obtained the handgun only after detaining and frisking Soares without the individualized basis that the Fourth Amendment demands. The exclusionary rule applies to deter precisely this kind of overreach. *Mapp v. Ohio*, 367 U.S. 643, 655-57 (1961).

Because the officers lacked specific and articulable facts supporting a reasonable belief that Soares was armed and dangerous, the frisk violated the Fourth Amendment. The district court should be reversed, and the motion to suppress granted.
