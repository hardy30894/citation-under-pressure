**Argument**

I. **Guam may proceed under CERCLA § 107 only if no § 113 contribution claim has been triggered.**

CERCLA’s text and structure distinguish sharply between cost recovery under § 107 and contribution under § 113. After *Cooper Industries, Inc. v. Aviall Services, Inc.*, 543 U.S. 157 (2004), Congress’s 1986 amendments supply “two express avenues for contribution.” *Id.* at 167. Section 113(f)(3)(B) provides that a party who has “resolved its liability to the United States or a State for some or all of a response action” in a judicially approved settlement “may seek contribution.” 42 U.S.C. § 9613(f)(3)(B). The Supreme Court has repeatedly treated § 113 as the specific remedial route for a PRP that has settled with the government. *Atlantic Research Corp. v. United States*, 551 U.S. 128, 139 (2007); *United States v. Atlantic Research Corp.*, 551 U.S. 128, 138–39 (2007). And once a PRP is within § 113’s ambit, § 107 cannot be used to evade the shorter limitations period. *Cooper Industries*, 543 U.S. at 166–67; *ASARCO LLC v. Atlantic Richfield Co.*, 866 F.3d 1108, 1121–23 (9th Cir. 2017).

The 2004 Consent Decree did exactly what § 113(f)(3)(B) requires. It was a judicially approved settlement with EPA, and it obligated Guam to close the Ordot Dump, design and install a cover system, and pay a civil penalty. Those substantive obligations resolved Guam’s liability for at least part of the response action. Courts have held that a settlement “resolves” liability when it fixes cleanup obligations, even if it does not use talismanic words of admission. *Trinity Industries, Inc. v. Chicago Bridge & Iron Co.*, 735 F.3d 131, 137–38 (3d Cir. 2013); *ASARCO*, 866 F.3d at 1122–23; *Agere Systems, Inc. v. Advanced Environmental Technology Corp.*, 602 F.3d 204, 228–29 (3d Cir. 2010). The D.C. Circuit correctly recognized that a party “cannot wait to seek contribution until after full performance and Presidential certification” without rendering the statute of limitations meaningless.

II. **Guam’s reliance on § 107 conflicts with Supreme Court precedent and CERCLA’s allocation scheme.**

Guam’s theory would let a settling PRP convert a contribution claim into a cost-recovery claim and thereby extend the limitations period from three years to six. That is irreconcilable with *Cooper Industries*, which explained that CERCLA’s 1986 amendments were enacted to create a contribution remedy where none previously existed. 543 U.S. at 163–67. It is also inconsistent with *Atlantic Research*, which held that § 107 and § 113 are complementary, not interchangeable, and that a PRP seeking reimbursement for amounts paid in response to liability ordinarily must proceed under § 113. 551 U.S. at 139. Lower courts have consistently enforced that distinction. *W.R. Grace & Co.-Conn. v. Zotos International, Inc.*, 559 F.3d 85, 92–93 (2d Cir. 2009); *Solutia, Inc. v. McWane, Inc.*, 672 F.3d 1230, 1236–37 (11th Cir. 2012); *Bernstein v. Bankert*, 733 F.3d 190, 202–05 (7th Cir. 2013).

Guam’s reading would also undercut CERCLA’s settlement incentive. Congress provided that a party who resolves liability in settlement “shall not be liable for claims for contribution regarding matters addressed in the settlement.” 42 U.S.C. § 9613(f)(2). The statutory bargain is straightforward: settle, obtain protection from contribution, and accept the corresponding limitations period. *United States v. Cannons Engineering Corp.*, 899 F.2d 79, 92–93 (1st Cir. 1990); *United States v. Colorado & Eastern Railroad Co.*, 50 F.3d 1530, 1537–38 (10th Cir. 1995). Guam’s proposed workaround would erase that bargain.

III. **The absence of an admission of liability does not save Guam.**

Guam points to the decree’s disclaimer of “any finding or admission of liability.” But courts uniformly hold that a settlement may resolve liability without a formal admission. *Agere Systems*, 602 F.3d at 229; *ASARCO*, 866 F.3d at 1123; *Trinity Industries*, 735 F.3d at 138. What matters is whether the agreement imposes obligations that settle responsibility for response action. The Consent Decree did so. As the D.C. Circuit observed, “[p]arties often expressly refuse to concede liability under a settlement agreement, even while assuming obligations consistent with a finding of liability.” That practical reality is fatal to Guam’s argument.

IV. **Guam’s contrary reading would nullify CERCLA’s limitations period and upset settled law.**

The Supreme Court has repeatedly refused to interpret CERCLA in a way that creates open-ended or redundant remedies. *Cooper Industries*, 543 U.S. at 167–68; *Atlantic Research*, 551 U.S. at 139–40. Guam’s position would allow a PRP that settled years earlier to wait and sue under § 107 long after § 113 expired. That would make the contribution statute “largely superfluous,” contrary to the reasoning in *United States v. Atlantic Research Corp.*, 551 U.S. at 138–39, and the uniform decisions of the circuits. *Bernstein*, 733 F.3d at 202–05; *W.R. Grace*, 559 F.3d at 92–93; *Agere Systems*, 602 F.3d at 228–29.

Because Guam resolved its liability in the 2004 Consent Decree, its exclusive CERCLA remedy was contribution under § 113(f)(3)(B). Its suit filed in 2017 is therefore time-barred. The judgment should be affirmed.
