**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE CONSTITUTION’S QUALIFICATIONS CLAUSES PROVIDE THE EXCLUSIVE AND FIXED STANDARDS FOR ELECTION TO CONGRESS; STATES MAY NOT ADD OR ALTER THEM.**

Article I, § 2, cl. 2, of the Constitution establishes three qualifications for the House: age (25 years), citizenship (seven years), and inhabitancy. Article I, § 3, cl. 3, sets parallel qualifications for the Senate: age (30 years), citizenship (nine years), and inhabitancy. This Court has long recognized that these provisions are “the exclusive list of qualifications” for federal office. Powell v. McCormack, 395 U.S. 486, 522 (1969). In *Powell*, the Court held that the Constitution vested in the Houses of Congress only the power to judge the election returns and qualifications of their members, and that “the Constitution leaves the House without authority to exclude any person, duly elected by his constituents, who meets all the requirements for membership expressly prescribed in the Constitution.” *Id.* at 548. The logic of *Powell* applies with equal force to the States: if Congress cannot add to the constitutional qualifications, a State surely cannot.

The Framers intended uniformity in federal legislative qualifications. Alexander Hamilton wrote in The Federalist No. 60 that “[t]he qualifications of the persons who may . . . be chosen . . . are defined and fixed in the Constitution; and are unalterable by the legislature.” James Madison echoed that the Constitution “had taken care to define the qualifications of members of the House of Representatives, leaving no power in the States to change them.” *The Debates in the Federal Convention of 1787* (Madison’s Notes, Aug. 13, 1787). This historical understanding confirms that the enumerated qualifications are exclusive. *See also Term Limits v. Thornton*, 514 U.S. 779, 800–01 (1995) (holding that the Constitution’s qualifications clauses are exclusive and that states cannot impose additional requirements).

Arkansas Amendment 73 is a direct affront to this settled principle. Section 3 prohibits a person who has served three or more terms in the House (or two or more in the Senate) from even appearing on the ballot. That is, in practical effect, an additional disqualification—a property qualification based on past service. A candidate who is 30 years old, a U.S. citizen for nine years, and an inhabitant of Arkansas is qualified under Article I. Yet Amendment 73 would bar that candidate from the ballot solely because of prior tenure. Such a restriction “undeniably imposes a qualification” for congressional office. *Thornton*, 514 U.S. at 827. The label “ballot access” does not alter the substance: a restriction that permanently excludes a class of otherwise qualified individuals from the electorate’s consideration is a qualification. *See id.* at 836–38 (striking down a similar ballot-access barrier as an impermissible qualification).

The State’s power to regulate the “time, place, and manner” of elections under Article I, § 4, does not authorize such a subterfuge. While States may enact procedural election regulations, they may not “favor or disfavor a class of candidates” in a way that “imposes a substantial burden on the right to vote.” *Anderson v. Celebrezze*, 460 U.S. 780, 788–89 (1983). Amendment 73 does not regulate the mechanics of voting; it permanently excludes an entire category of candidates with proven experience. That is not a manner regulation—it is an additional qualification. *Thornton*, 514 U.S. at 836.

Moreover, *Thornton* squarely rejected the argument that the States’ power to impose term limits derives from the Tenth Amendment or from a “reserved power” to restrict ballot access. The Court held that “the power to add qualifications is not part of the original powers of sovereignty that the Tenth Amendment reserved to the States.” *Thornton*, 514 U.S. at 802. The Constitution’s exclusive enumeration of qualifications “closed the door on any possibility of supplementing that list.” *Id.* at 800.

**II. THE ARKANSAS SUPREME COURT CORRECTLY HELD THAT SECTION 3 IS INSEVERABLE FROM THE ENTIRE AMENDMENT.**

The Arkansas Supreme Court ruled that Section 3 is unconstitutional and that its invalidity taints the entire Amendment because the voters were presented with a single package coupling state and federal term limits. The test for severability under Arkansas law is whether the remainder would have been enacted without the unconstitutional portion. *Combs v. Glenn Falls Ins. Co.*, 237 Ark. 745, 375 S.W.2d 809 (1964). The record here—including the Preamble’s reference to “elected officials” generally and the lack of a separate vote on state-only term limits—demonstrates that the voters could not have intended to approve state term limits alone. The Arkansas Supreme Court’s factual finding of inseverability is entitled to deference. *See Leavitt v. Jane L.*, 518 U.S. 137, 140 (1996) (recognizing that state courts are the final arbiters of severability under state law).

**CONCLUSION**

The Constitution’s qualifications for Congress are exclusive. Arkansas Amendment 73 impermissibly adds a term-based disqualification. The judgment of the Arkansas Supreme Court should be affirmed.
