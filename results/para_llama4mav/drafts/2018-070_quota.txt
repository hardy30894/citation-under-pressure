The Ninth Circuit erred in holding that the Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act (OCSLA) permits the application of state law whenever it pertains to the subject matter of the lawsuit and is not preempted by inconsistent federal law. Instead, the OCSLA allows state law to apply only when there is a gap in the coverage of federal law.

The OCSLA's choice of law provision, 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(2)(A), declares that state law is to be the law of the United States on the outer Continental Shelf "to the extent that they are applicable and not inconsistent" with federal law. The Supreme Court has interpreted this provision to mean that state law is adopted as surrogate federal law on the outer Continental Shelf. Rodrigue v. Aetna Cas. & Sur. Co., 395 U.S. 352, 355 (1969). However, the Court has not directly addressed the question of when state law is "applicable" and "not inconsistent" with federal law.

The Fifth Circuit has developed a test for determining when state law applies under the OCSLA. In Continental Oil Co. v. London S.S. Owners' Mut. Ins. Ass'n, 417 F.2d 1030, 1036 (5th Cir. 1969), the court held that state law is adopted as surrogate federal law only when there is a "necessity to fill a significant void or gap" in federal law. This test has been reaffirmed in subsequent Fifth Circuit decisions, including Union Texas Petroleum Corp. v. PLT Engineering, Inc., 895 F.2d 1043 (5th Cir. 1990), and Barker v. Hercules Offshore, Inc., 713 F.3d 208, 214 (5th Cir. 2013).

The Ninth Circuit's decision in this case departed from the Fifth Circuit's interpretation of the OCSLA. By holding that state law applies whenever it pertains to the subject matter of the lawsuit and is not preempted by inconsistent federal law, the Ninth Circuit effectively eliminated the requirement that there be a gap in federal law before state law can be applied.

This interpretation is inconsistent with the Supreme Court's decision in Rodrigue, which emphasized that the OCSLA was enacted to provide a body of law applicable to the outer Continental Shelf, and that federal law was to be the primary source of law governing the Shelf. 395 U.S. at 355. The Court also noted that the OCSLA's choice of law provision was intended to provide a uniform body of law, rather than to create a patchwork of state and federal laws. Id. at 357.

Furthermore, the Ninth Circuit's interpretation is also at odds with the principle of statutory construction that federal law is presumed to be comprehensive and exclusive in areas where it has been enacted. See, e.g., City of Milwaukee v. Illinois, 451 U.S. 304, 317 (1981); Ecological Rights Found. v. Pac. Gas & Elec. Co., 874 F.3d 1089, 1095 (9th Cir. 2017).

In this case, the federal Fair Labor Standards Act (FLSA) provides a comprehensive statutory scheme governing wage and hour claims. The FLSA's savings clause, 29 U.S.C. § 218(a), expressly allows for more protective state minimum wage and overtime laws, but this does not create a gap in federal law that state law can fill. Rather, it indicates that Congress intended for the FLSA to be the primary source of law governing wage and hour claims, with state law providing additional protections where it is more stringent.

The district court correctly held that the FLSA is a comprehensive statutory scheme that leaves no room for state law to address wage and hour grievances arising on the outer Continental Shelf. See, e.g., Williams v. Brinderson Constructors Inc., No. CV 15-2474-MWF(AGRx), 2015 WL 4747892 (C.D. Cal. Aug. 11, 2015). This conclusion is consistent with the Fifth Circuit's interpretation of the OCSLA and the Supreme Court's decision in Rodrigue.

In conclusion, the OCSLA permits the application of state law only when there is a gap in the coverage of federal law. The Ninth Circuit's decision to the contrary should be reversed, and the district court's dismissal of Newton's claims should be reinstated.
