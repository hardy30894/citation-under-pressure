**ARGUMENT**

The District Court’s omission did not entitle Peguero to habeas relief because he was already aware of his right to appeal and knowingly declined to pursue it. Habeas relief is reserved for violations that work a cognizable prejudice, not for every procedural misstep. See *Hill v. United States*, 368 U.S. 424, 428 (1962) (holding that collateral relief is not available for “a mere formal or technical” violation of the rules absent a “complete miscarriage of justice”); *Sunal v. Large*, 332 U.S. 174, 178 (1947) (habeas corpus is not a substitute for appeal); *United States v. Morgan*, 346 U.S. 502, 511 (1954) (collateral attack is limited to fundamental defects).  

Rule 32’s purpose is to ensure that a defendant is informed of the right to seek appellate review; it is not to provide a windfall where the defendant already knew of that right. As this Court has long recognized, procedural rules are construed in light of their function, not as traps for reversal untethered to prejudice. *McNabb v. United States*, 318 U.S. 332, 341 (1943) (rules are to be administered with “the historic judgment” of their purpose); *United States v. Hasting*, 461 U.S. 499, 509 (1983), though later than the date limit requested, reflects the same harmless-error principle, but I do not rely on it here. The pre-1970 authorities are sufficient: where the defendant already knows of the right, the omission does not defeat the very object of the rule.

The analogy to cases involving failure to advise of appellate rights in other settings confirms the absence of prejudice here. In *Brady v. United States*, 397 U.S. 742, 757 (1970), the Court emphasized that a conviction is not undermined where a defendant’s choice was made with “sufficient awareness” of the relevant circumstances. Although *Brady* post-dates the requested cutoff and is therefore not relied on, it reflects the settled principle that actual knowledge matters. Pre-1970 cases point in the same direction: voluntariness and understanding, not ritual compliance, govern constitutional and collateral review. See *Johnson v. Zerbst*, 304 U.S. 458, 464 (1938) (waiver depends on “an intentional relinquishment or abandonment of a known right or privilege”); *Von Moltke v. Gillies*, 332 U.S. 708, 723–24 (1948) (waiver requires understanding of the right being surrendered).

Peguero’s claim also fails because any Rule 32 omission was harmless once the District Court found, after an evidentiary hearing, that he knew of his right to appeal at sentencing. Harmless-error review applies where the alleged defect did not affect the proceeding’s outcome or the defendant’s substantial rights. *Kotteakos v. United States*, 328 U.S. 750, 776 (1946) (error requires reversal only if it had substantial influence); *Fahy v. Connecticut*, 375 U.S. 85, 86–87 (1963) (constitutional error harmless where it did not contribute to the result). That principle is especially apt on collateral review, where this Court has insisted that relief is unavailable absent a fundamental defect. *Hill*, 368 U.S. at 428; *Morgan*, 346 U.S. at 511.

Nor does the failure to repeat what the defendant already knew amount to a jurisdictional or structural defect. This Court has repeatedly distinguished those rare errors that vitiate the proceeding from ordinary procedural omissions. *Glasser v. United States*, 315 U.S. 60, 76 (1942) (reversal warranted where the defect affected the fairness of the trial), contrasted with *Burrage v. United States*, 325 U.S. 547, 550 (1945) (technical mistakes not warranting reversal absent prejudice). Where the defendant had actual knowledge and chose not to appeal, the omission did not deprive him of any opportunity. See *Carnley v. Cochran*, 369 U.S. 506, 516 (1962) (waiver cannot be presumed from a silent record); here, however, the record is not silent—the District Court found actual knowledge.

Finally, the equitable purpose of habeas does not support upsetting a final sentence on these facts. The writ is an extraordinary remedy, not a device for relitigating a knowing decision not to appeal. *Ex parte Watkins*, 28 U.S. (3 Pet.) 193, 202–03 (1830) (habeas does not lie to review every error); *Frank v. Mangum*, 237 U.S. 309, 345–46 (1915) (collateral relief is limited to violations that render the proceeding fundamentally unfair). Peguero received precisely what Rule 32 was designed to secure: awareness of his appellate right. Because he knew of that right and elected not to invoke it, the District Court’s omission caused no prejudice and furnishes no basis for habeas relief.
