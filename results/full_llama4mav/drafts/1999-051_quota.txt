The Sixth Circuit's decision in Carpenter's case is flawed because it allows a procedurally defaulted claim of ineffective assistance of counsel to serve as cause to excuse the procedural default of another habeas claim. This Court has established that a procedurally defaulted claim cannot serve as cause to excuse another procedural default unless the defaulted claim is itself excused. See Murray v. Carrier, 477 U.S. 478, 488-89 (1986). In other words, a petitioner must demonstrate cause and prejudice for the procedural default of the ineffective-assistance-of-counsel claim before it can serve as cause to excuse the default of another claim.

The Sixth Circuit's decision contravenes this principle, as it permitted Carpenter's procedurally defaulted ineffective-assistance-of-counsel claim to excuse the default of his sufficiency-of-the-evidence claim without requiring cause and prejudice for the default of the ineffective-assistance claim. See Edwards v. Carpenter, 529 U.S. 446 (2000) (requiring a showing of cause and prejudice for a procedurally defaulted ineffective-assistance claim). This approach is inconsistent with the Court's holding in Coleman v. Thompson, 501 U.S. 722, 753-54 (1991), which established that a petitioner must demonstrate cause and prejudice for a procedural default before it can be excused.

Furthermore, allowing a procedurally defaulted ineffective-assistance-of-counsel claim to serve as cause would undermine the principles of procedural default and the interests of finality and comity that underlie them. See Wainwright v. Sykes, 433 U.S. 72, 88-91 (1977). As the Court noted in Engle v. Isaac, 456 U.S. 107, 128-29 (1982), the procedural default doctrine is designed to promote the finality of state court judgments and to ensure that federal habeas corpus review is not used as a substitute for direct appeal.

In Martinez v. Ryan, 566 U.S. 1 (2012), the Court created a narrow exception to the general rule that a procedurally defaulted ineffective-assistance-of-counsel claim cannot serve as cause to excuse another procedural default. However, this exception is limited to cases where the state bars the petitioner from raising the ineffective-assistance claim on direct appeal and the claim is substantial. See id. at 14. Carpenter's case does not fall within this exception, as he was represented by new counsel on appeal and had the opportunity to raise his ineffective-assistance claim.

The Court's decision in Strickland v. Washington, 466 U.S. 668 (1984), established the standard for evaluating ineffective-assistance-of-counsel claims. However, Carpenter's procedurally defaulted ineffective-assistance claim cannot be evaluated under this standard unless the default is excused. See id. at 687-88.

In United States v. Frady, 456 U.S. 152 (1982), the Court emphasized the importance of demonstrating cause and prejudice for a procedural default. Carpenter's failure to demonstrate cause and prejudice for the default of his ineffective-assistance claim precludes its use as cause to excuse the default of his sufficiency-of-the-evidence claim. See id. at 167-68.

The Sixth Circuit's decision is also inconsistent with the Court's holding in McCleskey v. Zant, 499 U.S. 467 (1991), which established that a petitioner must demonstrate cause and prejudice for a procedural default before it can be excused. See id. at 493-94.

In conclusion, the Sixth Circuit's decision allowing Carpenter's procedurally defaulted ineffective-assistance-of-counsel claim to serve as cause to excuse the default of his sufficiency-of-the-evidence claim is contrary to established precedent. The Court should reverse the Sixth Circuit's decision and require Carpenter to demonstrate cause and prejudice for the procedural default of his ineffective-assistance claim before it can be used to excuse the default of another claim.
