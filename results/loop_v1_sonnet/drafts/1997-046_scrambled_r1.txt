**ARGUMENT**

**I. Woodard Has No Life or Liberty Interest in Clemency Proceedings Protected by the Due Process Clause.**

Executive clemency has never been treated as an extension of the judicial process to which due process attaches. From the founding of the Republic, this Court has described clemency as an act of sovereign grace, wholly distinct from the adjudication of guilt. In *United States v. Wilson*, 32 U.S. (7 Pet.) 150, 160 (1833), Chief Justice Marshall explained that "[a] pardon is an act of grace, proceeding from the power entrusted with the execution of the laws, which exempts the individual on whom it is bestowed from the punishment the law inflicts for a crime he has committed." That characterization has never wavered. In *Ex parte Garland*, 71 U.S. (4 Wall.) 333, 380 (1866), this Court likewise held that the clemency power extends to every offense and may be exercised before legal proceedings are taken, during their pendency, or after conviction and judgment—confirming that clemency operates entirely outside the ordinary sequence of trial and appeal that due process governs.

The discretionary, non-adjudicatory character of clemency was reaffirmed in *Ex parte Grossman*, 267 U.S. 87, 120–21 (1925), where Chief Justice Taft explained that the pardoning power may be exercised "before trial, during trial or after trial," and "is not to be fettered by legislative restrictions." Nothing in that description suggests that clemency functions as a further stage of adjudication subject to the procedural protections that attach to trial or appeal.

Due process protections attach only where the government threatens to deprive an individual of an interest to which he is already entitled by law. See *Cafeteria & Restaurant Workers Union v. McElroy*, 367 U.S. 886, 895 (1961) (the process due depends on "the precise nature of the government function involved as well as . . . the private interest that has been affected by governmental action"). A capital prisoner's expectation of clemency is not such an entitlement; it is, as *Wilson*, *Garland*, and *Grossman* make clear, wholly dependent on executive grace. Because clemency does not adjudicate guilt or redetermine a sentence already lawfully imposed, but merely asks whether mercy should mitigate a judgment that due process has already validated, no protected liberty interest arises from the clemency process itself. The Sixth Circuit therefore erred in extending due process scrutiny—however "minimal" or "barely perceptible"—to a proceeding this Court has consistently placed outside the adjudicatory system.

**II. Because Participation in the Clemency Interview Is Voluntary, It Does Not Compel Self-Incrimination in Violation of the Fifth Amendment.**

The Fifth Amendment forbids only *compelled* self-incrimination. As this Court held in *Malloy v. Hogan*, 378 U.S. 1, 8 (1964), the privilege "is fulfilled only when the person is guaranteed the right 'to remain silent unless he chooses to speak in the unfettered exercise of his own will, and to suffer no penalty . . . for such silence.'" Ohio's procedure does precisely that. A death-sentenced inmate may request a personal interview or may decline one; nothing in the regulation compels him to answer any question he does not wish to answer, and no penalty attaches to silence.

Where disclosure is voluntary, no Fifth Amendment violation can occur. See *Rogers v. United States*, 340 U.S. 367, 371 (1951) (privilege protects against compulsion, not voluntary disclosure); *Blau v. United States*, 340 U.S. 159, 161 (1950) (privilege applies only where compelled answers would themselves support a conviction or link the witness to criminal activity). The privilege is a personal one that a witness may elect to invoke or forgo; it is not self-executing. *Brown v. Walker*, 161 U.S. 591, 594–97 (1896). This principle is confirmed by *United States v. Sullivan*, 274 U.S. 259, 263–64 (1927), where Justice Holmes explained that a person confronted with a request for information must claim the privilege as to particular questions rather than refuse to participate altogether—precisely the choice Ohio's regulation preserves for Woodard.

Nor can Woodard manufacture a constitutional violation from speculation about hypothetical future prosecutions. This Court has cautioned against extending the privilege to remote and contingent risks. *Ullmann v. United States*, 350 U.S. 422, 430–31 (1956) (privilege does not protect against speculative future harm where no realistic threat of prosecution exists). Woodard's own complaint disclaims any effect on his existing convictions or sentence, and Ohio's regulation places no penalty on his silence at the interview. Any risk of self-incrimination is thus wholly theoretical, precisely the sort of contingency this Court has declined to remedy in advance.

Because the clemency interview is optional, and no adverse consequence follows from declining it or from limiting one's answers, Ohio's regulation does not compel Woodard to be a witness against himself. The Sixth Circuit's contrary holding should be reversed.
