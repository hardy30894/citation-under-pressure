ARGUMENT

I. Entitlement to an earlier filing date under 35 U.S.C. § 120 depends on the sufficiency of each application's own disclosure, not on later clarification.

A patentee seeking the benefit of an earlier filing date bears the burden of showing that every intervening application in the chain independently satisfies the disclosure requirements of the patent laws. This principle traces to the earliest patent-continuity decisions. In Godfrey v. Eames, 68 U.S. (1 Wall.) 317 (1864), the Supreme Court held that an amendment or subsequent filing "constitutes a part of the original application" only insofar as it embraces matter already disclosed, not matter supplied for the first time by hindsight. 68 U.S. at 326. The same rule was applied to defeat priority in Alexander Milburn Co. v. Davis-Bournonville Co., 270 U.S. 390 (1926), where the Court explained that a filed application is effective only "to the extent of what is really disclosed," id. at 401, and not to what an inventor merely intended or later explained he meant. The '838 and '768 applications must therefore stand or fall on what they themselves disclosed, examined at the time each was filed—not on what the '880 application's preliminary amendment later revealed.

The Court of Customs and Patent Appeals applied this same principle in the modern written-description context. In In re Ruschig, 379 F.2d 990 (C.C.P.A. 1967), the court held that an applicant must show he was "in possession" of the invention at the time the application was filed, and that the specification must describe the invention "in such full, clear, concise, and exact terms" as to satisfy that requirement without resort to extrinsic clarification. Id. at 996. Likewise, in In re Sus, 306 F.2d 494 (C.C.P.A. 1962), the court measured sufficiency of disclosure "as of the filing date," refusing to credit disclosure that depended on information supplied afterward. Id. at 504. These authorities establish that the '838 and '768 applications' incorporation statements must be evaluated on their own four corners as filed, without benefit of the transmittal sheet and preliminary amendment that later accompanied the '880 application.

II. The Board erred in transplanting Harari I's narrow "reasonable examiner" rule, crafted for the initial filing stage, onto the intervening continuation applications.

This Court's prior decision in Harari I addressed a discrete circumstance: an applicant's contemporaneous submission—at the moment of filing—of a transmittal sheet and preliminary amendment that resolved an ambiguity in the copied incorporation language. No such curative papers accompanied the '838 or '768 applications. Extending the "reasonable examiner" standard to those intervening filings converts a narrow rule about contemporaneous filing-stage evidence into a license to cure ambiguous incorporation language years later, by reference to documents that did not exist when the '838 and '768 applications were filed. That result cannot be squared with General Electric Co. v. Wabash Appliance Corp., 304 U.S. 364 (1938), where the Supreme Court insisted that a specification must give the public certainty "at the time" it issues, not merely upon later explanation, because "the specification and claims of a patent, particularly if the invention be at all a complicated one, are... to be read in the light of the circumstances surrounding the case." Id. at 369. If an incorporation statement is ambiguous when filed, and no clarifying document accompanies that particular filing, the statement cannot retroactively be deemed clear simply because a later, unrelated application in the chain happens to resolve the ambiguity.

III. Because Harari cannot establish unbroken compliance with § 120, the '880 application is entitled at most to the priority date of the '708 application.

The '708 application unquestionably discloses the '579 application. But the '838 and '768 applications, which intervene between the '566 application and the '708 application, do not—on their own, unaided record—clearly identify the '579 application. Under Godfrey and Milburn, a broken link in the chain of disclosure defeats the claim to the earliest date; the chain is only as strong as its weakest disclosure. 68 U.S. at 326; 270 U.S. at 401. Accordingly, the '880 application is entitled only to the December 20, 1996 filing date of the '708 application.

IV. Hollmer's conception predates that corrected priority date and entitles him to judgment.

An interference is resolved by determining which party first conceived a "definite and permanent idea of the complete and operative invention." Christie v. Seybold, 55 F. 69, 76 (6th Cir. 1893); accord Mergenthaler v. Scudder, 11 App. D.C. 264, 276 (D.C. Cir. 1897) (conception requires an idea "so clearly defined in the inventor's mind" that it could be reduced to practice without further invention). Once the '880 application's effective date is correctly fixed at December 20, 1996, Hollmer's earlier-established conception evidence precedes Harari's corrected priority date, entitling Hollmer to judgment as the prior inventor.

CONCLUSION

For these reasons, the Board's decision should be reversed and judgment entered awarding priority to Hollmer.
