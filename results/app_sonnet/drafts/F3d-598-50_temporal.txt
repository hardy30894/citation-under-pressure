**ARGUMENT**

**I. The District Court Abused Its Discretion by Admitting Opinion Testimony from the ADAs and the Lieutenant on Credibility and Probable Cause**

A. *Credibility determinations belong exclusively to the jury.* It is axiomatic that the assessment of a witness's truthfulness is committed to the jury alone, and that a witness — lay or otherwise — may not usurp that function. In *Quercia v. United States*, 289 U.S. 466 (1933), the Supreme Court reversed a conviction because the trial judge, by commenting on a defendant's demeanor, effectively told the jury which witness to believe, holding that such an intrusion into the jury's fact-finding domain could not stand. If even a presiding judge may not tell a jury whom to believe, two Assistant District Attorneys and a police lieutenant certainly may not do so from the witness stand. Yet that is precisely what occurred here: the ADAs and the lieutenant were permitted to testify, in substance, that Officers Ramos and Rivera were believable and that Appellants were not. This Court has long recognized that the jury's role in "select[ing] from among conflicting inferences and conclusions that which it considers most reasonable" is inviolable. *Tennant v. Peoria & Pekin Union Ry. Co.*, 321 U.S. 29, 35 (1944). Likewise, in *Lavender v. Kurn*, 327 U.S. 645, 653 (1946), the Court reaffirmed that resolving conflicts in testimony is a function reserved to the jury, not to be preempted by the say-so of any witness, however official his title. Allowing government witnesses to vouch for the arresting officers' honesty told the jury what result to reach on the single most contested question at trial.

B. *Probable cause is a legal conclusion, not a subject for witness opinion.* The witnesses were also permitted to testify that probable cause existed to arrest and charge Appellants. But whether probable cause existed is not a matter of opinion for a witness to supply; it is, at most, a mixed question of law and fact for the court and jury under proper instruction. As the Supreme Court explained more than a century ago, "whether the circumstances alleged are true and existed, is a matter of fact of which the jury are to judge; but whether, supposing them true, they amount to probable cause, is a question of law." *Stacy v. Emery*, 97 U.S. 642, 645 (1878). The determination turns on an objective standard applied to the facts known to the officer at the time, not on a third party's after-the-fact assessment. *Carroll v. United States*, 267 U.S. 132, 162 (1925); *Brinegar v. United States*, 338 U.S. 160, 175–76 (1949); *Draper v. United States*, 358 U.S. 307, 313 (1959). By permitting the ADAs and the lieutenant to render their own conclusions on this legal question, the District Court effectively allowed them to instruct the jury on the law and to answer the ultimate issue in the case — a role reserved for the court and the jury alone.

C. *These errors were not harmless.* Erroneously admitted testimony requires reversal unless it can be said "with fair assurance ... that the judgment was not substantially swayed by the error." *Kotteakos v. United States*, 328 U.S. 750, 765 (1946). No such assurance is possible here. The two issues on which the improper testimony centered — Ramos's and Rivera's credibility and the existence of probable cause — were the two issues on which the entire case turned. Testimony from ostensibly neutral government officials directly resolving both questions could not have failed to substantially influence the verdict. Reversal and a new trial are required.

**II. If Retried, the Jury Should Be Correctly Instructed on Obstructing Governmental Administration and Punitive Damages**

A. *Obstructing governmental administration requires a lawful arrest.* Higgenbottom was charged with obstructing governmental administration for allegedly interfering with Cameron's arrest. But an individual cannot be guilty of obstructing an arrest that is not itself lawful. The Supreme Court held long ago that where an arrest is unlawful, a person may lawfully resist it, because the obstruction offense presupposes lawful official action. *John Bad Elk v. United States*, 177 U.S. 529, 535 (1900) (recognizing that where an arrest is unlawful, the person "might resist the illegal attempt to arrest him"). It follows that the jury should have been instructed that it must first determine whether Cameron's arrest was lawful before it could find that Higgenbottom obstructed it. The District Court's failure to give such an instruction improperly permitted the jury to convict-in-effect Higgenbottom without resolving the predicate legal question.

B. *A punitive damages instruction was warranted.* Appellants' version of events — that Ramos and Rivera fabricated a story to justify baseless arrests and a malicious prosecution — would permit a jury to find the kind of malicious or wanton conduct for which punitive damages are traditionally allowed. See *Missouri Pacific Ry. Co. v. Humes*, 115 U.S. 512, 521 (1885) (exemplary damages proper where the wrong was "malicious, wanton, or oppressive"). Because a reasonable jury crediting Appellants' testimony could find such conduct, the District Court erred in declining to instruct on punitive damages, and an instruction of the kind Appellants requested should be given on remand.
