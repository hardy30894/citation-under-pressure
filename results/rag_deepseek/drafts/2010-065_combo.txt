**ARGUMENT**

The judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed because petitioner Carol Anne Bond, as a private individual, lacks standing to challenge her conviction under 18 U.S.C. § 229 on the ground that the statute violates the Tenth Amendment. The Tenth Amendment is a structural provision that reserves powers to the States; it does not confer individual rights that a private party may invoke.

**I. The Tenth Amendment Protects State Sovereignty, Not Individual Rights**

The Tenth Amendment provides that “[t]he powers not delegated to the United States by the Constitution, nor prohibited by it to the States, are reserved to the States respectively, or to the people.” This language “states but a truism that all is retained which has not been surrendered.” *United States v. Darby*, 312 U.S. 100, 124 (1941). The Amendment is a “declaration of the relationship between the national government and the states,” not a source of individual entitlement. *Tennessee Electric Power Co. v. TVA*, 306 U.S. 118, 143–44 (1939). In *Tennessee Electric*, private utility companies challenged federal power sales as a Tenth Amendment violation. This Court held that the companies “absent the states or their officers, have no standing in this suit to raise any question under the [Tenth] Amendment.” *Id.* at 144. That holding controls here.

**II. A Private Party May Not Assert the Rights of a State**

It is a “fundamental principle that a litigant may only assert his own constitutional rights or immunities.” *United States v. Raines*, 362 U.S. 17, 22 (1960). The Tenth Amendment safeguards the “residuary and inviolable sovereignty” of the States. *The Federalist No. 39* (J. Madison). A private defendant cannot “vicariously” assert a State’s interest in federalism. *Barrows v. Jackson*, 346 U.S. 249, 255 (1953). In *Tileston v. Ullman*, 318 U.S. 44, 46 (1943), this Court dismissed an appeal where the appellant “asserted no right under the Constitution of the United States” but only the rights of others. Similarly, Bond asserts only the State’s interest in prosecuting her conduct under local law. She has no personal stake in the allocation of power between the federal government and Pennsylvania.

**III. Bond’s Conviction Does Not Inflict a Cognizable Injury on Her Under the Tenth Amendment**

Standing requires an “injury in fact” that is “concrete and particularized” and “actual or imminent.” *Massachusetts v. Mellon*, 262 U.S. 447, 488 (1923). Bond’s injury—a federal criminal conviction—flows from her own violation of a valid federal statute. The Tenth Amendment does not create a personal right to be free from federal prosecution. *See Doremus v. Board of Education*, 342 U.S. 429, 434 (1952) (rejecting taxpayer standing to assert Tenth Amendment claim). Bond’s claim is a “generalized grievance” about the proper scope of federal power, which is “plainly not a case or controversy within the meaning of Article III.” *Ex parte Levitt*, 302 U.S. 633, 634 (1937) (per curiam).

**IV. The Court of Appeals Correctly Applied Settled Precedent**

The Third Circuit relied on *Tennessee Electric* and the majority of circuits that deny private-party Tenth Amendment standing. That conclusion is consistent with this Court’s repeated insistence that “one to whom application of a statute is constitutional will not be heard to attack the statute on the ground that impliedly it might also be taken as applying to other persons or other situations in which its application might be unconstitutional.” *United States v. Raines*, 362 U.S. at 21. Bond’s conduct—using toxic chemicals to poison a rival—falls squarely within the statute’s core prohibition. She cannot complain that the statute might, in other applications, trench on state prerogatives.

**V. The Treaty Power Provides an Independent Basis for the Statute**

Even if Bond had standing, the statute is a valid exercise of Congress’s power under the Necessary and Proper Clause to implement the Chemical Weapons Convention. *Missouri v. Holland*, 252 U.S. 416, 432 (1920), held that a treaty-implementing statute need not be authorized by a specific enumerated power. The Treaty Power is “not limited by the Tenth Amendment.” *Id.* at 433. Bond’s federalism challenge therefore fails on the merits. But the standing defect is dispositive.

**Conclusion**

For these reasons, the judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
