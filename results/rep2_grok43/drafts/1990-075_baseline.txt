ARGUMENT

I. THE CONTROLLED SUBSTANCES ACT SUPPLIES AN INTELLIGIBLE PRINCIPLE GUIDING THE ATTORNEY GENERAL’S TEMPORARY SCHEDULING AUTHORITY.

Article I, Section 1 vests “All legislative Powers herein granted” in Congress. U.S. Const. art. I, § 1. Congress may nevertheless obtain assistance from the Executive “by laying down by legislative act an intelligible principle to which the person or body authorized to [exercise the delegated authority] is directed to conform.” J.W. Hampton, Jr., & Co. v. United States, 276 U.S. 394, 409 (1928); Mistretta v. United States, 488 U.S. 361, 372 (1989). The Controlled Substances Act, 21 U.S.C. § 811(h), meets this standard.

Section 811(h) authorizes temporary placement of a substance on Schedule I only upon a finding that it “pose[s] an imminent hazard to the public safety.” The Attorney General must consider three statutory factors: the substance’s history and current pattern of abuse, the scope, duration, and significance of that abuse, and the risk it poses to public health. 21 U.S.C. § 811(h)(3) (incorporating § 811(c)(4)–(6)). Temporary scheduling expires after one year (extendable by six months), and the Attorney General must publish notice and solicit comments from the Secretary of Health and Human Services. Id. § 811(h)(2), (4). These constraints “meaningfully constrain[]” the delegated authority. Mistretta, 488 U.S. at 372.

The same intelligible-principle framework has sustained far broader delegations involving criminal sanctions. See, e.g., Yakus v. United States, 321 U.S. 414, 426 (1944) (price-control statute); United States v. Grimaud, 220 U.S. 506, 516–17 (1911) (Forest Service regulations). The two 1935 decisions striking down delegations involved statutes containing “no standard whatever.” Panama Refining Co. v. Ryan, 293 U.S. 388, 430 (1935); A.L.A. Schechter Poultry Corp. v. United States, 295 U.S. 495, 541–42 (1935). Section 811(h) supplies concrete criteria and temporal limits absent from those enactments.

II. THE ATTORNEY GENERAL LAWFULLY SUBDELEGATED TEMPORARY SCHEDULING AUTHORITY TO THE DEA ADMINISTRATOR.

Congress expressly authorized the Attorney General to “delegate any of his functions under this subchapter” to “any officer or employee of the Department of Justice.” 21 U.S.C. § 871(a). The Attorney General’s regulation assigning temporary-scheduling functions to the DEA Administrator, 28 C.F.R. § 0.100(b), therefore rests on statutory authority. Subdelegation of this character has long been sustained. See, e.g., United States v. Giordano, 416 U.S. 505, 512–13 (1974); Fleming v. Mohawk Wrecking & Lumber Co., 331 U.S. 111, 121–22 (1947).

Because the statute itself supplies an intelligible principle, the subdelegation raises no additional constitutional concern. The Third Circuit correctly rejected petitioners’ facial challenge.
