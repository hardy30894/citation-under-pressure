**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Bankruptcy Code exempts only funds that function as retirement savings for the debtor at the time the petition is filed.**

Section 522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12) exempt "retirement funds" that are held in tax-favored accounts. Whether an asset qualifies for that exemption depends on its character at the commencement of the bankruptcy case, not on what it once was in someone else's hands. This Court has long recognized that a debtor's exemption depends on the actual legal character of the property under the law governing it, not on its history or source. See *Lockwood v. Exchange Bank*, 190 U.S. 294 (1903). Applying that principle here, the inherited IRA must be evaluated as what it is in the Clarks' hands on the petition date—a fund that must be drawn down within a fixed term, without regard to Heidi Heffron-Clark's retirement. It is not, and cannot become, a retirement account "of" the debtor merely because it once belonged to her mother.

This Court's decision in *Wetmore v. Markoe*, 196 U.S. 68 (1904), confirms that bankruptcy's protective provisions are construed by reference to their actual operation for the debtor before the court, not by reference to the status the underlying obligation or asset held in a prior party's hands. The same principle governs here: the inherited IRA's status must be judged by how it functions for the Clarks, not by how the original account functioned for Ruth Heffron.

**II. The functional character of the asset, not its label, controls.**

This Court has repeatedly instructed that the treatment of a financial interest turns on its substance and function, not its formal name. In *Corliss v. Bowers*, 281 U.S. 376 (1930), the Court looked past the formal structure of a trust arrangement to the actual power and benefit retained by the party in question, rather than resting on the label attached to the interest. The word "IRA" in "inherited IRA" is a similar formal label; it does not change the fact that, functionally, the account no longer serves a retirement purpose for its current holder. Likewise, in *Helvering v. Horst*, 311 U.S. 112 (1940), this Court explained that "the power to dispose of income is the equivalent of ownership of it," *id.* at 118, underscoring that substantive control and use—not nominal characterization—determine an asset's true character. The inherited IRA should receive the same functional scrutiny: it is, in economic reality, a time-limited, freely spendable fund that must be distributed on an accelerated schedule wholly divorced from Heidi's own retirement date.

This Court's decision in *Holden v. Stratton*, 198 U.S. 202 (1905), confirms that the exempt character of a fund depends on whether it continues to serve its protective purpose in the debtor's own hands. There, the Court tied the exemption of a life-insurance policy to its ongoing function of providing for the insured's dependents. Similarly, in *Myers v. Matley*, 318 U.S. 622 (1943), this Court examined whether an annuity contract actually served the protective function required for exemption, rather than accepting its formal designation. Here, by contrast, the inherited IRA serves no analogous ongoing retirement-protective function for the Clarks; the very tax rules governing such accounts compel current, not future, consumption.

**III. Exempting inherited IRAs would contradict, not serve, the purpose of the bankruptcy exemption scheme.**

Exemptions exist to secure a "fresh start" for the debtor who has fallen on hard times—not to shelter windfalls from creditors. As this Court explained in *Local Loan Co. v. Hunt*, 292 U.S. 234, 244 (1934), one of the Bankruptcy Act's primary purposes is "to give to the honest but unfortunate debtor…a new opportunity in life and a clear field for future effort, unhampered by the pressure and discouragement of preexisting debt." That purpose is served by protecting funds a debtor has actually set aside, through sacrifice, for his or her own retirement. It is not served by shielding an inheritance that the debtor never contributed to, has no obligation to preserve, and must currently spend down by statute. Treating such funds as exempt "retirement funds" would extend the exemption beyond its purpose to protect ordinary creditors' legitimate expectations, a result this Court has cautioned against when a literal or formalistic reading would produce consequences at odds with the statute's aim. See *United States v. American Trucking Ass'ns*, 310 U.S. 534, 543 (1940).

For these reasons, the inherited IRA held by the Clarks is not "retirement funds" within the meaning of §522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12), and the judgment of the Seventh Circuit should be affirmed.
