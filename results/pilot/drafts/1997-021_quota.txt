**ARGUMENT**

I. THE NLRB’S “GOOD-FAITH REASONABLE DOUBT” STANDARD FOR EMPLOYEE POLLS IS IRRATIONAL AND CONTRARY TO THE NATIONAL LABOR RELATIONS ACT.

The National Labor Relations Act (NLRA) does not prohibit employers from testing employee sentiment regarding union representation. Indeed, Congress intended to preserve the employer’s right to act on its good-faith beliefs about the union’s continued majority support—so long as it does not engage in coercion or unfair labor practices. Yet the NLRB’s current “good-faith reasonable doubt” standard renders this right illusory. By requiring an employer to possess *objective indications sufficient to justify withdrawal of recognition* before conducting a poll, the Board effectively eliminates the utility of polling altogether. As the Board itself acknowledges, polling is “potentially disruptive and unsettling,” Texas Petrochemicals Corp., 296 N.L.R.B. 1057, 1061 (1989), and thus should be permitted only when it serves a legitimate purpose. But under the Board’s rule, an employer may only poll when it already has enough evidence to justify withdrawal—making the poll redundant and defeating its very purpose.

This circular logic is not only irrational but also inconsistent with the NLRA’s statutory framework. The Act grants employers the right to refrain from bargaining with a union that no longer represents a majority of employees. See *Fall River Dyeing & Finishing Corp. v. NLRB*, 482 U.S. 27, 37–41, 107 S.Ct. 2225, 2232–35, 96 L.Ed.2d 22 (1987). The Board’s standard, however, forces employers to choose between two unlawful alternatives: (1) bargain with a union they believe lacks majority support, or (2) conduct a poll that the Board deems unlawful unless the employer already has sufficient evidence to justify withdrawal. This creates a Catch-22 that violates the Act’s intent.

The Board’s own rationale for maintaining this standard is internally inconsistent. It claims that RM elections are the preferred method for testing union support, yet applies the *same* evidentiary threshold to polling—despite the fact that polls lack the procedural safeguards of Board-supervised elections. See *Texas Petrochemicals*, 296 N.L.R.B. at 1061. If the Board truly believes polling is more disruptive, then it should impose a *higher* evidentiary standard for polling than for RM elections, not the same one. But the Board does not. Instead, it permits employers to withdraw recognition without any polling or election—*without any procedural safeguards at all*—but bars them from polling unless they meet the same standard required for withdrawal. This is not a rational balance; it is a regulatory absurdity.

Courts of appeals have recognized this flaw. In *A.W. Thompson, Inc. v. NLRB*, 651 F.2d 1139, 1145 (5th Cir. 1981), the court held that an employer may poll employees “if there is other substantial, objective evidence of a loss of union support,” even if that evidence is insufficient to justify withdrawal. The court emphasized that polling is a “useful and legitimate tool” for employers in “sincere doubt” about a union’s majority status. *Id.* at 1145. The same conclusion was reached in *Mingtree Restaurant, Inc. v. NLRB*, 736 F.2d 1295, 1299 (9th Cir. 1984), where the court affirmed that an employer may poll if it has “substantial, objective evidence of a loss of union support,” even if that evidence does not independently justify withdrawal. *Id.* The Sixth Circuit echoed this in *Thomas Industries, Inc. v. NLRB*, 687 F.2d 863, 867 (6th Cir. 1982), holding that an employer may poll when it has “substantial, objective evidence of a loss of union support,” even if that evidence is not sufficient to justify withdrawal. *Id.* These decisions reflect a sound understanding of the NLRA: polling is a legitimate mechanism for resolving uncertainty, and the standard for its use should be lower than the standard for withdrawal.

The Board’s contrary rule is not only irrational but also unsupported by precedent. In *Fall River Dyeing*, the Supreme Court recognized that the presumption of majority status is rebuttable, but it did not define the standard for rebuttal. *482 U.S. at 41 n.8, 107 S.Ct. at 2235 n.8*. The Court did not suggest that an employer must have evidence sufficient to justify withdrawal before testing the union’s support. Nor did it endorse the Board’s current circular standard. Indeed, the Board’s own decision in *Texas Petrochemicals* admits that the standard “renders polling only marginally useful,” yet it refuses to change it. 296 N.L.R.B. at 1063. This is not a reasonable exercise of agency discretion—it is arbitrary and capricious.

II. THE NLRB’S FINDING THAT ALLENTOWN MACK LACKED GOOD-FAITH REASONABLE DOUBT IS NOT SUPPORTED BY SUBSTANTIAL EVIDENCE.

The Board’s conclusion that Allentown Mack lacked “objective reasonable doubt” rests on a selective and erroneous interpretation of the record. The ALJ and the Board refused to count statements from seven employees who expressed opposition to the union, despite the fact that these statements were made by employees who were part of the bargaining unit and were made in the context of the company’s transition.

First, the Board improperly excluded the statements of Pete McArthur, Dennis Wehr, and Randy Zoltack, who were not in the unit on January 25, 1991—the date of the company’s letter refusing recognition. But the Board’s use of that date as a cutoff is arbitrary. The company’s decision to conduct the poll was made *after* January 25, and the employees’ statements were made *before* the sale. The ALJ found that these employees had expressed opposition to the union during the transition period, and their statements were relevant to the company’s good-faith belief. The Board’s refusal to consider them undermines the factual basis for its conclusion.
