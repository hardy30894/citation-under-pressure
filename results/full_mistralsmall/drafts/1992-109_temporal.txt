**ARGUMENT**

I. The Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment requires a trial court to ensure that a defendant who seeks to waive counsel or plead guilty possesses the capacity for a "reasoned choice" among the available alternatives. *Godinez v. Moran*, 509 U.S. 389, 399-400 (1993) (citing *Harding v. Lewis*, 834 F.2d 853, 856 (9th Cir.1987)). This standard is higher than the competency to stand trial standard articulated in *Drope v. Missouri*, 420 U.S. 162, 172 (1975), which requires only a "rational and factual understanding" of the proceedings and the ability to assist counsel. *Pate v. Robinson*, 383 U.S. 375, 387 (1966); *Dusky v. United States*, 362 U.S. 402, 402 (1960) (per curiam). The Ninth Circuit correctly applied this heightened standard in reversing the district court’s denial of habeas relief. *Moran v. Godinez*, 972 F.2d 263, 270-71 (9th Cir.1992).

II. The trial court’s failure to conduct a competency hearing sua sponte violated due process where substantial evidence raised a good-faith doubt as to Moran’s competency to waive counsel and plead guilty. *Pate*, 383 U.S. at 384-85 (holding that due process requires a competency hearing whenever the record or the defendant’s behavior raises a bona fide doubt of incompetence). The Ninth Circuit has long held that a court must initiate such a hearing when there is "substantial evidence" of incompetence. *Harding*, 834 F.2d at 856 (citing *United States v. Veatch*, 674 F.2d 1217, 1223 (9th Cir.1981), cert. denied, 456 U.S. 946 (1982)). Here, Moran’s recent suicide attempt, his monosyllabic responses to leading questions, his stated desire to prevent the presentation of mitigating evidence, and his use of multiple medications—including sedatives and antiepileptics—created such substantial evidence. *Moran*, 972 F.2d at 268-69.

III. The state court’s post-conviction competency determination was not entitled to deference under 28 U.S.C. § 2254(d) because it applied the wrong legal standard. *Townsend v. Sain*, 372 U.S. 293, 315 (1963) (federal courts must apply the correct legal standard in habeas review). The state court relied solely on psychiatric evaluations that assessed Moran’s competency to stand trial—not his capacity for a "reasoned choice" in waiving constitutional rights. *Moran*, 972 F.2d at 272-73. The trial competency standard, as defined in *Drope*, 420 U.S. at 172, requires only a "rational and factual understanding" of the proceedings and the ability to consult with counsel. By contrast, the "reasoned choice" standard demands a higher level of mental functioning to weigh alternatives and make a voluntary, knowing, and intelligent waiver. *Harding*, 834 F.2d at 856; *Chavez v. United States*, 656 F.2d 512, 518 (9th Cir.1981).

IV. The record does not support a finding that Moran possessed the requisite capacity for a "reasoned choice" at the time of his plea. The psychiatric reports cited by the state court focused exclusively on Moran’s ability to assist counsel and understand the charges—not his ability to make critical decisions about waiving rights. *Drope*, 420 U.S. at 172. Moreover, the reports did not address the impairing effects of the medications Moran was taking at the time of his plea. *Moran*, 972 F.2d at 274-75. Given the absence of any evidence that Moran could meaningfully evaluate the consequences of his decisions, the trial court’s acceptance of his waiver and guilty pleas violated due process. *Pate*, 383 U.S. at 384-85.

V. The Ninth Circuit correctly concluded that the state court’s error was structural and not subject to harmless-error review. *Rose v. Clark*, 478 U.S. 570, 578 (1986) (structural errors require reversal without harmless-error analysis). The trial court’s failure to conduct a competency hearing sua sponte undermined the fundamental fairness of the proceedings, as it deprived Moran of the opportunity to demonstrate his incapacity. *Pate*, 383 U.S. at 384. The state’s post-conviction proceedings, which applied the wrong standard, could not cure this error. *Townsend*, 372 U.S. at 315.

**CONCLUSION**
The Ninth Circuit properly applied the "reasoned choice" standard and correctly determined that the record did not support a finding that Moran was competent to waive his constitutional rights. The judgment below should be affirmed.
