The Fourteenth Amendment due process clause does not guarantee a defendant the right to present expert evidence about his mental state to counter the prosecution's evidence of criminal intent, separate from an insanity defense. The Arizona Supreme Court correctly held that Arizona law permits the use of such evidence only in connection with an insanity defense, and not to negate the element of mens rea.

The longstanding rule in the United States is that a defendant's mental condition is relevant to his criminal liability only insofar as it bears on the insanity defense. As the Supreme Court held in Davis v. United States, 160 U.S. 469, 488 (1895), "the only insanity which is recognized by the law, in determining the question of criminal responsibility, is that which is of such a character as to render the accused incapable of distinguishing between right and wrong." This principle has been consistently applied in the federal courts. See, e.g., United States v. Freeman, 357 F.2d 606, 618-619 (2d Cir. 1966); Smith v. United States, 59 F.2d 631, 632 (D.C. Cir. 1932).

The use of expert testimony about a defendant's mental state is therefore limited to the insanity defense. As the court held in United States v. Currens, 290 F.2d 751, 773 (3d Cir. 1961), "the jury's task in determining criminal responsibility is not aided by expert testimony on the ultimate issue of whether the defendant had the capacity to commit the crime charged." The trial court's ruling in this case, limiting the use of Clark's expert evidence to his insanity defense, is consistent with this principle.

Moreover, the prosecution's burden to prove mens rea beyond a reasonable doubt is not affected by a defendant's ability to present evidence of his mental state. As the Supreme Court held in Leland v. Oregon, 343 U.S. 790, 798-799 (1952), "the burden of proof of insanity is on the defendant, and the prosecution is not required to prove sanity beyond a reasonable doubt." The Court has also recognized that the prosecution's burden to prove the elements of a crime, including mens rea, is a separate and distinct issue from the defendant's claim of insanity. See, e.g., Sinclair v. United States, 279 U.S. 263, 299 (1929); Davis v. United States, 165 U.S. 373, 378 (1897).

The rule that a defendant's mental state is relevant only to the insanity defense has been applied in various state courts as well. See, e.g., State v. White, 58 N.M. 324, 330 (1954); People v. Moran, 249 N.Y. 179, 182 (1928); State v. Maioni, 78 N.J.L. 339, 341 (1907). These cases demonstrate that the Arizona Supreme Court's decision is consistent with a long line of authority limiting the use of expert evidence about a defendant's mental state to the insanity defense.

In conclusion, the Fourteenth Amendment due process clause does not guarantee a defendant the right to present expert evidence about his mental state to counter the prosecution's evidence of criminal intent, separate from an insanity defense. The Arizona Supreme Court's decision is supported by a wealth of case law, including Davis v. United States, 160 U.S. 469 (1895), Leland v. Oregon, 343 U.S. 790 (1952), and numerous other federal and state court decisions. The judgment of the Arizona Supreme Court should be affirmed.
