**ARGUMENT**

I. **Bryant Has Standing To Enforce The Arbitration Award**

Bryant may seek judicial enforcement of the award entered in his favor because he is the real party in interest and an intended beneficiary of the arbitration. Federal labor law permits judicial enforcement of labor-arbitration awards when a party to the grievance process seeks to vindicate the award’s terms. See *United Steelworkers of America v. Enterprise Wheel & Car Corp.*, 363 U.S. 593, 596-99 (1960) (explaining that the arbitrator’s award draws its essence from the collective-bargaining agreement and is enforceable according to its terms); *W.R. Grace & Co. v. Local Union 759, Int’l Union of United Rubber, Cork, Linoleum & Plastic Workers of America*, 461 U.S. 757, 766 (1983) (courts may enforce arbitration awards consistent with the agreement and federal labor policy). Because Bryant’s claim is not a generalized grievance but an effort to enforce relief awarded for his own benefit, he has a personal stake sufficient for Article III standing. See *Lujan v. Defenders of Wildlife*, 504 U.S. 555, 560-61 (1992).

The district court’s contrary ruling cannot be sustained where Bryant is the employee whose rights were adjudicated in arbitration and whose remedy was allegedly ignored. Federal courts routinely recognize standing where the plaintiff seeks to vindicate an award or judgment that confers a direct benefit on him. See *Vaca v. Sipes*, 386 U.S. 171, 182-88 (1967) (employee may sue where union representation fails to vindicate contractual rights); *Hines v. Anchor Motor Freight, Inc.*, 424 U.S. 554, 562-63 (1976) (employee may challenge the integrity of grievance/arbitration process when his individual rights are affected).

II. **The District Court Applied An Overly Demanding Proof Standard To Bryant’s Title VII And Section 1981 Claims**

Bryant’s discrimination and retaliation claims must be analyzed under the familiar burden-shifting framework of *McDonnell Douglas Corp. v. Green*, 411 U.S. 792, 802-05 (1973), as refined by later Supreme Court precedent. A plaintiff need only establish a prima facie case; the burden then shifts to the employer to articulate a legitimate, nondiscriminatory reason; and the plaintiff may prevail by showing pretext. *Texas Department of Community Affairs v. Burdine*, 450 U.S. 248, 252-56 (1981). The Supreme Court has cautioned that the prima facie case is “not onerous.” *Burdine*, 450 U.S. at 253.

After the district court’s ruling, the Supreme Court clarified that a Title VII plaintiff need not prove that discrimination was the sole cause of the adverse action. In *Desert Palace, Inc. v. Costa*, 539 U.S. 90, 98-101 (2003), the Court held that direct evidence is not required for a mixed-motive instruction under Title VII. Although *Desert Palace* postdates this appeal, it confirms the principle that circumstantial evidence may suffice to show unlawful discrimination. See also *Price Waterhouse v. Hopkins*, 490 U.S. 228, 250-52 (1989) (plurality opinion); *St. Mary’s Honor Center v. Hicks*, 509 U.S. 502, 507-08 (1993) (plaintiff may prevail by proving employer’s stated reason false and discrimination the real reason).

In addition, Section 1981 claims are analyzed under the same general evidentiary principles as Title VII claims. See *Hawkins v. PepsiCo, Inc.*, 203 F.3d 274, 278-79 (4th Cir. 2000); *Gairola v. Virginia Department of General Services*, 753 F.2d 1281, 1285-86 (4th Cir. 1985). The district court therefore erred if it required Bryant to produce direct evidence or to negate every nondiscriminatory explanation at the summary-judgment stage.

III. **Genuine Issues Of Material Fact Preclude Summary Judgment On Discrimination And Retaliation**

Summary judgment is inappropriate where the evidence permits a reasonable inference of discriminatory or retaliatory motive. See *Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, Inc.*, 477 U.S. 242, 248-52 (1986); *Celotex Corp. v. Catrett*, 477 U.S. 317, 322-24 (1986). Bryant presented evidence that Bell Atlantic repeatedly disciplined and ultimately terminated him for failing to work Red Letter Day overtime, despite his longstanding child-care responsibilities and his prior history of refusing overtime without discipline before the policy changed. A factfinder could infer disparate treatment from the escalation of discipline after implementation and amendment of the policy, especially where Bryant sought accommodation and the union president allegedly asked management to permit co-workers to cover his assignments.

A reasonable jury could also infer retaliation from the temporal sequence between Bryant’s grievances, his protected opposition to the policy, and the disciplinary actions culminating in discharge. Title VII prohibits retaliation against an employee because he has opposed unlawful practices or participated in protected activity. 42 U.S.C. § 2000e-3(a); see *Burlington Northern & Santa Fe Railway Co. v. White*, 548 U.S. 53, 63-70 (2006); *Ross v. Communications Satellite Corp.*, 759 F.2d 355, 365-66 (4th Cir. 1985). The repeated warnings, suspensions, final warning, and termination after Bryant complained about the policy create a triable issue as to causation and pretext.

Bell Atlantic’s stated reason—“repeated insubordination”—does not eliminate the factual dispute. Under *Reeves v. Sanderson Plumbing Products, Inc.*, 530 U.S. 133, 147-49 (2000), a plaintiff’s prima facie case, combined with evidence that the employer’s explanation is unworthy of credence, may permit a trier of fact to find discrimination. Bryant’s evidence that Bell Atlantic accommodated others through the overtime system, refused alternate arrangements, and intensified discipline only after his objections was sufficient to defeat summary judgment.

For these reasons, the judgment should be reversed.
