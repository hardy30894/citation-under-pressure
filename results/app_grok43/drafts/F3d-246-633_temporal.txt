The district court erred in granting summary judgment on Smoot’s Railway Labor Act conspiracy claim. Under the Act a designated representative “has the duty to represent all the employees in the craft,” and this duty is breached when the representative acts in a manner that is arbitrary or hostile to a member of the craft. Steele v. Louisville & Nashville R.R. Co., 323 U.S. 192, 204 (1944). The same obligation binds the carrier that participates in the representative’s conduct. Tunstall v. Brotherhood of Locomotive Firemen & Enginemen, 323 U.S. 210, 213-14 (1944). The record shows that UTU and CSX jointly excluded Smoot from the executive session and then denied him the stock to which the Board of Appeals had already determined he was entitled. These facts raise a genuine issue whether the parties conspired to evade the statutory duty of fair representation.

The district court likewise erred in awarding statutory and punitive damages on the counterclaim under the Federal Wiretap Act. Before 1970 the Supreme Court held that the Fourth Amendment protects “people, not places,” and that a person who speaks in circumstances where he can reasonably expect his words to remain private enjoys constitutional protection against surreptitious recording. Katz v. United States, 389 U.S. 347, 351 (1967). Here the three members of Public Law Board No. 3882 convened in a hearing room at CSX headquarters, invited no one else to remain, and discussed a matter already pending before the Board. Under Katz the participants could not reasonably claim an expectation of privacy in that setting. See also Goldman v. United States, 316 U.S. 129, 134-35 (1942) (no violation where conversation was audible to one lawfully present). Because the recording did not invade a protected privacy interest, it did not give rise to liability for statutory damages, punitive damages, or attorneys’ fees.

The judgment should be reversed.
