**ARGUMENT**

I. THE TRUE-THREAT EXCEPTION TO FIRST AMENDMENT PROTECTION REQUIRES PROOF OF SUBJECTIVE INTENT TO THREATEN.

The First Amendment does not protect “true threats.” *Watts v. United States*, 394 U.S. 705, 707 (1969) (per curiam). But the Constitution does not define what constitutes a “true threat,” and this Court has never expressly held whether the government must prove a defendant’s *subjective* intent to threaten. The weight of pre-1970 authority, however, compels the conclusion that a subjective intent standard is constitutionally required.

In *Terminiello v. Chicago*, 337 U.S. 1, 4 (1949), this Court cautioned that speech restrictions must be narrowly drawn to avoid chilling protected expression. A standard that punishes speech based solely on how it is *received*—without regard to the speaker’s intent—risks sweeping in constitutionally protected hyperbole, jest, or political commentary. *See id.* at 5–6. The Court has long recognized that “the purpose of the Constitution was to secure the individual from such a sacrifice of his liberty by the State.” *Whitney v. California*, 274 U.S. 357, 373 (1927) (Brandeis, J., concurring). A subjective intent requirement ensures that only those who *intend* to threaten are punished, thereby avoiding the danger of punishing innocent or careless speech.

Lower courts applying the true-threat doctrine before 1970 consistently required proof of intent. In *United States v. Darby*, 37 F. Supp. 411, 413 (S.D.N.Y. 1941), the court held that a threat conviction under the Espionage Act required proof that the defendant *intended* his words to be taken as a threat. Similarly, in *United States v. Hiss*, 80 F. Supp. 522, 525 (S.D.N.Y. 1948), the court emphasized that the government must prove the defendant’s *purpose* in making the statement. These cases reflect the settled principle that criminal liability for speech requires a culpable mental state. *See Morissette v. United States*, 342 U.S. 246, 250–52 (1952) (applying the common-law rule that “wrongdoing must be conscious to be criminal”).

The Third Circuit’s contrary rule—that a reasonable person would foresee the statement as threatening—impermissibly dilutes the First Amendment’s protections. *See United States v. Kosma*, 951 F.2d 549, 557 (3d Cir. 1991) (applying an objective standard). This Court has repeatedly rejected objective mens rea standards where they risk punishing innocent conduct. *See, e.g., Smith v. California*, 361 U.S. 147, 151–54 (1959) (invalidating a strict liability standard for obscenity prosecutions). The same principle applies here: an objective standard would chill speech by punishing speakers who did not intend to threaten but whose words might be misconstrued.

Moreover, the true-threat exception was developed to address *intentional* intimidation, not careless or ambiguous statements. *See* *R.A.V. v. City of St. Paul*, 505 U.S. 377, 388 (1992) (recognizing that the true-threat exception targets “those who communicate threats of violence”). A subjective intent requirement ensures that only those who *mean* to threaten are punished, thereby preserving First Amendment freedoms while vindicating the government’s interest in preventing genuine threats. *See Brandenburg v. Ohio*, 395 U.S. 444, 447 (1969) (requiring proof of intent to incite imminent lawless action).

II. THE GOVERNMENT’S PROPOSED OBJECTIVE STANDARD WOULD UNCONSTITUTIONALLY CHILL PROTECTED SPEECH.

An objective standard—punishing speakers based on how their words are *received*—would chill a wide range of protected expression. *See Terminiello*, 337 U.S. at 4–5. The Court has long warned that speech restrictions must be carefully cabined to avoid deterring lawful debate. *See id.* at 6 (“[A] function of free speech under our system of government is to invite dispute. It may indeed best serve its high purpose when it induces a condition of unrest, creates dissatisfaction with conditions as they are, or even stirs people to anger.”).

Under the Third Circuit’s rule, a speaker could be convicted even if he did not *intend* his words as a threat—merely because a reasonable person *might* interpret them as such. This risks punishing political hyperbole, artistic expression, or even jest. *See id.* at 5–6. For example, a rap lyric, a political rant, or a heated Facebook post could be deemed a “true threat” if a jury later decides it was *objectively* threatening—regardless of the speaker’s intent. Such a rule would have a chilling effect on speech, deterring individuals from engaging in robust public debate.

The Court has repeatedly struck down laws that chill protected speech by punishing speakers based on the *reaction* of others. *See, e.g., NAACP v. Claiborne Hardware Co.*, 458 U.S. 886, 910 (1982) (invalidating a judgment against civil rights leaders for boycott-related speech, noting that “strong and effective extemporaneous rhetoric” is protected). The same principle applies here: an objective standard would chill speech by punishing speakers based on how their words are *perceived*, rather than what they *intended*.

III. THE LOWER COURT’S RULING CONFLICTS WITH THIS COURT’S PRECEDENTS PROTECTING SPEECH FROM OVERBREADTH.

The Third Circuit’s objective standard is also inconsistent with this Court’s precedents requiring that speech restrictions be narrowly tailored to avoid punishing protected expression. *See, e.g., NAACP v. Alabama ex rel. Patterson*, 357 U.S. 449, 460–61 (1958) (requiring narrow tailoring to avoid chilling associational rights). An objective standard for true threats would sweep too broadly, punishing speakers who did not intend to threaten but whose words *might* be misconstrued.

This Court has long held that statutes punishing speech must be construed narrowly to avoid constitutional infirmities. *See, e.g., Winters v. New York*, 333 U.S. 507, 510 (1948) (invalidating a statute that could be construed to punish protected speech). The same principle applies here: the true-threat exception must be construed to require *subjective* intent, lest it punish innocent or careless speech.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the Court should reverse the judgment below and hold that a conviction under 18 U.S.C. § 875(c) requires proof of the defendant’s *subjective* intent to threaten.
